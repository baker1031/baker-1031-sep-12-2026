# 1031 Exchange vs. Selling and Paying the Tax

I keep seeing tax deferral treated as the automatic win and a taxable sale as a mistake. That gets the decision backward. Deferral is a tool; the right question is whether the tax saved, the asset retained, and the loss of liquidity actually fit the next chapter.

For a large gain that will remain in real estate, a 1031 can be enormously valuable. For a small gain, a low-income year, a genuine need for cash, or a bad replacement deal, paying the tax can be the cleaner investment decision.

## What selling actually costs you

A sale can stack four taxes. In 2026, federal long-term capital-gains rates are 0%, 15%, or 20%. The 0% bracket reaches about $49,450 of taxable income for single filers and $98,900 for married couples filing jointly; the 20% rate begins above roughly $545,500 single and $613,700 joint. Most investors with meaningful gain are in the 15% or 20% range. See [capital gains tax on real estate](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html).

NIIT adds 3.8% when modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 single or $250,000 joint, a threshold a property sale can cross. Put another way, the glossary thresholds are 200,000 dollars for a single filer and 250,000 dollars for a married couple filing jointly. See the [NIIT guide](net-investment-income-tax.html). Depreciation recapture adds another layer: unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is taxed at up to 25%; a building held and depreciated for fifteen years can have six figures of recapture exposure. See [depreciation recapture explained](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html).

State tax follows, with California taxing capital gain as ordinary income. Together, federal gain, NIIT, recapture, and state tax can total roughly **25% to 35%** of gain. On a $1,000,000 gain, that is $250,000 to $350,000 no longer compounding.

## How deferral actually works

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) postpones rather than erases tax: a QI holds proceeds, replacement real estate is acquired, and gain follows into the new basis until a later cash sale.

From relinquished closing, the exchanger has **45 days** to identify replacement property in writing and **180 days** to close. The 180-day period is capped by that year’s tax-return due date, so a late-year sale may shorten it unless an extension is filed. There are no good-faith extensions.

Full deferral requires equal-or-greater value, reinvestment of all equity, and replacement of debt paid off at sale. Cash removed is cash boot; unoffset mortgage reduction is mortgage boot. Boot is taxable now but does not invalidate the deferred portion.

## The time value of money, plainly

Deferral leaves the tax dollars invested—an interest-free loan from the government. On a $1,000,000 gain taxed at 30%, a sale leaves $700,000 to reinvest while an exchange leaves $1,000,000. At 7% for 20 years, those amounts grow to about $2.71 million and $3.87 million, respectively: a roughly $1.16 million difference from the extra $300,000 compounding.

Long horizon and large gain widen that gap. Short horizon and modest gain narrow it. See [tax deferral strategies compared](tax-deferral-strategies-compared.html).

## The exit that makes deferral permanent

Under current law, property held at death receives a fair-market-value step-up in basis. The carried gain can disappear for heirs, who may sell near that value with little or no capital-gains tax. This “swap till you drop” path makes long-term deferral potentially permanent for an estate-plan holder.

## When paying the tax is the smarter move

Paying can win when gain is small, recapture is minor, a 0% gain bracket is available, liquidity or an exit from real estate is the actual goal, or the only replacement is a poor deadline-driven deal. A forced purchase can cost more than tax saved. [Ways to handle capital gains tax](how-to-avoid-capital-gains-tax-investment-property.html) include options that do not require remaining in real estate.

## When deferral clearly wins

Deferral is strongest with large gain, meaningful 25%-rate recapture, a long-term intent to own real estate, and an attractive replacement. A [DST](dst-guide.html) can address the management issue: a fractional, professionally managed institutional-real-estate interest that can qualify as like-kind. A [1031 exchange into a DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) can defer tax without tenant calls. DSTs are speculative, illiquid securities offered only to verified accredited investors through a PPM and can lose principal.

## Defer vs. pay: where each wins

| Situation | Usually better | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large gain, heavy depreciation, staying in real estate | 1031 | More capital compounds and recapture is deferred. |
| Hold for life and leave to heirs | 1031 | Step-up can erase deferred tax. |
| Small gain, little depreciation | Pay tax | Modest benefit versus 45-day constraint. |
| Gain fits 0% bracket | Pay tax | Recognize tax-free federal gain. |
| Need cash or want out | Pay tax | No forced return to real estate. |
| Done being landlord, still want deferral | 1031 into DST | Passive ownership; accredited-investor PPM only. |
| Only bad replacement available | Pay or wait | Deadline pressure can destroy value. |

Illustrative only. State, tax bracket, and facts change the answer; use your CPA.

## How to actually decide

Price the all-in tax, including gains, 3.8% NIIT, up-to-25% recapture, and state tax. A [sell vs. 1031 calculator](sell-vs-1031-exchange-calculator.html) provides an initial estimate. Compound the amount that deferral keeps invested over the true holding horizon, then address liquidity, estate plan, management preference, and actual replacement quality.

First-order thinking sees the tax bill. Second-order thinking compares it with the price of being locked into the wrong asset. Where gain and recapture are meaningful, the investor wants real estate, and a strong replacement exists, deferral often wins. Where cash, a low bracket, or freedom from property is worth more, paying can be rational.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 1031 exchange worth it versus selling?
Large long-term gain and recapture can make it worthwhile; small gain, liquidity needs, real-estate exit, or 0% bracket can make sale smarter. Deferring a $20,000 tax bill while taking on a 45-day clock and forced reinvestment is rarely compelling.

### How much tax do you actually pay if you sell instead of exchanging?
Often roughly 25% to 35% after 0%/15%/20% federal gain, 3.8% NIIT, up-to-25% recapture, and state tax.

### Why is 1031 deferral valuable?
It compounds full pre-tax capital like an interest-free government loan; long-term step-up can eliminate carried gain.

### What are rules for full deferral?
45-day written identification, 180-day completion, equal-or-greater value, all equity reinvested, and debt replacement; cash or mortgage boot is taxable.

### When should you pay instead?
Small gain, minor recapture, 0% bracket, a liquidity/exit goal, or an unattractive replacement.

### What if you want deferral but no management?
A DST can provide passive like-kind ownership, but it is a speculative, illiquid, accredited-investor PPM security and can lose principal.

### Does deferral mean never paying tax?
Not by itself. A later sale without another exchange recognizes gain; holding to death can produce the step-up outcome.

## Glossary

- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing tax so pre-tax capital compounds.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior deductions; Section 1250 gain is up to 25%.
- **Net Investment Income Tax:** 3.8% above $200,000 single/$250,000 joint.
- **Boot:** Non-like-kind cash or net debt relief taxable in exchange year.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Death-time fair-value basis reset that can erase heir gain.
- **Like-Kind Property:** Investment/business real estate; a DST interest can qualify.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust:** Fractional managed institutional real estate that can support passive 1031 deferral.
