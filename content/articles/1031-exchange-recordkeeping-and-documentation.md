# 1031 Exchange Recordkeeping & Documentation

I keep noticing that exchange paperwork is treated like closing debris. It is not. In a 1031, the file is the evidence for a deferred tax position that can follow the investor for decades, through later exchanges, or until death.

The agreement, assignments, identification, closing statements, and tax reporting explain the transaction. The basis records explain the tax consequences that outlive it. Good recordkeeping supports the deferral, keeps future reporting accurate, and provides the proof if the IRS asks questions years later.

## Why recordkeeping matters

Your [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) must be documented to support Form 8824 and the deferral it reports. The relevant facts include the properties, values, dates, debt, [boot](insights.html), and basis. Records substantiate those facts; without them, the reported exchange is harder to support if questioned.

The deferral also creates a long-lived tax position. The carried-over basis in the replacement property affects future depreciation and gain when the replacement is later disposed of, which may be years or decades away. The records showing how basis moved from the relinquished property through the exchange remain relevant well beyond the original tax year.

If the IRS audits or questions the exchange, the documentation supports the like-kind investment or productive-use property, the timing, the structure, and the basis calculation. Records are how an investor substantiates the reported exchange, supports carried-over basis, and defends the transaction over its long tax life.

## The key exchange documents

The exchange agreement with the [qualified intermediary](insights.html) establishes the exchange structure and the QI’s role. Assignment documents show that rights under the sale and purchase contracts were assigned to the QI, supporting the exchange structure.

The written identification of replacement property within 45 days is critical evidence that the replacement was named on time. Closing or settlement statements for both the relinquished sale and replacement purchase show values, dates, debt, costs, and proceeds. They support the boot calculation and the basis calculation.

Keep the purchase and sale contracts, including exchange-cooperation language; the QI’s accounting of funds; and Form 8824. Together with the agreement, assignments, identification, and both closing statements, those records tell the complete story: structure, identification, timing, values, and reporting.

> The agreement, assignments, identification, both closing statements, contracts, and Form 8824 form one continuous record from exchange structure through tax reporting.

## Records for the relinquished and replacement

Keep full records for both properties, not only the exchange packet. For the relinquished property, retain the original purchase records, capital-improvement records, and depreciation taken. Its adjusted basis carries into the replacement, and its depreciation affects recapture. The relinquished-property basis history starts the replacement-property basis story.

For the replacement, retain acquisition records, the carried-over-basis calculation showing how relinquished basis changed for boot and additional investment, and ongoing depreciation and improvement records. That replacement basis affects later depreciation and later gain.

The two files belong together. The exchange links the relinquished property’s basis to the replacement’s basis. In serial exchanges, the chain can span property after property, so each property’s history and every exchange document must remain with the basis record.

## Tracking basis across the exchange

Basis tracking is one of the most consequential recordkeeping tasks. In general, replacement basis equals the relinquished property’s adjusted basis, plus additional investment or excess basis, minus boot. It is often lower than the replacement property’s market value because deferred gain is embedded in the basis. It determines future depreciation and gain on a later disposition.

Document the calculation and keep it moving forward. For depreciation, carryover basis continues under the old depreciation schedule while excess basis is depreciated as new basis under the regulations. Errors do not stay isolated; they can affect every future depreciation deduction and the eventual gain.

Serial exchanges magnify the need for a clean chain. Basis from property one moves to property two, then property three, and so on. The basis at the end depends on the entire history, which is why the CPA’s basis tracking and complete supporting records matter.

## Records for an audit

An audit or inquiry requires proof that the exchange qualified: like-kind investment or productive-use real property, [45-day identification](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html), 180-day completion, proper QI structure without constructive receipt, and correct boot and basis calculations. The exchange agreement, dated identification, closing statements, and basis schedules provide that proof.

Audit readiness means organizing those documents so they can be produced without reconstruction. The identification shows timely and proper designation. The closings show values and timing. The agreement shows structure. The basis calculation supports the tax reporting.

An exchange can be questioned long after closing, while the basis continues to affect returns. Keep audit-ready records for the life of the tax position. A potential inquiry should become a straightforward demonstration of compliance, not a scramble to recreate the file.

### Key Takeaways

- Records support the exchange reported on Form 8824, the carried-over basis, and an audit defense.
- The core file includes the exchange agreement, assignments, identification, both closing statements, contracts, QI accounting, and Form 8824.
- Preserve basis records for both properties and track them through every serial exchange.
- Retain records as long as the replacement property and its basis affect taxes, plus the audit period.

## How long to keep records

The retention period is longer than ordinary tax recordkeeping because the carried-over basis persists. Keep exchange records until well after the final taxable disposition of the replacement property, because basis affects the gain reported at that point, and then retain them through the applicable audit or statute-of-limitations period. The deferral and basis can last decades, continue across new exchanges, or remain until death.

In practice, retain the exchange and basis documentation for the full time the replacement is held and through all subsequent exchanges. After the final taxable disposition is reported, retain it for the statute-of-limitations period—generally at least three years, and longer in some circumstances. The relevant period follows the property and basis, not merely the original exchange year.

Use durable, organized records and digital backups. Coordinate retention with the CPA. First-order thinking keeps the file for the ordinary few years. Second-order thinking follows the basis to its eventual tax result. For an exchange, think long-term retention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is recordkeeping important for a 1031 exchange?

It supports Form 8824 and the deferral, preserves the carried-over basis that can affect taxes for decades, and provides proof if the IRS questions the exchange. Good records substantiate the exchange, basis, and audit defense.

### What documents should I keep from my exchange?

Keep the exchange agreement with the QI, assignments, the written 45-day identification, both closing statements, purchase and sale contracts, QI fund accounting, Form 8824, and basis records for both properties. Together they document structure, timing, values, and reporting. Retain them long-term.

### What is the carried-over basis, and why track it?

It is generally adjusted relinquished basis plus additional investment or excess basis, minus boot, carried into the replacement. Deferred gain is embedded in it, so it is often lower than market value. It determines future depreciation and future gain and must be followed across a serial-exchange chain.

### How long should I keep exchange records?

Keep them for as long as the replacement and its carried-over basis affect taxes—potentially decades and through serial exchanges—plus the audit period after the final taxable disposition, generally at least three years and sometimes longer.

### Will the IRS audit my exchange?

It is possible. An audit file needs to show qualified like-kind investment property, deadlines, proper QI structure, and correct boot and basis. Agreement, dated identification, closings, and basis calculations provide the proof. Organized records make an inquiry easier to answer.

### What records prove my exchange qualified?

The exchange agreement shows the QI structure and no constructive receipt; dated identification shows timely 45-day identification; closing statements show values, dates, and 180-day completion; basis schedules show proper boot and basis reporting.

### Do I need to keep records of the relinquished property?

Yes. Purchase records, capital improvements, and depreciation establish the adjusted basis that carries to the replacement and supports recapture. Do not discard the relinquished-property file after the exchange.

### How does recordkeeping work for serial exchanges?

Keep every property’s and every exchange’s records. Basis moves from property one to property two, then to property three and beyond, and the final basis depends on the full documented chain. The CPA tracks it; the investor retains the support.

### Who keeps the exchange records — me, the QI, or my CPA?

The QI provides exchange records and the CPA tracks basis and Form 8824, but the investor should maintain a complete organized copy. Do not rely solely on either professional: the taxpayer is ultimately responsible for supporting the tax position.

### What if I lose my exchange documents?

Request copies from the QI, title or escrow company, CPA, and counterparties. Reconstruction can fill gaps, but it is better to retain complete copies and digital backups from the outset. Missing records weaken support if questioned.

### Should I keep digital or paper records?

Either or both works if records remain durable, organized, and accessible over the long term. Digital backups protect against physical loss; keeping paper originals of key documents may also be worthwhile.

### Does Baker 1031 keep my records for me?

Baker helps order key documents and coordinate with the QI, CPA, and DST documentation, but the investor must maintain a complete backed-up copy for the long term. Baker supports the file; the investor retains it.

### What records should I keep for a DST exchange specifically?

Keep the private placement memorandum, subscription documents, closing or acquisition records showing investment amount and percentage interest, QI documents, and annual tax reporting. DSTs typically issue a 1099 or grantor-trust statement showing the investor’s income share rather than a K-1. Keep basis records showing how relinquished basis reached the DST interest.

### How do records support my depreciation deductions?

Records establish depreciable basis and the carryover/excess-basis split. Without the relinquished-through-replacement basis documentation, annual replacement-property depreciation is difficult to support. The CPA uses that documentation to compute and substantiate deductions during the holding period.

## Glossary

- **Recordkeeping:** Maintaining documentation that substantiates and protects the exchange.
- **Exchange Agreement:** The foundational document establishing the exchange and the QI’s role.
- **Assignment:** Documents assigning contract rights to the QI and supporting structure.
- **Identification Document:** The written 45-day identification proving timely designation.
- **Closing Statement:** A settlement statement recording values, dates, debt, and costs.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form reporting the exchange and a key record.
- **Carried-Over Basis:** Basis flowing from relinquished property to replacement property.
- **Excess Basis:** Additional basis from new investment, tracked separately for depreciation.
- **Basis Tracking:** Documenting basis as it moves through an exchange and serial exchanges.
- **Serial Exchange:** Repeated exchanges requiring records across the entire chain.
- **Audit Readiness:** Organized records available to demonstrate exchange validity if questioned.
- **Statute of Limitations:** The period during which the IRS can audit, affecting retention.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Purchase basis plus improvements, less depreciation.
- **Depreciation Records:** Evidence of depreciation taken, affecting recapture and basis.
- **Retention Period:** How long records are kept, tied to the property’s and basis’s long life.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party providing exchange documents and coordinating records with the taxpayer.
