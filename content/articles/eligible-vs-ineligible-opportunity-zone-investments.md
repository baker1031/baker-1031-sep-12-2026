# Eligible vs. Ineligible Opportunity Zone Investments

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Opportunity Zone Funds Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how often an Opportunity Zone is described as if the map itself does the work. It does not. First-order thinking sees an address inside a zone and a tax benefit. Second-order thinking asks whether the property, business activity, capital deployment, and fund compliance actually satisfy the rules that support that benefit.

Not everything in an Opportunity Zone qualifies for the tax benefits. This guide covers what businesses and properties are eligible—qualified OZ property and business—the substantial-improvement requirement, the excluded “sin businesses,” the working-capital safe harbor, and how to vet a fund’s holdings for compliance.

The tax benefits depend not simply on investing in a zone, but on investing in qualifying property and businesses within the program’s rules. There are specific requirements for qualified OZ property and qualified OZ business, plus a substantial-improvement test and exclusions. The practical job is to vet the fund: confirm that its holdings genuinely qualify, avoid compliance problems, and verify current, time-sensitive rules with a tax advisor.

## Qualified OZ property & business

Qualified OZ investments fall within the categories of qualified [opportunity zone](opportunity-zones-guide.html) property. A QOF must hold one or more of the following:

- Qualified OZ business property: tangible property used in a trade or business in a zone, such as real estate being developed or equipment.
- Qualified OZ stock: stock in a corporation that is a qualified OZ business.
- Qualified OZ partnership interests: interests in a partnership that is a qualified OZ business.

A qualified OZ business broadly operates substantially within a zone. It must meet requirements such as having a substantial portion of tangible property in the zone, deriving a substantial portion of income from active conduct in the zone, and avoiding excluded businesses. The underlying business or property must genuinely be in, and operate within, the zone.

That is the dividing line. Eligible investments are qualifying OZ business property, stock, or partnership interests in businesses that meet the zone-based property and income tests and are not excluded. The standards are intended to ensure a genuine zone investment, not merely an investment with a zone-related label.

## The substantial improvement requirement

Existing property has its own eligibility test. For a QOF to count non-original-use property as qualifying OZ business property, it must substantially improve it—generally by investing in improvements at least equal to its basis in the building within a set period, generally **30 months**. In practical terms, that roughly doubles the building investment.

The rule is aimed at genuine development or improvement, rather than a passive purchase of an existing property. New, original-use property—such as newly constructed buildings—qualifies through original use and does not need a separate substantial-improvement calculation.

Buying an existing building is not enough. If the fund does not substantially improve it, the property does not qualify and the tax benefits can be jeopardized. For existing property, the test is central: substantial improvement, generally a doubling of the building investment within about 30 months. For new construction, original use is the qualifying route.

## Excluded “sin” businesses

The program specifically bars certain businesses from qualifying as OZ businesses. The excluded “sin businesses” are:

- Golf courses
- Country clubs
- Massage parlors
- Hot tub facilities
- Suntan facilities
- Racetracks and other gambling facilities
- Stores whose principal business is selling alcoholic beverages for off-premises consumption, including liquor stores

These exclusions reflect a policy judgment about which uses the program should not incentivize. Being located inside a zone does not override the exclusion: a QOF generally cannot treat an investment in one of these business types as a qualifying OZ business. When vetting a fund, confirm that no holding falls within this list; a fund that invests in an excluded business has a compliance problem that can put the tax benefits at risk.

## Working-capital safe harbor

The working-capital safe harbor gives an OZ business room to hold cash temporarily while it deploys capital into a project. A QOF normally must keep **90%** of assets in qualifying OZ property, and an OZ business must limit non-qualifying assets. Development takes time, however, and construction capital cannot always be invested the day it is raised.

Under the safe harbor, an OZ business can hold working capital—cash—for a period generally up to **31 months**, with extensions possible, under a written plan to deploy it into the OZ project. The cash does not count against the asset requirements during that period. The provision lets a development-oriented fund raise capital and deploy it over a documented timeline without violating the asset tests. A fund relying on the safe harbor should have the written deployment plan in place.

## Vetting a fund’s holdings

Vetting is where the tax analysis becomes investment due diligence. Check that each property or business is qualifying OZ property in a designated zone, meets the property and income tests, and—where it is existing property—is being substantially improved. Confirm that the fund avoids excluded businesses.

Also examine the fund’s compliance practices: its legal structure, written working-capital plan when relevant, and record of meeting the **90% asset test** and other requirements. A fund can have an attractive property and still have a weak compliance process. That distinction matters because compliance problems can jeopardize tax benefits.

Eligibility is technical. An advisor, CPA, and attorney may need to examine the fund’s structure and holdings. The investor’s task is not to operate the compliance program; it is to test the qualification, improvement, exclusions, and compliance process before relying on the expected tax treatment.

## Key Takeaways

- Eligible OZ investments are qualified OZ property—business property, stock, or partnership interests—in businesses genuinely operating within a zone.
- Existing property is eligible only if it is substantially improved, generally by roughly doubling the building investment; new construction qualifies as original use.
- Golf courses, country clubs, gambling facilities, liquor stores, and the other listed “sin businesses” are specifically excluded.
- The working-capital safe harbor lets funds hold cash temporarily under a written plan. Vet a fund’s holdings and compliance practices before relying on the tax benefits.

## Why eligibility matters to investors

Eligibility is not a technical footnote. The OZ tax benefits depend on the investment qualifying. If a fund’s holdings fail—for example, because existing property is not substantially improved, an excluded business is involved, or the fund fails an asset test—the expected deferral or 10-year exclusion can be jeopardized and penalties may apply.

First-order thinking treats eligibility as the sponsor’s back-office work. Second-order thinking recognizes that the investment’s value includes the tax treatment the investor expects. Vetting eligibility is therefore part of due diligence: a non-compliant fund puts the benefits that motivated the investment at risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What qualifies as an eligible Opportunity Zone investment?

A QOF must hold qualified OZ property: qualified OZ business property, qualified OZ stock, or qualified OZ partnership interests. Business property is tangible property used in a trade or business in a zone, such as developing real estate or equipment. Qualified stock and partnership interests must be in qualified OZ businesses. Those businesses operate substantially within a zone, meet property and active-income tests, and are not excluded businesses. The standards are designed to ensure a genuine zone investment.

### What is the substantial improvement requirement?

For existing, non-original-use property, a QOF generally must invest in improvements at least equal to the building’s basis within a set period, generally **30 months**—roughly doubling the building investment. The requirement ensures genuine development or improvement rather than a passive purchase of an existing property. New construction qualifies as original use and does not need a separate improvement calculation.

### What are “sin businesses” excluded from OZs?

The program excludes golf courses, country clubs, massage parlors, hot tub facilities, suntan facilities, racetracks and other gambling facilities, and stores principally selling alcoholic beverages for off-premises consumption, including liquor stores. A QOF generally cannot count an investment in these business types as a qualifying OZ business, even when the business is inside a zone. Confirm that a fund’s holdings avoid them.

### What is the working-capital safe harbor?

The safe harbor allows an OZ business to hold cash for a period generally up to **31 months**, with extensions possible, under a written plan to deploy it into an OZ project. That cash does not count against asset requirements. It accommodates the time needed to raise capital and deploy it into development while the QOF maintains its **90%** qualifying-property discipline. A fund using the safe harbor should have a proper written deployment plan.

### How do I vet a fund’s holdings for eligibility?

Confirm the property and businesses qualify, are in designated zones, meet the property and income tests, and avoid excluded businesses. For existing property, confirm substantial improvement. Review the fund’s compliance practices, structure, written working-capital plan where applicable, and record of meeting the **90% asset test**. Because the analysis is technical, an advisor, CPA, and attorney often help examine the fund’s structure and holdings.

### Why does eligibility matter to me as an investor?

The tax benefits depend on the investment qualifying. A failure of substantial improvement, use of an excluded business, or failure of an asset test can jeopardize the expected deferral and 10-year exclusion and can produce penalties. Eligibility therefore directly affects whether the investor receives the benefits that motivated the OZ investment.

### Can a QOF invest in any business in a zone?

No. A business must be a qualified OZ business: it operates substantially within the zone, meets the property and income tests, and is not an excluded “sin business.” A business that is mostly outside the zone or falls within an excluded type does not qualify. A QOF must invest in genuinely qualifying businesses or qualifying property, not simply any business with a zone address.

### What happens if a fund’s investment does not qualify?

If a holding fails the requirements—for example, it is not substantially improved, involves an excluded business, or the fund fails asset tests—the tax benefits can be jeopardized. The fund can face penalties for failing the **90%** test, and investors may not receive the expected deferral or 10-year exclusion. That is why genuine qualification, substantial improvement, permitted businesses, and compliance controls must be reviewed before investing.

### Is OZ eligibility something I or the fund handles?

The fund and its professionals handle the technical work: structuring investments to qualify, meeting improvement and asset requirements, avoiding excluded businesses, and maintaining compliance. The investor should still vet that process as part of due diligence because it affects the investor’s tax benefits. In short, the fund must achieve and maintain eligibility; the investor must assess whether it is doing so, often with professional help.

### Does the property have to be entirely within the zone?

Generally, qualifying tangible property must be located within the Opportunity Zone and used in a business there. A qualified OZ business must have a substantial portion of tangible property in the zone and derive a substantial portion of income from active conduct in the zone. A property straddling a boundary or a business operating mostly outside the zone can raise qualification issues. Confirm the in-zone location and activity tests.

### Can an OZ fund hold multiple properties or businesses?

Yes. A QOF can hold multiple qualifying properties or businesses across one or more zones, creating a diversified multi-asset fund, or it can hold one concentrated project. Each holding must qualify, meet the substantial-improvement and in-zone requirements, avoid excluded businesses, and fit within the fund’s **90% asset test**. A multi-asset fund spreads exposure across projects but requires each project to comply; a single-asset fund concentrates both investment and compliance risk.

## Glossary

- **Qualified OZ Property:** The property a QOF must hold (90% test).
- **Qualified OZ Business Property:** Tangible property used in a trade or business in a zone.
- **Qualified OZ Stock:** Stock in a corporation that’s a qualified OZ business.
- **Qualified OZ Partnership Interest:** An interest in a partnership that’s a qualified OZ business.
- **Qualified OZ Business:** A business operating substantially within a zone.
- **Substantial Improvement:** Roughly doubling the building investment for existing property.
- **Original Use:** New property qualifying without separate improvement.
- **Sin Business:** An excluded business (golf course, liquor store, etc.).
- **Excluded Businesses:** The specific business types barred from qualifying.
- **Working-Capital Safe Harbor:** Holding cash to deploy under a written plan.
- **90% Asset Test:** The QOF requirement to hold 90%+ in OZ property.
- **Active Conduct:** The income test for a qualified OZ business.
- **Compliance:** Meeting the program’s requirements to preserve benefits.
- **Vetting:** Confirming a fund’s holdings genuinely qualify.
- **Penalties:** Consequences of failing the asset tests.
- **Due Diligence:** Examining a fund’s eligibility and compliance.

## Eligible vs. ineligible OZ investments at a glance

How qualifying Opportunity Zone investments differ from ineligible ones across the rules a fund must satisfy:

| Factor | Eligible OZ Investment | Ineligible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Property type | Qualified OZ business property or business | Assets that fail the location or use tests |
| Substantial improvement | Meets the doubling-of-basis requirement | Acquired property left unimproved |
| Business activity | Permitted active trade or business | Excluded “sin” businesses |
| Working capital | Deployed under the safe harbor | Idle cash beyond the safe harbor |
| Tax benefit | Deferral and potential step-up at 10 years | Benefits lost or recaptured |
| Investor impact | Intended OZ tax treatment preserved | Compliance failure puts benefits at risk |

## Which should you choose?

Eligibility is not optional. Only investments meeting the qualified-property, substantial-improvement, permitted-business, and working-capital rules deliver the Opportunity Zone tax benefits. Before investing, test a fund’s holdings against those standards: confirm the property qualifies, confirm the improvement requirement is met, confirm the business is not excluded, and confirm capital is deployed within the safe harbor. A non-compliant holding can put the entire tax benefit at risk.
