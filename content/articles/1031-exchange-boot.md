# What Is Boot in a 1031 Exchange?

I keep seeing investors focus on the 45-day calendar and the qualified intermediary, then find the tax leak later in the closing statement. That leak is often boot: not a failed exchange, and not a legal penalty, but a piece of gain that no longer qualifies for deferral because part of the value came back to the investor.

That distinction matters. A partially taxable 1031 exchange is still a valid exchange, and boot can be deliberate. But it is frequently avoidable. First-order thinking says, “The exchange closed, so the tax must be deferred.” Second-order thinking asks whether cash, debt, and closing charges were structured so the capital actually stayed invested. That is where the result is decided.

This is a guide to where boot comes from, how it is taxed, and how to keep it from appearing by accident.

## Key Takeaways

- Boot is value received in an exchange that is not like-kind real property, most often leftover cash or a net reduction in debt.
- It can be cash (equity) boot, mortgage (debt) boot, or both in the same exchange.
- It is taxable only up to realized gain, and depreciation recapture is generally recognized first.
- Cash can offset mortgage boot, but more debt can never offset cash boot.
- Full deferral calls for equal-or-greater value, all equity reinvested, and replacement of paid-off debt.

## What Boot Means

**Boot** is any value received in a 1031 exchange other than like-kind real property. The word comes from the old English phrase “to boot” — from bōt, meaning advantage or remedy — for something handed over on top of the main trade. In the exchange context, the usual extras are cash not reinvested and a net reduction in debt. The IRS treats that value as gain the investor has chosen to realize and taxes it accordingly.

The reason is simple. Section 1031 rests on the idea that the investor has continued the same investment in a new form rather than cashed out. To the extent money is pocketed, or debt is shed without replacement, that continuity ends for that portion. Boot measures the portion actually liquidated: keep no non-like-kind value and full deferral remains possible; keep some and that portion is taxable. For the wider framework, see the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html).

## Cash Boot and Mortgage Boot

**Cash boot**, also called equity boot, is the more visible form. It is money that leaves the exchange and comes to the investor: sale proceeds not reinvested, cash taken at closing, or funds a qualified intermediary returns because the replacement property cost less than the relinquished property. If a dollar of exchange equity lands in the investor’s pocket rather than the replacement property, it is cash boot.

**Mortgage boot**, or debt boot, is less visible. It arises when debt on the replacement property is lower than debt paid off on the relinquished property. Relief from the old mortgage is economically similar to receiving cash. If the investor does not take on at least as much new debt, or bring enough personal cash to replace the missing debt, the IRS can treat the difference as boot even though no cash passed through the investor’s hands.

An exchange can have both forms at once. An investor might retain some cash and reduce leverage. The figures are calculated and combined to determine total boot.

## The Netting Rule That Matters

The rule is asymmetric: **you may offset mortgage boot with cash, but you may never offset cash boot with debt.**

If replacement debt falls short, the investor may bring additional out-of-pocket cash to closing. That cash substitutes for the missing debt, so the mortgage boot disappears. The reverse does not work. If cash leaves the exchange, taking on extra debt elsewhere does not cure it. Cash received is cash boot.

The practical “napkin test” for full deferral has three parts:

1. Buy replacement property of **equal or greater value**.
2. Reinvest **all** net equity.
3. Carry **equal or greater debt**, or substitute the shortfall with personal cash.

Meet all three and there is no boot. Miss one and the shortfall is taxable.

## How Boot Is Taxed

Boot is taxable up to the lesser of the boot received or the **realized gain** on the sale. That ceiling is important: a taxpayer cannot be taxed on more gain than was actually realized, even if the boot amount is larger.

The character of recognized boot generally follows the gain it represents. **Depreciation recapture is generally recognized first.** To the extent gain is recognized through boot, it is generally treated first as prior depreciation, potentially taxed at up to **25%** as unrecaptured Section **1250** gain, before ordinary capital gain treatment. The **3.8%** net investment income tax can also apply. For a long-held, heavily depreciated property, a small amount of boot can therefore carry a higher effective rate than expected.

## Worked Examples

These examples are illustrative and simplified to isolate the mechanics.

### Example 1: Trading Down Creates Cash Boot

Assume you sell a debt-free property for **$1,000,000** and receive **$1,000,000** of equity. You acquire a replacement property for **$850,000**. The unused **$150,000** comes back as cash boot and is taxable, up to realized gain.

### Example 2: Reducing Debt Creates Mortgage Boot

Assume you sell a **$1,000,000** property with a **$400,000** mortgage. You reinvest the full **$600,000** of equity in an **$800,000** replacement property carrying a **$200,000** loan. Every dollar of equity was reinvested, but debt declined by **$200,000**. That **$200,000** debt relief is mortgage boot.

### Example 3: Personal Cash Can Cure Mortgage Boot

Use the same facts as Example 2, but bring an additional **$200,000** of personal cash to acquire a **$1,000,000** replacement property while retaining the **$200,000** loan. The added cash replaces the missing debt. There is no boot, and the full gain can defer.

### Example 4: A Deliberate Partial Exchange

Assume you sell for **$1,000,000**, want **$100,000** for another purpose, and reinvest the remaining **$900,000**. You knowingly take **$100,000** of cash boot, pay tax on that amount, and defer the rest. That can be a legitimate, common approach. Boot is not always an error.

## Where Accidental Boot Hides

The troublesome boot is usually unplanned and often sits in the settlement statement. Watch for:

- **Excess proceeds with the intermediary:** If the replacement costs less than expected, leftover proceeds can become cash boot.
- **Non-transactional costs paid from exchange funds:** Prorated rents, security-deposit transfers, and certain loan fees and points can be boot when exchange proceeds cover them rather than separate funds.
- **Cash-out refinancing around the exchange:** A refinance immediately before or after the transaction can be recharacterized as boot.
- **Seller financing retained by the seller:** A buyer’s note is not like-kind property.
- **Unnoticed debt reduction:** A less-leveraged replacement property can create mortgage boot even where all sale cash was reinvested.

The defense is mundane and effective: review the settlement statement line by line with the qualified intermediary and tax advisor **before** closing, and pay non-exchange costs from outside funds.

## How to Avoid Boot

Use the napkin test with discipline:

- **Trade up, not down:** Buy replacement property with total value at least equal to the property sold.
- **Reinvest every equity dollar:** Do not retain cash or leave unused proceeds with the intermediary.
- **Replace the debt:** Take on at least as much debt as was paid off, or add personal cash for the difference.
- **Watch the closing statement:** Keep non-exchange costs off the exchange ledger.

Sometimes a direct replacement cannot absorb the exact proceeds. A [Delaware Statutory Trust](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) can accept almost any dollar amount, which may allow residual equity that would otherwise become cash boot to remain in the exchange.

## When Taking Boot Is the Right Decision

Tax deferral should not become an instruction to make a poor investment decision. An investor may genuinely need cash to pay other debt, diversify, or fund a life event. A calculated partial exchange can then be cleaner than reshaping an otherwise unsuitable purchase to defer a small additional amount of gain.

The mistake is not necessarily taking boot. It is taking boot without knowing it, or allowing the deferral to override the actual financial goal. Decide in advance how much cash, if any, should come out, and structure the exchange to that decision.

## Multiple Properties: One Exchange Equation

Boot is calculated across the exchange **as a whole**, not property by property. You may sell one property and acquire several, or sell several and acquire one. The IRS nets the cash and debt across the combined transaction.

Add up the value, equity, and debt surrendered; then compare them with the total value, equity, and debt acquired. Total new debt that meets or exceeds total old debt means no mortgage boot. Full reinvestment of total equity means no cash boot. The same asymmetry remains: cash paid into the deal can offset total debt relief, while cash taken out cannot be cured by adding debt elsewhere in the exchange.

That can expand the choices. A second replacement property, or a fractional interest such as a [DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html), can absorb the remainder. The transaction is judged as one equation, not several separate ones.

## Special Cases That Need Extra Care

### Related-Party Exchanges

Under Section **1031(f)**, a related-party exchange generally requires both parties to hold their properties for at least **two years**. An early disposition can unwind the deferral and trigger recognized gain. These rules are aimed at preventing basis shifting and are easy to get wrong without advance advice.

### Seller-Carried Financing

A promissory note received from the buyer is not like-kind property, so it can be boot. It may be brought into the exchange, for example by assigning the note to the qualified intermediary or purchasing it personally so that cash, rather than the note, funds the replacement property.

### Refinancing Near the Exchange

Pulling equity through a refinance immediately before or after an exchange can be viewed as disguised boot under step-transaction reasoning. An independent business purpose and meaningful separation in time can reduce the risk, but this is a transaction to review with an advisor before acting.

None of these situations is automatically fatal. They reward planning. Boot is seldom surprising to a prepared investor and often surprising to everyone else.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is boot always taxable?

Yes, but only up to realized gain. If realized gain is lower than the boot received, the taxable amount is capped at the gain.

### Can I offset mortgage boot with cash?

Yes. Personal cash added to the replacement purchase can offset a debt reduction and eliminate mortgage boot. More debt cannot offset cash boot; cash received remains boot.

### How is boot taxed if I have a lot of depreciation?

Depreciation recapture is generally recognized first. Recognized boot can be treated as prior depreciation, taxed up to **25%**, before ordinary capital gain, so even modest boot can carry a higher effective rate.

### Are closing costs considered boot?

Some can be. Customary transaction costs such as commissions and title fees generally do not create boot, but non-exchange items such as prorated rents and certain loan charges can if paid from exchange funds. Review the settlement statement with your advisors.

### Can I do a partial 1031 exchange on purpose?

Yes. You can deliberately retain a specified amount of cash or reduce debt, pay tax on that boot, and defer the remaining gain. A partial exchange is a legitimate, common strategy.

### How do I defer 100% of my gain?

Buy replacement property of equal or greater value, reinvest all equity, and carry at least as much debt as was paid off, or substitute personal cash for debt not replaced. Satisfy all three and there is no boot.

## Glossary

- **Boot:** Non-like-kind value received in an exchange, usually cash or net debt relief, taxable up to realized gain.
- **Cash (Equity) Boot:** Sale proceeds or cash not reinvested in the replacement property.
- **Mortgage (Debt) Boot:** A net reduction in debt between relinquished and replacement properties, taxable unless offset with cash.
- **Realized Gain:** Total economic gain on the sale; it caps the amount of boot that can be taxed.
- **Napkin Test:** The rule of thumb for full deferral: trade up in value, reinvest all equity, and replace all debt.
- **Partial Exchange:** An exchange in which the investor intentionally takes some boot and defers the remaining gain.
