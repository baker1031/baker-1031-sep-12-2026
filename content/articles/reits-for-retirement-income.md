# REITs for Retirement Income

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about the gap between an income source that looks attractive on a statement and one that will still be useful when a retiree needs it most. A high distribution yield is not the same thing as a dependable retirement paycheck. Still, real estate investment trusts can earn a deliberate place in an income plan when their risks are kept in proportion.

REITs can offer relatively higher, real-estate-backed income, diversification, and some inflation-protection potential. They can also be liquid and passive when publicly traded. But they carry equity-like and interest-rate risk, their distributions are never guaranteed, and account placement changes the after-tax result. For the structural foundation, see this [in-depth REIT guide](reits-guide.html). The question here is narrower: how can a retiree use REIT income without mistaking it for a bond coupon or making it the foundation of the plan?

## Why Retirees Use REITs

Income is the core attraction. The REIT structure requires a REIT to distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders. That requirement often produces a relatively higher yield than the broad stock market, backed by income-producing property such as apartments, warehouses, medical buildings, and net-lease retail. For someone whose portfolio needs to provide cash flow rather than simply compound, that is a useful starting point.

REITs can add more than yield. They broaden a retirement portfolio’s sources of return through real estate exposure that behaves somewhat differently from stocks and bonds. Rents and distributions can rise over time, which offers some inflation-protection potential. Publicly traded REITs are also passive and liquid: an investor can own real estate without handling tenants, maintenance, or financing and can adjust the position as needs change.

The first-order attraction is obvious: higher current income. The second-order question is whether that income can survive vacancy, rates, a cut in distributions, and the rest of a retiree’s withdrawal needs. That is why REITs belong in an integrated plan, not in a yield contest.

## Income Stability Considerations

REIT distributions are not guaranteed. A REIT can reduce or suspend its dividend when its underlying income falls because of rising vacancies, tenant defaults, lower rents, higher operating costs, or higher interest rates. That can reduce cash flow at the same time a share price is under pressure. REIT income should therefore be treated as relatively higher but variable, not as a bond-like guaranteed payment.

Several habits can improve the odds of stability without eliminating risk. Diversifying across sectors and many REITs reduces the impact of one holding’s cut or one sector’s downturn. Favor distributions that are comfortably covered by adjusted funds from operations, or AFFO, rather than fragile high yields. Durable-income sectors such as net-lease, healthcare, and certain residential real estate can offer long leases and creditworthy tenants. Just as important, size the allocation so ordinary price and income volatility remain manageable.

Treat the distribution as a cash-flow contributor, not a contractual promise. An AFFO-covered payout and a diversified set of tenants do not make a dividend certain, but they can make the plan less dependent on a single favorable outcome.

## Inflation Protection

Inflation matters more when income has to last for decades. REITs own real assets, and rents can rise through contractual escalations, higher market rents, or re-leasing. That can allow cash flow and distributions to keep pace with inflation in a way a fixed bond coupon cannot.

This is potential, not a guarantee. The ability to raise rents varies by sector and lease structure. Shorter-lease sectors, including some residential and hotel REITs, can reset rents to market more quickly. Very long fixed leases may adjust more slowly, though many include escalators. Inflation also often arrives with rising interest rates, which can pressure REIT share prices in the short term even while rents rise.

The useful frame is an inflation-hedging tendency, not a precise hedge. Real assets can help protect purchasing power, but rate sensitivity remains part of the bargain.

## Tax-Efficient Placement

Where a retiree holds REITs can change the net income received. Most REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at lower qualified-dividend rates. For that reason, traditional or Roth IRAs can often be sensible homes for REIT ordinary dividends: the distributions are not taxed annually inside the account, allowing the income to compound or be drawn without the annual ordinary-income drag of a taxable account.

There is an important trade-off. The Section 199A deduction provides a 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends, but it applies only in taxable accounts. Holding REITs in an IRA shelters ordinary income but gives up the 199A deduction. Holding them in a taxable account exposes the dividend to ordinary rates but can capture that deduction. The appropriate choice depends on tax bracket, account mix, and the larger plan.

Asset location—choosing which accounts hold which investments—is a real after-tax lever. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice, so coordinate the placement decision with your tax advisor.

## Key Takeaways

- Retirees use REITs for relatively higher, real-estate-backed income, diversification, inflation potential, and passive liquidity.
- REIT income is not guaranteed. Diversification, sustainable AFFO-covered distributions, durable sectors, and sensible sizing can improve resilience.
- Real assets and rents can provide inflation-protection potential, but it is a tendency rather than a guarantee and it comes with rate sensitivity.
- Ordinary REIT dividends are often sheltered in IRAs, while the 20% Section 199A deduction helps only in taxable accounts.

## Building a Retirement REIT Sleeve

A retirement REIT sleeve is a dedicated real-estate-income allocation inside a broader retirement portfolio. It sits alongside bonds, dividend stocks, cash, and other assets. It is not the whole portfolio. The sleeve should be sized so that REIT volatility or a distribution cut does not jeopardize the overall income plan. The broader allocation question is covered in [how much of a portfolio should be in REITs](how-much-of-your-portfolio-should-be-reits.html).

Within that sleeve, diversify across sectors and holdings. A broad REIT ETF can provide one route; a laddered set of individual REITs can provide another. Favor sustainable, AFFO-covered distributions and durable-income sectors. Consider tax-efficient placement, often in IRAs, and stagger payment dates if regular monthly cash flow matters.

Maintain the sleeve through periodic review and rebalancing as distributions, prices, and personal needs change. Risk tolerance, time horizon, and income needs should determine its size, leaving room for the volatility that will eventually arrive. A sleeve is a useful image: one part of the portfolio, never the whole suit.

## REITs vs. Other Retirement Income Sources

REITs work best when compared honestly with the alternatives. Versus bonds, they may offer higher yields and inflation potential through rising rents, but they also bring equity-like volatility and no guaranteed principal. Bonds provide defined contractual income and return principal at maturity, subject to credit risk. They can anchor the stable portion of a plan, especially for essential expenses. REITs are a complement, not a substitute.

Versus dividend stocks, REITs add real estate diversification and generally higher yields because of the 90% distribution rule, but they are often more sensitive to interest rates. Versus direct rental property, [REITs can provide passive, liquid, diversified real estate income](reit-vs-rental-property-for-passive-income.html) without landlord work or single-property concentration.

For an investor exiting direct property with a taxable gain, the tax path matters. A REIT share is a security, not like-kind real property. A [DST can be 1031-eligible while providing passive income](reit-vs-dst-for-retirement-income.html), and it may later have a route into a REIT structure through a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html). Each income source has different trade-offs. A diversified retirement plan can use more than one rather than relying on a single answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do retirees use REITs for income?

REITs often pay relatively higher yields than the broad stock market because they must distribute at least 90% of taxable income. The income is backed by real estate, and public REITs can give retirees passive, liquid exposure without tenants, maintenance, or financing to manage. They also add real estate diversification and some inflation potential because rents and distributions can rise. The limits matter: REIT income is not guaranteed and carries equity-like risk. Use REITs as a right-sized component of a diversified retirement income plan rather than relying on them alone.

### Is REIT income reliable enough for retirement?

It can contribute meaningfully, but it is variable rather than guaranteed. Vacancies, tenant defaults, lower rents, higher operating costs, and rising rates can reduce a REIT’s income, dividend, and share price. Improve resilience by diversifying across many REITs and sectors, favoring sustainable AFFO-covered distributions, emphasizing durable-income sectors such as net-lease, healthcare, and certain residential real estate, and right-sizing the sleeve. Pair it with steadier sources such as bonds instead of treating it as the sole support for retirement cash flow.

### Do REITs protect against inflation?

REITs have inflation-protection potential because they own real assets and can raise rents through contractual escalations, market increases, or re-leasing. That can allow distributions to rise while a fixed bond coupon does not. But the protection depends on sector and lease structure. Shorter leases can reset faster; long fixed leases may adjust slowly, even though many contain escalators. Rising inflation can also mean rising interest rates and short-term price pressure for REITs. Think of this as a tendency, not a guaranteed inflation shield.

### Where should a retiree hold REITs for tax efficiency?

Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, so many retirees place them in traditional or Roth IRAs to shelter annual tax and permit compounding or withdrawals without that annual drag. The counterweight is Section 199A: qualified REIT dividends receive a 20% deduction only in taxable accounts. An IRA shelters the income but gives up the deduction; a taxable account carries ordinary rates but captures it. Tax bracket, account mix, and the overall plan decide the answer. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; ask your tax advisor about your situation.

### What is a retirement REIT sleeve?

It is a dedicated real-estate-income allocation inside a broader retirement portfolio, deliberately sized to risk tolerance and integrated with bonds, dividend stocks, cash, and other holdings. “Sleeve” means component, not entire portfolio. A modest, well-diversified sleeve can add yield and real estate exposure without making one distribution cut decisive. It can use a broad REIT ETF, a laddered set of REITs, or both; favor AFFO-covered distributions and durable sectors, place holdings tax efficiently, stagger payments for cash-flow timing, and rebalance as needs and conditions change.

### How large should my REIT allocation be in retirement?

There is no universal number. The right size depends on income needs, risk tolerance, time horizon, and the rest of the portfolio. Because REITs have equity-like and interest-rate risk and distributions can be cut, a measured allocation is generally more defensible than an outsized one. Diversification within the sleeve matters as much as total size; a single REIT or sector can concentrate risk. A financial advisor can assess the complete situation, and a non-traded REIT or DST also requires a suitability review. Revisit the allocation as retirement circumstances change.

### Are REITs better than bonds for retirement income?

Neither is simply better; they serve different jobs. Bonds provide defined contractual income and return principal at maturity, subject to credit risk, making them a more predictable resource for essential expenses. REITs may offer higher yield and inflation potential because rents can rise, but bring equity volatility, rate sensitivity, and no guaranteed principal. A retirement plan can use bonds for predictable cash flow and a right-sized REIT sleeve for extra yield, real estate diversification, and inflation potential. Treat REITs as a higher-risk, higher-potential-reward complement, not as a bond substitute.

### Can REIT distributions be cut during retirement?

Yes. A REIT can reduce or suspend its dividend if income falls from vacancies, tenant defaults, lower rents, higher costs, or higher interest rates. A cut can arrive when the share price is also falling. The risk cannot be eliminated, but it can be managed: diversify among REITs and sectors, seek distributions well covered by AFFO, favor durable-income sectors and creditworthy tenants, and keep the allocation modest. Bonds and Social Security can cover essential expenses while variable REIT income plays a supporting role.

### Should I use individual REITs, REIT ETFs, or both in retirement?

All three approaches can work. A broad REIT ETF offers low-cost, liquid, immediate diversification across many REITs and sectors and can be a simple core. Individual REITs allow a retiree to favor durable-income sectors, sustainable payers, or particular payment schedules, but they require research, monitoring, and enough holdings for diversification. A common approach uses an ETF core plus selected individual REITs or sector ETFs to refine timing and sector exposure. The choice depends on how much control and effort you want; sustainable distributions and appropriate sizing remain the priority.

### How are REIT distributions taxed in retirement?

Most REIT ordinary dividends are taxed as ordinary income because the REIT itself paid no corporate tax. A 20% Section 199A deduction applies to qualified REIT dividends, reducing the effective top federal rate on them to roughly 29.6%. The 199A deduction was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation and applies only in taxable accounts. A distribution can also include return of capital, which is not currently taxed but reduces cost basis and defers tax until sale, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gain rates. The REIT reports the breakdown on Form 1099-DIV. The after-tax result depends on distribution character and account location. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; verify your treatment with your tax advisor.

### Can I move from owning rental property into REITs for retirement?

Yes, but the tax route changes the outcome. Selling rental property and buying REIT shares generally triggers capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture because REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property, and do not qualify for a 1031 exchange. A Delaware Statutory Trust, or DST, can be 1031-eligible like-kind property. A seller can exchange proceeds into a DST for passive income while deferring gain. A DST property can later be acquired by a REIT through a 721, or UPREIT, exchange, converting the interest into REIT operating-partnership units while preserving deferral. If capital is new or deferral is unnecessary, REITs can be purchased directly for liquid, diversified passive income. Coordinate with your tax advisor.

### What are the risks of relying on REITs in retirement?

Key risks include distribution cuts, public share-price declines, interest-rate pressure, and—particularly for mortgage REITs—pressure on income spreads. Sequence-of-returns risk also matters: a market decline early in retirement combined with withdrawals can do more damage than the same decline later. Concentrating in REITs or one sector amplifies each risk. Inflation protection is not guaranteed. Manage the exposure by diversifying within and beyond REITs, favoring sustainable distributions, right-sizing the sleeve, covering essentials with steadier sources, and keeping a cash buffer. REITs can be one diversified component, not the foundation, of retirement income. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How do I build a steady monthly income from REITs in retirement?

Start by staggering payment dates. Most REITs pay quarterly on one of three cycles: Jan/Apr/Jul/Oct, Feb/May/Aug/Nov, or Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec. Combining holdings across the three cycles, and adding any monthly payers, can create monthly rather than quarterly-lump cash flow. That is a REIT income ladder. Diversify across sectors, prefer AFFO-covered distributions and durable-income sectors, place holdings tax efficiently—often in IRAs—and decide whether to reinvest or take cash. Review and rebalance as payments and prices change. Keep other stable income sources for essentials because REIT income remains variable; a financial advisor can help construct and maintain the sleeve.

## Glossary

**Retirement REIT Sleeve:** A right-sized real-estate-income allocation within a retirement portfolio.

**Distribution Yield:** The annual dividend as a percentage of share price.

**Income Stability:** How reliable and steady a REIT’s distributions are.

**Distribution-Cut Risk:** The risk that a REIT reduces or suspends its dividend.

**Inflation Protection:** Rents and distributions rising to help income keep pace.

**Real Assets:** Tangible property that can rise in value with inflation.

**AFFO:** Adjusted funds from operations, used to assess distribution coverage.

**Sustainable Distribution:** A payout comfortably covered by AFFO cash flow.

**Tax-Advantaged Account:** An IRA or 401(k) sheltering REIT ordinary income.

**Asset Location:** Choosing which accounts hold which investments for tax efficiency.

**Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends in taxable accounts only.

**Ordinary Income:** How most REIT dividends are taxed.

**Diversification:** Spreading holdings across sectors and REITs to reduce risk.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** 1031-eligible passive real estate for deferring a property-sale gain.

**721 / UPREIT Exchange:** Contributing property to a REIT for OP units while preserving deferral.

**90% Distribution Rule:** The REIT rule producing the relatively higher yields retirees use.
