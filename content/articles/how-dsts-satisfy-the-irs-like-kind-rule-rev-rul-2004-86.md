# How DSTs Satisfy the IRS Like-Kind Rule (Rev. Rul. 2004-86)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often a DST is described as “passive” without saying what that passivity costs. It is not a marketing detail. The narrow operating limits are central to the tax characterization that lets a properly structured DST serve as 1031 replacement property.

At first glance, a beneficial interest in a trust does not look like real estate. A 1031 exchange requires like-kind real property. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 explains why a properly structured Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) interest can meet that standard: for federal-tax purposes, it is treated as a direct interest in the real property beneath the trust, not as a partnership interest or ordinary business interest. The same restrictions that make this possible also limit what the trust can do.

This article is educational, not tax or legal advice. A first-order response is to hear that a DST “qualifies” and stop there. A second-order response asks whether the particular trust is properly structured and operated, whether the exchange requirements are satisfied, and whether the loss of flexibility fits the investor’s goals. Verify current rules and your particular facts with a CPA and attorney.

## What Revenue Ruling 2004-86 Says

Revenue Ruling 2004-86, issued by the IRS in 2004, is the foundation for using certain DST interests in [1031 exchanges](1031-exchange-guide.html). The ruling considered a specific arrangement: an investor exchanges into a beneficial interest in a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) that holds rental real estate. The trustee has narrowly limited powers, and investors remain passive. The question was how the investor’s beneficial interest should be characterized for federal-tax purposes.

The ruling reached two linked conclusions. First, a properly structured DST is an investment trust—a grantor-type arrangement—rather than a partnership or other business entity. The reasoning is that the trustee is not conducting a business; it is holding property under strict constraints. Second, because the trust is treated that way, each beneficial interest is treated as a direct ownership interest in the underlying real property.

That second conclusion is the essential one for an exchange. A properly structured beneficial interest is treated as real property and therefore can be like-kind to other real estate. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 does not turn every trust interest into replacement property. It establishes the federal-tax treatment of a DST that has the required structure and operating limits.

## Why a DST Interest Is Real Property

Section 1031 requires like-kind real property. It also expressly excludes partnership interests. If a DST were treated as a partnership, an investor could not use its beneficial interest as replacement property. The DST structure avoids that result only because it is built to operate as a passive investment trust rather than as an active business.

The trustee’s powers are limited to holding and maintaining the property. It cannot run the arrangement in a way that raises capital, varies investors’ interests, or actively operates a business. In those circumstances, the IRS looks through the trust to the real estate beneath it. Each beneficial owner is treated as holding a proportionate direct interest, much as a tenant-in-common holds an undivided interest in a property.

This look-through treatment also explains an apparent contradiction that is not actually a contradiction. A DST interest is generally a security in the way it is offered and sold. Yet for a qualifying 1031 exchange, the same interest can be treated as real property for federal-tax purposes. Securities law and tax law are answering different questions.

> Revenue Ruling 2004-86 matters because it treats a properly structured DST as an investment trust, not a partnership. Partnership interests are excluded from 1031 exchanges; real property can qualify.

## Conditions for Like-Kind Treatment

The favorable treatment is conditional. A trust must be structured and operated so it remains a passive investment trust rather than a business entity. If it begins raising new capital, reinvesting actively, or conducting the property as a business, it can risk reclassification. The look-through treatment could be lost, and with it the intended 1031 qualification.

These conditions are often collected under the name [“seven deadly sins”](the-seven-deadly-sins-dst-trustee-restrictions.html). A properly structured DST trustee generally cannot:

1. Accept new capital contributions after the offering closes.
2. Refinance or renegotiate existing debt, or borrow new funds.
3. Reinvest proceeds after the property is sold.
4. Make more than minor, non-structural improvements to the property.
5. Reserve cash beyond what is reasonably needed for ordinary operations.
6. Enter into new leases or renegotiate existing leases; a master-lease structure is often used so leasing remains passive.
7. Exercise powers beyond passive holding and maintenance of the property.

The purpose of every restriction is the same: the trust must not look like an active business. Its passivity preserves the investor’s direct-real-property treatment. That is why the restrictions are not just administrative details. They are the qualification.

## The Trustee Restrictions Behind It

The restrictions explain both the DST’s usefulness and its limits. A passive trust can collect rent, pay expenses and debt service, maintain property, sell it, and distribute proceeds to investors. It cannot respond to every problem the way a direct owner can.

Because it cannot raise new capital or refinance, the trust needs enough capital and reserves at the outset for its intended holding period. It cannot solve a later capital need by calling investors for more money or taking out a new loan. Because it cannot make major improvements or reinvest sale proceeds, it is not a vehicle for an active value-add plan. And because it cannot refinance, an investor cannot later take equity out through a cash-out refinance.

That is a material comparison with direct ownership or a TIC. A direct owner or TIC co-owner can often refinance, improve, and reposition property. A DST investor gives up those levers in exchange for passive, fractional ownership and the desired 1031 tax treatment.

Sometimes sponsors use a “springing LLC” provision for an emergency, such as a major capital need or a property that requires active repositioning. A conversion can provide a path for the situation the DST cannot handle, but it ends the property’s 1031 eligibility going forward. The backstop does not erase the trade-off.

### Key Takeaways

- Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a properly structured DST as an investment trust, so each beneficial interest is treated as a direct interest in underlying real property and can be 1031-eligible.
- The interest is like-kind because the trust is not a partnership, which Section 1031 excludes; the IRS looks through to the real estate.
- The favorable treatment depends on strict limits: no new capital, refinancing, reinvestment, or more than minor improvements, among others.
- Those limits also restrict the investment: no cash-out refinancing and no active value-add plan. Passivity is both the qualification and the constraint.

## How DST Like-Kind Treatment Compares

Compared with owning a whole property directly, a DST can provide the same general 1031 deferral and real-property characterization in a passive, fractional form. The investor does not manage the property or personally qualify for its loan. The trade-off is flexibility. A direct owner can refinance, improve, or reposition property; a DST generally cannot.

Compared with a tenancy-in-common (TIC), both structures can be like-kind real property. A TIC is a deeded undivided interest. A qualifying DST interest reaches real-property treatment through the Revenue Ruling 2004-86 look-through. A TIC can permit refinancing and a later cash-out; the DST’s restrictions prohibit those actions.

The contrast with a REIT is sharper. A REIT share is an interest in a company and is a security, not direct like-kind real property. An investor cannot sell investment real estate and use a 1031 exchange to acquire a REIT share directly. There can be an indirect bridge: an investor may exchange into a DST, and the DST property may later be acquired by a REIT through a 721 (UPREIT) exchange, converting the position into operating-partnership units while preserving deferral. That outcome is technical and must be confirmed with the investor’s CPA.

The DST’s distinct role is therefore passive, fractional ownership of real property that can qualify for 1031 treatment. It offers less control than direct ownership or a TIC, and a different tax result from a direct REIT share.

## What This Means for Your Exchange

For a 1031 exchange, a properly structured DST can be valid replacement property. An investor may [identify and acquire a DST interest within the exchange](dst-1031-exchange-using-a-dst-as-replacement-property.html), and if all relevant requirements are satisfied, defer capital-gains tax as with directly owned real estate. The DST may also help satisfy the [debt-replacement requirement](1031-exchange-debt-replacement-mortgage-boot.html): non-recourse financing held by the trust is allocated proportionately to investors, so the investor may replace debt without personally applying for or guaranteeing a new loan.

That can be helpful for investors seeking a passive replacement property and for investors who cannot easily obtain new financing. It is not a blanket answer. The same restrictions that allow the DST to qualify mean no refinancing, no new capital, no reinvestment of sale proceeds, and no later cash-out. The particular DST also must be properly structured and operated, which is why sponsor quality and legal structure deserve careful review.

Before committing, confirm the treatment, debt allocation, timing, and fit for your exchange with a qualified intermediary, CPA, and attorney. A ruling is the legal foundation; the facts and execution determine the result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Revenue Ruling 2004-86?

Revenue Ruling 2004-86 is the 2004 IRS ruling that supports use of a properly structured DST in a 1031 exchange. It analyzed a DST holding rental real estate with passive investors and a strictly limited trustee. The IRS concluded that the DST is an investment trust rather than a partnership or business entity and that each beneficial interest is a direct interest in underlying real property. That makes a qualifying interest like-kind replacement property. This is educational background, not tax advice; confirm its application with a CPA.

### Why isn’t a DST treated as a partnership?

That classification is crucial because Section 1031 excludes partnership interests. A properly structured DST limits the trustee to passively holding and maintaining property. The trust does not conduct a business, vary investors’ interests, or actively manage in a way that would cause business-entity treatment. The IRS therefore treats it as an investment trust and looks through to the underlying real property. If the arrangement strays beyond those limits, partnership reclassification and loss of qualification become risks.

### How does a DST interest qualify as like-kind real property?

Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured DST is an investment trust whose beneficial interests are treated as direct interests in underlying real estate—not partnership or ordinary business interests. An investor is treated as holding a proportionate direct interest, like a TIC owner’s undivided interest. Because the investor is treated as acquiring real property held for investment or business use, the interest can be like-kind. The result depends on the DST being properly structured, so confirm it with a CPA.

### What are the seven deadly sins of a DST?

They are the strict trustee restrictions that preserve the trust’s tax treatment: no new capital after closing; no refinancing or new borrowing; no reinvestment of sale proceeds; no more than minor, non-structural improvements; no excess reserves; no new or renegotiated leases, often addressed through a master lease; and no powers beyond passively holding and maintaining the property. All seven keep the trust from being an active business. They make a DST qualify and limit it at the same time.

### Can a DST be used to defer capital gains in a 1031 exchange?

Yes, when a properly structured DST interest is used as like-kind replacement property and the exchange requirements are met. The DST must be identified within the 45-day identification window, acquired by the 180-day deadline, and the exchange proceeds must be handled through a qualified intermediary. The DST’s allocated non-recourse financing can also help with debt replacement. The deferral is postponed, not forgiven, and the details should be confirmed with the CPA and qualified intermediary.

### Does a DST let me replace my debt in a 1031 exchange?

It can. To fully defer gain, an investor generally needs to replace both equity and debt from the relinquished property; an unreplaced debt shortfall can create taxable boot. A DST’s non-recourse financing is allocated proportionately to the investor, which can allow the investor to satisfy some or all of the debt-replacement requirement without personally applying for, qualifying for, or guaranteeing a loan. This can be useful for retirees or other investors who do not want a new loan process. The exact allocation depends on the offering and should be checked with the CPA and qualified intermediary.

### What happens if a DST violates the seven deadly sins?

Refinancing, taking new capital, reinvesting sale proceeds, or actively operating property could cause the DST to be reclassified from an investment trust to a business entity or partnership. That could undermine look-through treatment and jeopardize the 1031 qualification investors expected. In an emergency, a springing LLC can sometimes address a major capital need or active repositioning, but its use ends the property’s 1031 eligibility going forward. Sponsor quality, sound structure, and adviser review matter.

### Why can’t a DST refinance or do a cash-out?

The refinancing restriction is one of the rules preserving passivity. Refinancing, renegotiating debt, or borrowing new funds would be an active financial decision that could threaten investment-trust treatment. The financing is set at the outset and normally remains in place for the holding period. A direct owner or TIC owner may refinance and take out equity; a DST investor cannot. If later flexibility or cash-out capacity is important, a TIC or direct ownership may be a better fit.

### Is a DST interest a security or real property?

It is both in different legal contexts. It is offered as a Regulation D private-placement security through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review, with a private placement memorandum and securities-law compliance. For a qualifying 1031 exchange, Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats the beneficial interest as a direct interest in underlying real property. The securities offering process and the tax characterization do not conflict; they answer different questions.

### Does Rev. Rul. 2004-86 guarantee my exchange will work?

No. The ruling establishes that a properly structured DST can be valid like-kind replacement property. A specific exchange still requires a qualified intermediary, identification within 45 days, closing within 180 days, correct debt and equity handling to avoid boot, investment or business use, and a DST that is properly structured and operated. The ruling creates a legal foundation, not a guarantee. A qualified intermediary, CPA, and attorney should verify the specifics.

### How is a DST different from a REIT for 1031 purposes?

A qualifying DST interest is treated as direct real property and can be used in a 1031 exchange. A REIT share is a company security and cannot be acquired directly with a 1031 exchange. An investor may exchange into a DST and, if the DST property is later acquired by a REIT through a 721 (UPREIT) transaction, receive operating-partnership units while preserving deferral. That is an indirect path, not a direct 1031 into a REIT, and it requires tax advice.

### What does properly structured mean for a DST?

It means the DST is set up and operated to satisfy Revenue Ruling 2004-86. Its documents limit the trustee to passive holding and maintenance, and the trust observes the seven restrictions: no new capital after closing, no refinancing or new borrowing, no reinvestment of sale proceeds, only minor non-structural improvements, no excess reserves, and passive leasing often through a master lease. The offering must also be conducted properly as a securities private placement. Reputable sponsors and CPA and attorney review are important because the qualification is at risk if the limits are not observed.

No. This article provides educational background, not tax or legal advice for a specific transaction. Like-kind treatment, debt replacement, deadlines, and the structure of an individual DST are technical, fact-dependent issues. Before using a DST, engage a qualified intermediary, ask a CPA to confirm treatment and timing, and have an attorney review the structure and offering documents. Rules can change, so current verification matters.

### Has the IRS reaffirmed DST treatment since 2004?

Revenue Ruling 2004-86 remains the controlling federal authority on properly structured DST beneficial interests, and the IRS has not reversed it. DSTs have been used in exchanges on the ruling’s strength for two decades. The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act limited 1031 exchanges to real property beginning in 2018, but that change did not undermine DSTs because qualifying DST interests are treated as real property. Tax authorities and rules can evolve, so ask a CPA to confirm the current law and its application.

## Glossary

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86.** The IRS ruling making properly structured DST interests 1031-eligible real property.

**Like-Kind Property.** Real property of the same nature that can be eligible for a 1031 exchange.

**Beneficial Interest.** A DST investor’s fractional stake, treated as direct real property for a qualifying exchange.

**Investment Trust.** A passive trust that holds property without conducting a business.

**Look-Through Treatment.** Treating a DST interest as a direct interest in underlying real estate.

**Partnership Exclusion.** Section 1031’s bar on partnership interests in an exchange.

**Seven Deadly Sins.** Trustee restrictions that keep a DST passive and 1031-eligible.

**Trustee.** The party with strictly limited powers to hold and maintain the property.

**New Capital Restriction.** The bar on the trust accepting new contributions after closing.

**Refinancing Restriction.** The bar on refinancing or taking on new debt.

**Master Lease.** A structure used to handle DST leasing passively.

**Springing LLC.** An emergency provision that converts a DST to an LLC and ends 1031 eligibility.

**Debt Replacement.** Matching relinquished-property debt through allocated DST financing.

**Non-Recourse Loan.** Debt secured only by the property and allocated to DST investors.

**Boot.** Non-like-kind value, such as unreplaced debt, that can be taxable.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST).** A trust owning real estate; its interests can qualify as like-kind under Rev. Rul. 2004-86.
