# Identifying Replacement Property: Strategy & Backups

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how often a 1031 exchange is decided before the sale closes. The decision is not only which property an investor prefers. It is whether the identification list still works if that preferred property stalls.

The first-order response is to identify the one deal that looks best. The second-order response is to ask what remains available after day 45, when no replacement can be added or changed. A sound list pairs ambition with a route to completion: strong primaries and reliable backups.

## Building your identification list

Start before the 45-day window, ideally before the sale. The goal is to enter the [identification period](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) with researched candidates, so the window is for confirming a plan rather than a [frantic search for replacement property](how-to-find-1031-replacement-property-fast.html).

The candidates should fit the investor's goals—income, growth, diversification, or passivity—and the value and debt targets. For full deferral, the replacement generally needs equal-or-greater value and replaced debt. That narrows the list to property that can actually fit the exchange.

A good list holds both the [replacement property](what-is-replacement-property-1031.html) most wanted and at least one backup that is certain to close if the primary stalls. Direct deals can be attractive but uncertain. A reliable backup gives the exchange a path forward.

Respect the identification rules, use proper descriptions, and include only candidates that are genuinely acquirable. An implausible property consumes a limited slot without providing useful protection. Every item should have a job: fit for the primary, or certainty for the backup.

## Primary vs. backup properties

A primary is the first-choice replacement: the property that best fits the investor's plan and is intended to close if diligence, financing, and negotiations work. It is often a direct acquisition in progress.

A backup has a different role. It is identified as a fallback, chosen for its probability of closing if the primary collapses, financing fails, or the deal cannot close by day 180. A backup need not be the ideal property; it must be dependable when needed.

This distinction matters because identifying only a primary leaves the exchange exposed. If that deal fails after day 45, there is no new item to add. Identifying a backup costs nothing unless it is used, and it converts a stalled-primary problem into a manageable contingency.

The practical habit is simple: pair the property you want with one that can close. The former reflects upside. The latter protects the deferral.

## Why DSTs make ideal backups

[Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html) are often used as backups because they offer speed and certainty. A DST is a pre-assembled, securitized interest in institutional real estate that the IRS treats as direct real-property ownership for 1031 purposes. The trust already exists and the real estate is already acquired, so it can close in days rather than the weeks or months that a direct acquisition can require.

That speed can matter late in the 180-day period. A direct deal can fail after an inspection issue, a financing problem, or a seller's decision. A DST that is structured and available gives an investor the ability to pivot while there is still time to close.

A DST does not require new financing from the investor, lengthy negotiation, or a late diligence race. Leveraged DSTs have pre-arranged debt that can also help with debt matching. Their known value makes them straightforward to identify alongside a primary under the three-property rule.

For those reasons—speed, certainty, no new financing, ease of identification, and debt-matching help—DSTs have become a standard backup for [1031 exchangers](1031-exchange-guide.html). DSTs are securities offered through a broker-dealer and require a suitability review, but their role as an identified contingency can be especially useful.

## Applying the identification rules

The **three-property rule** permits up to three properties of any value. It is the normal fit for one direct primary and one or two DST backups. A high-value primary and a DST backup can be identified without a value cap.

The **200% rule** permits any number of properties as long as their combined value does not exceed 200% of what was sold. It can suit a diversified basket of multiple replacements, such as several DSTs. Its trade-off is clear: the total identified value cannot be more than twice the sale value.

The **95% rule** permits any number and any value but requires acquisition of at least 95% of the total identified value. That is a strict threshold, which is why most exchangers rely instead on the three-property rule or the 200% rule.

The choice follows the strategy: a primary plus backups under the three-property rule, or a diversified set under the 200% rule. An adviser can help match the rule to the number and value of candidates.

## Avoiding a failed exchange

Identification mistakes often create the failed exchange. The familiar case is one identified property that stalls after day 45. With no fallback, the exchange fails and the full tax is triggered. A backup—often a fast-closing DST—addresses that central risk.

Other mistakes matter too. Naming property that cannot realistically be acquired wastes slots. Vague descriptions, late notice, or delivery to the wrong party can invalidate an identification. Exceeding the selected rule's limits—more than three under the three-property rule or more than 200% under the 200% rule—can invalidate the whole list. The notice must go correctly to the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) by day 45.

The better approach is to prepare before the sale: research the candidates, choose the primary, identify a certain backup, and choose the governing rule. Improvising as day 45 approaches can turn a property search into an avoidable tax risk.

## Key takeaways

- Build the identification list before the sale around goals, value and debt targets, primaries, and a certain backup.
- Pair the primary with a backup that can close; identifying only one property is a common cause of failed exchanges.
- DSTs can be useful backups because they can close quickly, require no new financing, are easy to identify, and can help with debt matching.
- Use the appropriate rule, deliver the notice correctly to the QI by day 45, and avoid the errors that can invalidate the list.

## Timing the identification well

The 45-day deadline is absolute. A prudent internal deadline is a week or two earlier—day 30, for example. That buffer allows time to prepare, sign, and deliver the written notice to the QI, and to adjust if a candidate drops out before the list is final.

Delivery mechanics are as important as timing. The written notice must describe each property unambiguously by address or legal description, be signed by the exchanger, and reach the QI, not the real-estate agent or attorney, on or before day 45. The QI's form is the normal way to preserve these requirements.

Early finalization changes the rest of the exchange. With a primary and certain backup already identified, an investor can pursue the primary without pretending that its closing is guaranteed. That is the practical value of the buffer.

## Sample identification strategies

The classic **primary plus DST backup** uses the three-property rule. Identify the direct property being pursued and one or two fast-closing DSTs. Pursue the primary, knowing a DST can complete the exchange if the primary fails.

A **diversified DST basket** treats several DSTs as intended replacements, not backups. An investor pursuing diversification and passivity might identify three DSTs under the three-property rule, or a larger group under the 200% rule, and close on all of them. [Multiple replacement-property options](1031-exchange-replacement-property-options-compared.html) can be used from the outset rather than only as insurance.

A **two strong primaries plus a backup** approach keeps two desirable direct choices available under the three-property rule while retaining a DST fallback. The risky alternative is **all-direct with no backup**: it may be appropriate only with unusual certainty that a direct deal will close. The stronger strategies build certainty into the list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is identification the most important step?

After day 45, the identified list is the full menu from which the exchanger can close. If the only identified property stalls, the exchange can fail. A primary plus a certain backup is the risk management that protects the deferral.

### How do I build an identification list?

Prepare before the sale. Research candidates against income, growth, diversification, passivity, and value and debt targets. Identify the preferred primaries and at least one certain backup, comply with the rules, describe every candidate properly, and include only property that can realistically be acquired.

### What's the difference between a primary and a backup?

A primary is the desired first choice, usually selected for fit. A backup is identified for closing certainty and is there if the primary fails. The two choices solve different problems and a prudent list includes both.

### Should I always identify a backup?

Almost always. A primary can fail after day 45, leaving no replacement option if no backup is identified. A backup preserves an option at no cost unless used and turns the biggest exchange risk into a contingency.

### Why do DSTs make ideal backups?

DSTs are pre-assembled and can close in days, which helps when a primary stalls late in the 180-day period. They require no new financing, leveraged DSTs can assist debt matching, they require little late diligence, and their known value is easy to identify.

### What are the identification rules?

The three-property rule allows up to three properties of any value and fits primary-plus-backup lists. The 200% rule allows any number within 200% of relinquished value and fits diversified baskets. The 95% rule permits any number only if 95% of identified value is acquired, so it is rarely used.

### Which identification rule should I use?

Usually the three-property rule for a primary and one or two backups. Use the 200% rule when a diversified basket of more than three properties is desired within its value limit. The 95% rule is rarely suitable because of the acquisition requirement.

### What causes a failed exchange at identification?

The most common cause is one identified property that stalls. Other causes include unrealistic candidates, vague descriptions, late delivery, delivery to the wrong party, or exceeding the selected rule's limit. A rule-compliant notice to the QI and a backup reduce these risks.

### When should I finalize my identification?

Set an internal target one or two weeks before day 45—day 30 is a useful example. That leaves time to prepare, sign, and deliver the notice and to replace a candidate before the final list is committed.

### How do I deliver the identification correctly?

Send a written, signed notice that describes each property unambiguously by address or legal description to the QI, not the agent or attorney, on or before day 45. The QI's form and receipt process help document validity.

### Can I change my identification after day 45?

No. The exchanger cannot add, change, or substitute property after the deadline. The list must include every option that might be needed before the window closes.

### Does identifying a DST backup commit me to buying it?

No. Identification preserves the option to acquire a property; it does not require acquisition. If the primary closes, the backup is not used. That is why the DST backup can be effective insurance.

### What's the most common identification strategy?

It is a direct primary plus one or two fast-closing DST backups under the three-property rule. The primary is pursued, and the DST provides an alternate route if that deal falls through.

### Can I use DSTs as my main replacements, not just backups?

Yes. A diversified DST basket can use several DSTs as the intended replacements under the three-property or 200% rule. The investor closes on the group, using the DSTs' certainty for the full replacement plan.

### What identification strategy is riskiest?

An all-direct list with no certain backup. If the direct deals stall after day 45, there is no route to replace them. It is suitable only when there is unusual confidence in a direct closing.

## Glossary

**Identification:** Written naming of replacement candidates within 45 days, delivered to the QI.

**Identification List:** The candidate set, ideally including primaries and a certain backup.

**Primary Property:** The desired first-choice replacement.

**Backup Property:** A fallback selected for closing certainty if the primary fails.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A pre-assembled, fast-closing securitized real-property interest that can serve as a backup.

**3-Property Rule:** An identification method allowing up to three properties of any value.

**200% Rule:** An identification method allowing any number of properties up to 200% of relinquished value.

**95% Rule:** An identification method requiring acquisition of 95% of identified value; it is rarely used.

**45-Day Identification Period:** The window after sale for written replacement-property identification.

**Identification Notice:** The signed written notice describing candidates and delivered to the QI by day 45.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party to whom the identification notice is delivered.

**Failed Exchange:** An exchange that does not complete, often because of identification mistakes, triggering the full tax.

**Value and Debt Targets:** The equal-or-greater value and debt replacement needed to fully defer, which guide the list.

**Diversified Basket:** Multiple replacements, often DSTs, identified under the 200% rule to spread risk.

**Buffer:** Finalizing the list before day 45 to leave preparation and change time.

**Suitability Review:** The assessment that a DST is appropriate for an investor.
