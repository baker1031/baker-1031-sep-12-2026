# Types of REITs: Equity, Mortgage & Hybrid

I keep seeing REIT conversations begin with a dividend yield and end before anyone asks how that dividend is actually earned. That shortcut can turn three very different investments into one label.

The first-order view is that every REIT is simply a real estate investment with an income stream. The second-order view starts with the underlying engine: ownership and rent, mortgage financing and an interest-rate spread, or a blend of the two. The engine determines the sensitivity to occupancy, rates, leverage, volatility, and dividend cuts.

REITs come in three main types, each generating income differently. Equity REITs own property and earn rents. Mortgage REITs, or mREITs, finance real estate and earn an interest-rate spread. Hybrid REITs combine both. Equity REITs tend to be more stable; mortgage REITs can offer higher yields but have greater interest-rate sensitivity and risk. REIT investments carry risk, yields are not guaranteed, past performance doesn't guarantee future results, and this is educational information rather than investment or tax advice. Suitability should be verified with an investor’s professionals.

For the broader framework, including private and non-traded structures, see [our complete REIT investor guide](reits-guide.html).

## Equity REITs

Equity REITs are the most common type. They own and operate income-producing real estate: offices, apartments, retail centers, industrial buildings, hotels, data centers, and other property. They acquire, develop, and manage those assets, lease them to tenants, and collect rent. After expenses, net rental income funds dividends, and property appreciation can contribute to total return.

That makes an equity REIT a publicly investable real estate owner-operator. Its performance is driven by real estate fundamentals: occupancy, rent growth, property values, and the economic health of its sectors and markets. An equity REIT’s income and value therefore reflect the performance of the underlying property.

## Mortgage REITs (mREITs)

[Mortgage REITs (mREITs)](mortgage-reits-mreits-explained.html) take a different approach. They do not own property. They finance real estate by holding mortgages and mortgage-backed securities (MBS), and earn interest income. An mREIT commonly borrows at lower short-term rates, invests in higher-yielding mortgages or MBS, and earns the difference: the net interest margin, or spread.

An mREIT is closer to a specialized real estate lender than a property owner. Its profitability depends on the spread between its borrowing and lending rates, so rate changes and the shape of the yield curve can directly affect it. Rate volatility can be damaging. mREITs often use leverage to amplify the spread, which can amplify both return and risk.

This is why mREITs generally have higher dividend yields than equity REITs, but also more volatility and risk. A higher yield is not free income; it is compensation for a more rate-sensitive, leveraged business model.

> An equity REIT is a landlord. A mortgage REIT is a lender. Both invest in real estate, but their income mechanics and risks are not interchangeable.

## Hybrid REITs

Hybrid REITs combine the two approaches. They own some property, like an equity REIT, and hold some mortgages or MBS, like a mortgage REIT. Their income can therefore include both rent and interest.

The combination seeks to capture the relative stability and appreciation potential of property ownership alongside the higher yield of mortgage investments. Diversifying between property and financing exposure may smooth some volatility compared with a pure mortgage REIT while adding some yield. But it also introduces both sets of risks: real estate fundamentals on the equity side and interest-rate changes on the mortgage side. The actual risk profile depends on the mix.

## Risk & Yield by Type

The three types sit at different points on a risk-and-yield spectrum. Equity REITs are generally the most stable because they own real assets that produce rental income. They typically offer moderate, relatively stable yields plus appreciation potential. They still face real estate cycles, interest rates, and sector risk.

Mortgage REITs commonly offer the highest yields, but also the highest risk and volatility. Reliance on an interest-rate spread and leverage makes them sensitive to changing rates. Their dividends can be less stable and have been cut in stressed environments. Hybrid REITs fall between the two, depending on their equity-versus-mortgage balance.

The practical ordering is generally equity first for stability and moderate yield, hybrid in the middle, and mortgage last for higher yield with higher risk. An investor should match that tradeoff to risk tolerance rather than choose only by headline income.

### Key Takeaways

- Equity REITs own and operate property, earn rents, and are the most common type; occupancy, rent growth, and property values drive their relatively stable, moderate yields.
- Mortgage REITs finance real estate through mortgages and MBS, earn an interest-rate spread, and often use leverage; they generally offer higher yields with greater rate sensitivity and risk.
- Hybrid REITs blend property and mortgage exposure, combining rental and interest income while carrying both real estate and rate risk.
- Risk and yield generally rise from equity to hybrid to mortgage. The appropriate type depends on goals and risk tolerance.

## Choosing a REIT Type

The choice begins with goals, risk tolerance, and views about the underlying exposure. Investors seeking relatively stable income, appreciation potential, and ownership of real assets often use equity REITs as the core choice. They are the most common and generally more stable type for broad real estate exposure.

Mortgage REITs may appeal to someone seeking higher current yield who can tolerate greater risk and interest-rate sensitivity. They should not be treated as a stable-income substitute, because their dividends can be cut. Many investors use primarily equity REITs, diversified across property sectors, and treat mREITs as a smaller, higher-yield, higher-risk allocation if they use them at all. Hybrid REITs may suit someone seeking a blend.

The question is whether an investor prioritizes stability and appreciation or higher yield with higher risk. For many investors, equity REITs are the core. mREITs are a tactical, risk-tolerant addition rather than a default.

## REITs by Property Sector

The equity/mortgage/hybrid distinction is only the first decision. Equity REITs are also divided by property sector. Common sectors include residential—apartments and single-family rentals; retail—shopping centers and malls; office; industrial and logistics—warehouses and distribution facilities; healthcare—medical offices and senior housing; data centers; cell towers and communications; self-storage; [hospitality (hotels)](hotel-and-lodging-reits-explained.html); and specialty sectors such as [timber](timberland-reits-explained.html) and gaming.

Each sector has its own demand drivers, risks, and cycle. Industrial and data centers have benefited from e-commerce and digital growth. Some retail and office sectors have faced structural headwinds. Sector can shape an equity REIT’s prospects and risk as much as the broad equity-versus-mortgage classification. Diversifying across sectors, or using a diversified REIT fund, can reduce sector-specific risk, though it cannot eliminate it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main types of REITs?

There are three main types: equity REITs, mortgage REITs (mREITs), and hybrid REITs. Equity REITs own and operate income-producing property such as offices, apartments, retail, and industrial buildings, earning primarily from rent. They are the most common type.

mREITs finance real estate by holding mortgages and MBS and earn the spread between borrowing and lending rates. Hybrid REITs combine property ownership with mortgage holdings, blending rental and interest income. Equity REITs tend to be more stable; mREITs may offer higher yield with more rate sensitivity and risk; hybrids tend to fall between them.

### What is an equity REIT?

An equity REIT owns and operates income-producing real estate. It acquires, develops, and manages properties such as offices, apartments, retail, industrial assets, hotels, and data centers, leases them to tenants, and collects rent. After expenses, net rental income funds dividends, while property appreciation can contribute to total return.

Equity REITs represent the majority of the REIT market. Their results depend on occupancy, rent growth, property values, and the economic health of their sectors and markets. They are generally the more stable REIT type, with moderate, relatively stable yields and appreciation potential.

### What is a mortgage REIT (mREIT)?

An mREIT finances real estate rather than owning it. It holds mortgages and mortgage-backed securities and earns interest. It commonly borrows at lower short-term rates and invests in higher-yielding mortgages or MBS, capturing the difference as a net interest margin or spread.

Because the business depends on borrowing and lending rates, mREITs are very interest-rate-sensitive. Changes in the yield curve and rate volatility can affect profitability, portfolio value, and dividends. Leverage can increase the spread and current yield, but it also magnifies risk. Higher yield comes with higher volatility and potential dividend cuts.

### What is a hybrid REIT?

A hybrid REIT owns some property and holds some mortgages or mortgage-backed securities. It earns both rent and interest. The combination aims to blend the relative stability and appreciation potential of equity REITs with the higher yield of mortgage investments.

The blend can potentially smooth some pure-mREIT volatility while adding yield, but it also exposes the investor to property fundamentals and interest-rate changes. Its position on the risk-and-yield spectrum depends on its equity-versus-mortgage mix.

### Which REIT type is the safest?

Equity REITs are generally viewed as the more stable type, while mREITs are generally riskier and more volatile. Equity REITs own real assets producing rental income and typically offer moderate, relatively stable yields plus appreciation potential. They still face property-cycle, sector, and interest-rate risks.

mREITs rely on an interest-rate spread and often leverage, which makes them rate-sensitive and can make dividends less stable. Hybrid REITs fall between them. No REIT is risk-free: all can face market, real estate, and interest-rate risk. If stability is the priority, diversified equity REIT exposure is generally the better fit.

### Why do mortgage REITs have higher yields?

mREITs earn an interest-rate spread and often use leverage. Borrowing at a lower short-term rate and investing in higher-yielding mortgages or MBS can generate more current income and support a higher dividend.

The cost is higher risk. Rate changes can compress the spread, leverage magnifies losses as well as returns, and dividends can be cut when rates move adversely or the spread narrows. A very high mREIT yield can signal elevated risk, not just opportunity. The yield should be weighed against volatility and the possibility of a cut.

### How do I choose between equity and mortgage REITs?

Match the type to goals and risk tolerance. Equity REITs may fit an investor seeking relatively stable income and appreciation potential from real assets. They are the more common, more stable starting point for most investors seeking REIT exposure.

mREITs may suit someone seeking higher current yield who can tolerate greater rate sensitivity, volatility, and a dividend that may be cut. Many investors use a sector-diversified equity-REIT core and, if suitable, a smaller mortgage-REIT allocation for yield. Hybrid REITs are a possible blend. The choice is stability and appreciation versus higher yield with higher risk.

### Are REITs further divided by property sector?

Yes. Equity REITs are further divided into residential, retail, office, industrial and logistics, healthcare, data centers, cell towers and communications, self-storage, hospitality, and specialty sectors such as timber and gaming. Residential can include apartments and single-family rentals; retail can include shopping centers and malls; healthcare can include medical offices and senior housing; and industrial and logistics can include warehouses and distribution facilities.

Each sector has separate demand drivers, risks, and cycles. Industrial and data centers have benefited from e-commerce and digital growth, while parts of retail and office have faced structural headwinds. Sector diversification can reduce sector-specific risk. For equity REITs, sector can matter as much as the equity/mortgage distinction.

### Can I invest in all three types of REITs?

Yes. Investors can buy equity, mortgage, and hybrid REITs individually or through diversified REIT mutual funds and ETFs. A fund can provide exposure across types and sectors in a single investment.

Many investors weight a REIT allocation toward equity REITs for relatively stable income and appreciation, diversified across sectors. They may add a smaller amount of mortgage-REIT exposure for higher yield if its risk is acceptable, and possibly hybrids. A mix should reflect goals and risk tolerance, with attention to the higher risk of mREITs.

### Are mortgage REITs more sensitive to interest rates?

Yes. An mREIT’s income depends directly on the spread between borrowing and lending rates. As rates change or the yield curve shifts, the spread can compress or widen, affecting profitability and dividends. Rising short-term rates can increase borrowing costs, and rate volatility can reduce the value of mortgage holdings. Leverage amplifies that sensitivity.

Equity REITs are also affected by rates because higher rates can pressure property values and raise borrowing costs. The impact is less direct than for mREITs, whose business model is the rate spread. mREIT performance and dividends can therefore be more volatile in changing-rate environments.

### Do all REIT types follow the 90% distribution rule?

Yes. Equity, mortgage, and hybrid REITs must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income annually to maintain REIT status and avoid corporate-level tax. The rule applies whether income comes from rents, interest spread, or both.

That is why all REIT types tend to be high-dividend payers. The type changes how the income is earned, not the requirement to distribute most taxable income. mREITs can have higher yields than equity REITs, but their higher income does not change the **90%** rule.

### Which REIT type is best for income?

It depends on risk tolerance. mREITs often have the highest current yields, but that yield comes with higher interest-rate sensitivity, leverage, volatility, and less stable dividends. The distribution can be cut.

Equity REITs generally offer moderate, relatively stable yields and appreciation potential, which can make them a more dependable, though lower-yielding, income source. Many income investors use quality equity REITs in steady sectors as a core and treat mREITs as a limited addition, if at all. Sustainability matters more than the highest yield.

### Do REIT shares qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No. REIT shares—equity, mortgage, or hybrid; traded or non-traded—are securities and personal property, not like-kind real property. Since **2017**, 1031 treatment is limited to real property, so an investor cannot directly exchange relinquished real estate into REIT shares.

Buying REIT shares is a taxable purchase, not a direct 1031 exchange. There is an indirect route: a 1031 exchange into a DST, followed by a **721**/UPREIT exchange that can convert DST property into REIT operating-partnership units while maintaining deferral. That route does not make ordinary REIT shares directly 1031-eligible.

### Are any REIT types guaranteed or risk-free?

No. All REIT types carry risk, including loss of principal, dividend cuts, and price declines. Equity REITs face occupancy, rent, property-value, sector, economic-cycle, and interest-rate risk. mREITs have pronounced interest-rate and leverage risk and more volatile dividends. Hybrids carry both sets of exposure.

Yields and returns are not guaranteed, and past performance doesn't guarantee future results. Diversification across types, sectors, and the broader portfolio, along with attention to quality and sustainability, can help manage risk but cannot eliminate it. No REIT provides guaranteed income or principal protection.

## Glossary

- **Equity REIT:** A REIT that owns and operates property, earning rents.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT that finances real estate through mortgages and MBS.
- **Hybrid REIT:** A REIT combining property ownership and mortgage holdings.
- **Rental Income:** Income an equity REIT earns from leasing property.
- **Net Interest Margin:** The spread an mREIT earns between lending and borrowing rates.
- **Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS):** Securities backed by mortgages, held by mREITs.
- **Interest-Rate Sensitivity:** How much rate changes affect a REIT; it is high for mREITs.
- **Leverage:** Borrowing to amplify returns and risk, common in mREITs.
- **Property Sector:** The category of property an equity REIT owns.
- **Diversification:** Spreading across types and sectors to reduce risk.
- **Occupancy:** The leased percentage of an equity REIT’s property.
- **Real Estate Fundamentals:** Occupancy, rents, and values driving equity REITs.
- **Yield Curve:** The rate structure affecting mREIT spreads.
- **Dividend Stability:** Reliability of distributions, generally lower for mREITs.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** All REIT types must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income.
- **Total Return:** Income plus appreciation across REIT types.
