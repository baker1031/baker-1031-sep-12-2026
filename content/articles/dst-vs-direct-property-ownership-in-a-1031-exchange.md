# DST vs. Direct Property Ownership in a 1031 Exchange


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I spent part of this week looking at two replacement-property decisions that appeared identical on a closing calendar. Both could defer the same gain. One would make the investor a landlord again; the other would make the investor a passive owner. That difference carries more weight than the calendar does.

First-order thinking treats a DST and direct ownership as interchangeable because both can meet a 1031 deadline. Second-order thinking asks who controls the asset, who takes the calls, what is concentrated, how liquid the capital remains, and what has already been given up for a fast close. This guide compares active and passive ownership, management burden, diversification and minimums, control and liquidity, closing speed, and fit.

A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) and directly owned real estate can both serve as 1031 replacement property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. Both can defer capital-gains tax. Everything else differs: active or passive ownership, management, diversification, minimum capital, control, liquidity, and timing. DST interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after a suitability review. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice; verify current rules with your advisor.

## Active vs. Passive Replacement Property

With direct ownership, an investor buys a specific rental, commercial building, or net-lease asset and remains the owner-operator. The owner signs leases, hires and oversees management, makes decisions, and carries day-to-day responsibility. A 1031 exchange defers tax; it does not retire the investor from landlording.

With a DST, an investor buys a fractional beneficial interest in a trust that already owns income-producing real estate. A professional sponsor operates the asset through a master lease. The investor receives a pro-rata share of net rental income but makes no operating decisions and has no landlord duties. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats the beneficial interest as like-kind real property for 1031 purposes, so the same tax deferral can accompany a fully passive position.

## Management Burden Compared

Direct owners remain responsible, directly or through a manager they hire and oversee, for tenant sourcing and screening, leases and renewals, rent collection, repairs and capital expenditures, taxes, insurance, compliance, and problems as they arise. Even with a property manager, the owner remains the decision-maker. The buck stops with the owner.

A DST removes that operating burden. The sponsor handles leasing, management, maintenance, and operations through a master lease. The investor receives a share of current cash flow without a tenant call, roof-repair approval, or lease signature. This structure also helps the trust comply with Revenue Ruling 2004-86: the ruling restricts certain actions, the so-called seven deadly sins, including negotiating new leases, so the master lease handles operations. Direct ownership is hands-on; a DST is hands-off.

The difference is clearest on a Saturday night when a tenant reports a burst pipe. In a directly owned building, the call is yours. In a DST, it does not reach you.

## Diversification & Minimums

Direct ownership typically puts exchange proceeds into one building in one market, concentrating capital in one asset, tenant base, and local economy. Buying several replacement properties to diversify usually requires substantial capital and multiple closings inside the strict 1031 timeframe.

DST minimums are often roughly $25,000 to $100,000, though terms vary by sponsor and offering. That can allow one exchange to be spread across several DSTs holding multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, medical office, and other property types in different markets. It can also provide fractional access to institutional-quality real estate that is out of reach for most individual direct buyers. The minimums make diversification across sponsors, sectors, and geographies practical, but diversification reduces rather than eliminates risk.

## Control and Liquidity Trade-Offs

Direct ownership offers control: the owner can refinance, renovate, re-tenant, sell, and adjust strategy as conditions change. The owner may sell subject to market conditions and can refinance to access equity. The owner captures all upside and bears all downside.

A DST gives up that control. The sponsor determines operation, financing, and sale timing. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 limits actions such as refinancing or renegotiating debt. There is little or no secondary market for an interest, so an investor generally remains invested until the sponsor sells at the end of the hold, commonly around five to seven years. There is no practical early exit or ability to tap equity through a refinance. Direct ownership provides a route to liquidity; a DST trades it for passivity.

## Key Takeaways

- Both a DST and a directly owned building qualify as 1031 replacement property and defer the same capital-gains tax — the difference is everything else.
- Direct ownership keeps an investor active, with full control and a liquidity path. A DST creates a passive position with no management burden, but no control or early exit.
- DSTs have accessible minimums, often roughly $25,000 to $100,000, and can diversify across sectors and markets; direct ownership tends to concentrate capital in one building.
- DST interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after a suitability review. Direct ownership has no such gate but carries hands-on responsibilities.

## Closing Speed and the 1031 Timeline

Speed is an underappreciated distinction inside the 1031 deadlines: 45 days to identify replacement property and 180 days to close. A direct purchase requires a buyer to find, negotiate, diligence, finance, and close a specific building. That process can be slow, competitive, uncertain, and vulnerable to a failed deal that jeopardizes the exchange.

A DST can often close in days because the property is already acquired and financing already arranged; the investor subscribes for a fractional interest. Investors may identify one or more DSTs alongside a direct purchase so a DST can close quickly if the direct deal collapses. The trust's pre-arranged non-recourse debt can satisfy a debt-replacement requirement without personal loan qualification. A DST can therefore provide speed and closing certainty that a direct purchase may not match.

When the 45-day clock is running and a direct deal wobbles, a pre-packaged DST that can close in days may separate a completed exchange from the tax the investor meant to defer.

## Which Fits Your Goals

Direct ownership tends to fit someone who wants control, accepts or enjoys active management, wants to refinance or sell on a personal timetable, and will concentrate capital in a chosen building for its specific upside. It is for investors who value involvement more than passivity or instant diversification.

A DST tends to fit an [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html) ready to leave active landlording, trade control for professionally managed income, diversify across institutional assets at accessible minimums, and close quickly for a 1031 deadline without requiring liquidity during the hold. It can suit aging investors simplifying an estate, people relocating, and investors who want income without the work. Many investors blend the two: a direct property for control plus DSTs for diversification, a fast backup, or to place leftover exchange proceeds and avoid taxable boot. The decision is an appetite for control versus passivity, concentration versus diversification, and timing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a DST be used as 1031 replacement property?

Yes. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a beneficial interest in a properly structured DST is a direct interest in like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. An investor can sell appreciated investment real estate and reinvest in a DST to defer capital-gains tax, just as with a directly owned building. The investor becomes a passive fractional owner rather than an owner-operator. DST interests are securities offered by a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review. Confirm eligibility and timing with a tax advisor and qualified intermediary.

### What is the main difference between a DST and direct ownership?

The difference is active versus passive ownership. A direct owner buys a specific building, signs leases, controls decisions, and carries landlord responsibility even with a property manager. A DST investor owns a fractional interest; the sponsor operates the real estate through a master lease and the investor receives a share of income without decisions or management burden. Both can defer the same gain in a 1031 exchange, but they differ in management, diversification, minimums, control, and liquidity.

### Is a DST more passive than owning property directly?

Yes. A DST sponsor handles leasing, tenants, rent collection, maintenance, repairs, taxes, insurance, and compliance through the master lease. The investor receives distributions from current cash flow and has no operating responsibilities. The master lease supports compliance with Revenue Ruling 2004-86's restrictions, including no new-lease negotiation by the trust. A direct owner remains responsible even with a manager. A DST is therefore far more passive for someone seeking real-estate income without landlording.

### What are typical DST minimum investments?

DST minimums are commonly roughly $25,000 to $100,000, although each sponsor and offering sets its own terms. That can let one exchange fund several trusts across property types and markets instead of one building. It also supplies fractional access to institutional-quality multifamily, industrial, and net-lease assets. Review the specific offering documents for minimums, fees, and terms; DSTs are securities available to accredited investors after suitability review.

### Does a DST offer more diversification than direct ownership?

Generally, yes. Direct ownership normally concentrates exchange proceeds in one building, one tenant base, and one local economy, while several direct closings require large capital and coordination within a 1031 timeline. DST minimums can support allocations among several sponsors, sectors, including multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail and medical office, and geographies. That is practical diversification, not a guarantee: every DST still has property, tenant, illiquidity, sponsor, and market risks.

### Can I control a DST property the way I control my own building?

No. A DST investor does not control operations, financing, leasing, or sale timing; the sponsor does. The trust is also limited by Revenue Ruling 2004-86: it cannot raise new capital, refinance or renegotiate debt, negotiate new leases, or reinvest sale proceeds — the seven deadly sins. The master lease handles operations. A direct owner can refinance, renovate, re-tenant, sell, and change strategy. Loss of control is the cost of a DST's passivity.

### Are DSTs liquid?

No. A DST interest generally has little or no secondary market and cannot be sold readily. The investor waits for the sponsor's sale, commonly around a five-to-seven-year hold, which can be shorter or longer. The investor cannot refinance to pull equity because the ruling restricts trust refinancing. A direct owner can sell subject to market conditions or refinance. A DST should be funded only with capital that can remain committed for the full hold; confirm the expected hold and plan cash-flow needs accordingly.

### How quickly can a DST close compared to a direct purchase?

A DST often closes in days because the property and financing are already in place and the investor is subscribing for an interest. A direct acquisition requires selection, negotiation, due diligence, financing, and closing, which can take weeks or months and can fail. This makes a DST useful under the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines. Investors often identify DSTs as a backup to a direct acquisition; if the direct deal fails, the DST can close quickly. Its pre-arranged non-recourse debt can meet debt replacement without personal qualification. Confirm timing with a qualified intermediary.

### Which is better for an investor tired of being a landlord?

For someone who wants out of active landlording, a DST is often the better fit. The sponsor handles leases, tenants, maintenance, repairs, taxes, insurance, and compliance, while the investor receives income without decisions. This can let a long-time landlord defer capital-gains tax through a 1031 exchange while stepping away from daily work, and may fit aging investors simplifying life or estates and people relocating. Direct ownership keeps the investor in the operator role. Weigh illiquidity, lack of control, and fees, and complete a suitability review.

### Do both options defer the same capital-gains tax?

Yes, when used properly as replacement property in a 1031 exchange. Direct real estate qualifies. A properly structured DST qualifies because Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats its beneficial interest as real property. Both require a qualified intermediary, the 45- and 180-day deadlines, and replacement of equity and debt where required. The tax outcome does not favor either structure; ownership experience does. Confirm technical eligibility, deadlines, and debt replacement with your tax advisor and qualified intermediary.

### Can I invest in both a DST and a direct property in one exchange?

Yes. Investors commonly allocate part of exchange proceeds to a direct property and part to one or more DSTs. That can combine direct control with passive, diversified income. DSTs can absorb leftover proceeds and avoid taxable boot, or provide a fast close when a direct purchase falls through. Identify the direct property and each DST within the 45-day window under the three-property or 200% rules, then close all properties within 180 days. Coordinate carefully with a qualified intermediary and tax advisor.

### What are the risks of choosing a DST over direct ownership?

A DST brings illiquidity, loss of control over operations, financing, and sale, sponsor risk, an upfront load and ongoing fees, property/tenant/market concentration, interest-rate and refinancing risk for leveraged assets, and limited flexibility because of the no-refinance rule. Distributions and returns are projections, not guarantees. Direct ownership keeps control and a liquidity path but has concentration, management burden, and personal-loan liability. Neither path is risk-free; they carry different risk profiles. Review offering risk factors and suitability.

### Are DSTs only for accredited investors?

Generally yes. DST interests are private securities, typically offered under Regulation D, often Rule 506(c), to accredited investors. An individual may qualify through income above $200,000, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse, in each of the prior two years with an expectation of the same, or through net worth above $1 million excluding the primary residence, among other categories. The broker-dealer confirms accredited status and conducts a suitability review of financial situation, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. Direct ownership has no accreditation gate. Confirm status and the particular offering's requirements with the broker-dealer.

### How do I decide between a DST and direct ownership?

Choose direct ownership if control, active management, refinancing and sale on your own timetable, and the upside of a specific building outweigh a need for passivity or instant diversification. Choose a DST if you want to leave landlording, value professionally managed income and diversification at lower minimums, need to close quickly in a 1031 exchange, and can live without liquidity through the hold. A blend can fit both goals. Consider control versus passivity, concentration versus diversification, timeline, accredited status, and suitability with your advisor, CPA, and broker-dealer.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)** — A trust holding income-producing real estate in which investors own fractional interests.

**Beneficial Interest** — A DST investor's fractional, 1031-eligible ownership stake.

**1031 Replacement Property** — The like-kind real estate acquired to complete a 1031 exchange.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86** — The IRS ruling treating a DST interest as like-kind real property.

**Direct Ownership** — Owning and operating a specific building yourself.

**Passive Investment** — Owning real estate income without managing it.

**Master Lease** — The structure through which a DST sponsor operates the property.

**Management Burden** — The ongoing work of operating real estate (heavy for direct owners).

**Diversification** — Spreading exchange proceeds across multiple DSTs or assets.

**Minimum Investment** — A DST's entry amount (often roughly $25,000 to $100,000).

**Illiquidity** — The inability to readily sell a DST interest before the hold ends.

**Hold Period** — A DST's multi-year term (commonly around five to seven years).

**Non-Recourse Debt** — DST financing with no personal loan liability for investors.

**Backup Identification** — Naming a DST as a fast-closing fallback to protect a 1031 deadline.

**Accredited Investor** — An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DST offerings.

**Suitability Review** — Assessing whether a DST fits the investor before investing.

## DST vs. direct ownership at a glance

Two ways to complete a 1031 exchange — a passive DST interest or buying replacement property outright — compared:

| Factor | DST | Direct Ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Fractional beneficial interest | Full ownership of a specific building |
| Management | Passive; sponsor uses master lease | Active; owner manages or oversees manager |
| Diversification | Low minimums can support several assets | Usually one building per exchange |
| Control | Sponsor controls operations, financing, and sale | Owner controls strategy and timing |
| Liquidity | Little or no secondary market; sponsor controls exit | Owner can sell or refinance, subject to market |
| Capital | Often roughly $25,000 to $100,000 per DST ($25–$100k in short form) | Substantial equity, financing, and reserves |
| Closing speed | Often days, with financing already in place | Weeks or months, with diligence and contingencies |
| 1031 treatment | Like-kind property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 | Direct like-kind real property |

## Which should you choose?

Choose a DST when you want to retire from active management, diversify proceeds across several assets, need a fast close for exchange deadlines, or need to place leftover equity and avoid boot.

Choose direct ownership when control, leverage, and actively adding value matter more than passivity and you have the time and capacity to manage a property. Many investors use a primary direct purchase plus a DST for the remainder.
