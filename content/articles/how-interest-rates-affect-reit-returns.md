# How Interest Rates Affect REIT Returns

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html), [REITs](insights.html)
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing REITs treated as a levered bond whenever rates move. That first reaction is understandable. It is also incomplete.

Interest rates are among the largest inputs in REIT pricing. They affect valuations, cap rates, financing costs, and the appeal of REIT income relative to bonds. But rents can grow, balance sheets differ, and sectors do not reset revenue at the same speed. This guide explains why rates move REIT valuations, how cap rates respond, which sectors are most rate-sensitive or resilient, what a high-rate setting can mean, and how to think about positioning across the cycle.

First-order thinking says “rates up, REITs down.” Second-order thinking asks how much of the rate move is already priced in, whether income can reset, when debt matures, and what the broader economy is doing. Market conditions change constantly. ## Why Rates Move REIT Prices

Rates reach REIT prices through three channels that often point in the same direction. First is the discount-rate channel. A REIT is valued from expected future cash flows; when the rate used to discount those cash flows rises, their present value falls. Income-oriented REITs can have long-duration cash flows, so this is similar to the way a long-lived stream of bond income becomes less valuable when rates rise.

Second is borrowing cost. REITs commonly use leverage to acquire and develop property. Higher rates make new debt and floating-rate debt more expensive, raising expense and reducing cash available for distributions and growth.

Third is competition from bond yields. Investors often own REITs for income. When safer bonds offer more income, investors have a lower-risk alternative, and REIT dividend yields may need to rise to remain competitive. Since yield and price move in opposite directions, that can mean a lower REIT price. These effects can compound when rates climb.

The useful framework is simple: higher discount rates reduce the present value of future income; higher borrowing costs reduce the cash the REIT keeps; higher bond yields compete for income capital. That is why REITs are often rate-sensitive, though the degree of sensitivity is not uniform.

## Cap Rates and Valuations

The connection between rates and property value runs mainly through capitalization rates, or cap rates. A cap rate is net operating income divided by property value: the yield a buyer requires on the purchase price. When the cost of capital rises, buyers usually demand a higher yield. Cap rates therefore often rise as interest rates rise.

Value is roughly income divided by the cap rate. Hold income constant and raise the cap rate, and property value falls. This is how rate moves can reach the value of the real estate owned by a REIT.

The relationship is not mechanical or immediate. Rent-growth expectations, supply and demand for a property type, and investor appetite all matter. Strong rent growth can partly or fully offset cap-rate expansion; weak growth paired with higher rates can make the valuation effect harsher. Periodically appraised NAVs for non-traded REITs can also lag market cap-rate changes.

> Value is roughly income divided by the cap rate. When rising rates lift the cap rate, the same rent supports a lower property value unless income grows fast enough to keep up.

## Rate-Sensitive vs. Resilient Sectors

Sector characteristics matter. Long-duration, slow-growth income tends to act more like a bond. Net-lease REITs with long fixed leases and many healthcare REITs fit that description: steady income, limited rent growth, and a higher discount rate with little offset. Mortgage REITs are usually the most rate-sensitive because they earn an interest-rate spread and use significant leverage; rate changes can sharply affect book value and earnings.

The more resilient side tends to have pricing power, short leases, or structural growth. Residential REITs can reset annual leases, hotels can reprice rooms nightly, and self-storage can adjust rates frequently. Those features can let income keep pace with rates and [inflation](reits-and-inflation-hedge-or-headwind.html). Some industrial and data-center properties have also benefited from secular demand that supports rent growth even as rates rise.

The decisive question is not simply whether an issuer is a REIT. It is whether its income can grow quickly enough to offset the rate headwind. Our [sector-by-sector playbook for rising rates](reits-and-rising-rates-a-sector-playbook.html) explores that distinction further.

## REITs in a High-Rate Environment

In a sustained high-rate period, pressures arrive together. Transactions can slow while buyers and sellers disagree on value during cap-rate resets. Refinancing costs more, and interest expense rises as low-rate debt matures. Public REIT share prices may fall before appraised property values move because markets quickly reprice the discount-rate and bond-competition channels. The result can be a gap between the share price and appraised property value.

Still, a high-rate period is not uniformly bad. Low leverage, staggered maturities, fixed-rate debt, and pricing power can give a REIT time to keep growing rents. Higher rates can also slow construction, tighten supply, and support rents at existing properties. REIT cash flow can grow, unlike a bond coupon, so higher rates in an economy with solid growth and rent gains can look quite different from higher rates in a weak economy.

The surface story is pressure on prices and slower deals. The second-order story is balance-sheet strength, pricing power, future supply, and the economy. Conditions vary; verify the current setting rather than assuming a fixed outcome.

## Key Takeaways

- Rising rates generally pressure REIT prices through higher discount rates, higher borrowing costs, and competing bond yields.
- Cap rates tend to rise with interest rates, lowering property value for a given income, although rent growth can offset the effect.
- Mortgage REITs and long-duration, slow-growth sectors such as net-lease and healthcare are usually most rate-sensitive; short-lease, pricing-power sectors tend to be more resilient.
- REITs are not a simple bond proxy. Rent growth and fundamentals matter, and diversification across sectors and the cycle can help manage rate risk.

## Duration, Leverage, and Lease Length

Two features explain much of the difference between one REIT and another: the duration of its cash flow and the balance sheet. Duration here means how long and how fixed the income stream is. A net-lease REIT with **12-year** leases and small annual escalators has long, bond-like duration, so changes in discount rates carry more weight. A residential REIT that re-leases annually has short effective duration because it can reprice income more quickly.

Leverage matters just as much. High leverage, floating-rate debt, and near-term maturities bring rate pressure into current expense and expensive refinancing. Modest leverage, largely fixed-rate debt, and well-laddered maturities lock in more of the interest cost and give rents time to grow.

Lease length connects the two. Short leases can allow rents to rise with costs; long fixed leases lock in income that may not keep up. These elements—not the REIT label alone—set rate sensitivity.

> Two REITs can face the same rate move and react very differently: one with long fixed leases and floating-rate debt feels it quickly, while one with annual leases and laddered fixed-rate debt has room to adapt.

## Positioning Across the Cycle

Rates move in cycles, and sector and balance-sheet exposure vary. A common approach is to position across the cycle rather than attempt to time every change. Blending rate-sensitive income sectors such as net-lease and healthcare with shorter-lease, pricing-power exposure such as residential, self-storage, and some industrial can spread rate risk. Low leverage, fixed-rate debt, and laddered maturities are another way to reduce vulnerability without forecasting rates.

Rates also fall. The same [commercial real estate cycles](commercial-real-estate-cycles-and-reit-performance.html) that create pressure when rates rise can offer support when they ease: lower discount rates can lift values, cheaper debt can reduce expenses, and lower bond yields can improve the relative appeal of REIT income. A multi-year allocation is exposed to both phases.

This is a risk-management framework, not a prediction. Long-term investors may hold through rate moves, but the appropriate positioning depends on goals, horizon, and risk tolerance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do interest rates affect REITs?

Higher rates usually pressure REIT prices through three reinforcing channels: future cash flows are discounted more heavily, new and floating-rate debt costs more, and higher bond yields compete with REIT dividends. Rents can grow, however, so the magnitude depends on sector, balance sheet, and the economy.

### Why do rising interest rates lower REIT prices?

They lower the present value of future income, raise borrowing costs, and can require a higher REIT dividend yield to compete with bonds, which means a lower price. Long-duration income makes the discount-rate effect particularly important. The precise impact varies by sector and debt structure.

### What is a cap rate and how does it relate to interest rates?

A cap rate is net operating income divided by property value. A building producing **$1 million** of NOI and selling for **$20 million** has a **5%** cap rate. As capital costs rise, buyers typically require higher yields, so cap rates often rise. Since value is roughly income divided by cap rate, the same income supports a lower value. Rent growth, supply and demand, and investor appetite can alter the result.

### Are REITs a good investment when interest rates rise?

There is no blanket answer and no forecast here. Rising rates create headwinds, but short-lease, pricing-power sectors can reset rent, constrained construction can support existing rents, and low-leverage fixed-rate balance sheets can be more resilient. Long-duration net-lease and mortgage REITs can struggle more. Diversify, focus on balance-sheet strength, and verify current conditions. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Which REIT sectors are most sensitive to interest rates?

Long-duration, slow-growth income tends to be most sensitive: net-lease REITs, many healthcare REITs, and especially mortgage REITs. Residential, hotels, and self-storage can reset income more frequently; some industrial and data-center exposure has had secular-demand support. Sensitivity turns on income growth relative to the rate headwind.

### Why are mortgage REITs so sensitive to interest rates?

Mortgage REITs finance real estate through mortgages and mortgage-backed securities rather than owning property directly. They earn the spread between asset income and borrowing cost, usually with significant leverage. Short-term rate increases can raise funding costs, long-term rate changes can affect mortgage-asset values, and yield-curve moves, prepayments, and hedges add complexity. They can offer higher yields, but they carry greater rate risk than equity REITs.

### How does the discount rate affect REIT valuations?

The discount rate converts expected future cash flow to present value. A higher rate makes those future distributions worth less today. Because REIT income can be relatively stable and long-lived, it can have substantial duration, much like a long-term bond. Rent growth can partly offset the effect, especially for shorter-duration or higher-growth cash flows.

### Do REITs behave like bonds when rates change?

They share an income and duration component with bonds, so both may react to rates. But a REIT is not a bond proxy: rents can grow, and occupancy, property fundamentals, and the economy create equity-like risks and rewards. Expect some bond-like response, but do not assume the same movement as fixed income.

### What happens to REITs in a high interest rate environment?

Transactions may slow, refinancing becomes more expensive, low-rate debt rolls over at higher cost, and traded REIT prices can move ahead of appraised values. Low leverage, fixed-rate and laddered debt, pricing power, and construction restraint can cushion the effect. Sector and the broader economy remain important.

### Can REITs still do well when rates are high?

Some can, though nothing is guaranteed. Pricing power and short leases can support rent resets; low leverage, fixed-rate debt, and staggered maturities can protect interest expense; and structural demand in some industrial and data-center sectors can support rent. Higher rates can curb new construction. The advantage shifts toward quality and resilience, not a universal outcome.

### How does leverage affect a REIT's interest-rate risk?

Leverage amplifies both return and risk. Floating-rate debt resets quickly and near-term maturities may need refinancing at higher cost, reducing cash for distributions and growth. Modest leverage, mostly fixed-rate debt, and laddered maturities reduce that immediate exposure. Two REITs in one sector can react very differently because their debt structures differ.

### What is duration in the context of REITs?

Duration describes how long and fixed a REIT’s income is, and therefore how rate-sensitive its value can be. Long fixed leases with small escalators are bond-like; annual residential leases and nightly hotel pricing reset more quickly. The longer and more fixed the income, the more a REIT can behave like a long bond when rates move.

### How should I position a REIT allocation for interest-rate risk?

Rather than precisely time rates, diversify sectors: balance rate-sensitive net-lease and healthcare exposure with shorter-lease, pricing-power sectors such as residential, self-storage, and some industrial. Favor low leverage, fixed-rate debt, and laddered maturities. Size the allocation for your goals, horizon, and risk tolerance, and verify current conditions instead of reacting to every rate move.

### Do falling interest rates help REITs?

Generally, falling rates can support REIT valuations: the discount rate falls, debt and refinancing costs may decline, bond yields become less competitive, and cap rates may compress. But it is a tendency, not a guarantee. Rates can fall because of economic weakness, and falling rents or rising vacancy can offset the benefit. Ask why rates are falling and what property fundamentals are doing.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
- **Interest Rate:** The cost of borrowing money, set largely by the Federal Reserve and markets.
- **Discount Rate:** The rate used to convert future cash flows into present value.
- **Cap Rate:** Net operating income divided by property value — the yield a buyer demands.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** A property’s income after operating expenses, before debt and taxes.
- **Duration:** How long and fixed an income stream is, and thus its rate sensitivity.
- **Leverage:** The debt a REIT uses to finance property, amplifying rate exposure.
- **Floating-Rate Debt:** Debt whose interest cost rises and falls with market rates.
- **Fixed-Rate Debt:** Debt with a locked interest cost, insulating against rate moves.
- **Net-Lease REIT:** A REIT with long fixed leases — long-duration and rate-sensitive.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT earning an interest-rate spread — very rate-sensitive.
- **Pricing Power:** The ability to raise rents to offset higher rates and inflation.
- **Bond Proxy:** Treating REITs like bonds — misleading, since REIT cash flows grow.
- **Bond Yield:** The income a bond pays, which competes with REIT dividends.
- **Maturity Laddering:** Staggering debt maturities to reduce refinancing risk.
- **Across the Cycle:** Positioning for both rising and falling rate phases.
