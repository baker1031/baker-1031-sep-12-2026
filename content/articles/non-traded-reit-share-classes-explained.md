# Non-Traded REIT Share Classes Explained

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing the letter on a non-traded REIT share class treated as a minor label. It is usually a cost decision. Non-traded REITs commonly offer Class A, T, S, D, and I shares, which differ in upfront sales loads and ongoing fees. This guide explains why the classes exist, who can access them, and how to compare their total cost over the hold.

First-order thinking sees several ways to buy the same portfolio. Second-order thinking asks how the selling channel is paid, how much capital is actually deployed, and what recurring charges will subtract over years. The underlying real estate can be identical while the net return is not.

## Key Takeaways

- Non-traded REITs offer multiple share classes—A, T, S, D, and I—of the same underlying real estate, with different upfront loads and ongoing fees.
- Load classes (A, T, and S) carry upfront sales charges and/or trailing distribution fees; no-load or low-load classes (D and I) generally carry little or none and lower ongoing fees.
- Ongoing distribution fees compound over a multi-year hold and can erode return as much as an upfront load. Total cost matters more than the headline load.
- Eligibility depends on the advisor relationship, account type, and investment minimum. Compare only the classes actually available to you.

When offering documents list several share classes, each still represents ownership in the same real-estate portfolio and draws distributions from the same income. The difference is the cost wrapper: upfront sales loads, ongoing distribution and servicing fees, or both. The class available to an investor depends on the advisor relationship, account type, and minimum investment. Those differences can compound over a multi-year hold and affect net return even though the assets are the same.

## Why Multiple Share Classes Exist

Multiple classes let one real-estate portfolio be sold through commission-based brokerage relationships, fee-based advisory accounts, and institutional or larger-minimum channels. Those channels compensate the advisor or selling party differently, so a sponsor can issue several classes rather than create separate REITs.

Class A, T, and S shares are commonly offered through retail or commission-based channels. They can carry an upfront sales load, ongoing distribution and servicing fees, or both, compensating the selling broker. Class D and I are commonly aimed at fee-based advisory accounts, institutions, or investors meeting higher minimums. They generally have no or lower upfront load and lower ongoing fees because an advisor may be compensated separately through an account-level advisory fee. The real estate is the same; the channel and cost structure differ.

## Load vs. No-Load Classes

A sales load is a one-time commission deducted when capital is invested. It reduces the amount that reaches the real estate. Class A has historically carried the highest upfront load, sometimes several percent. Class T and S may pair a smaller upfront load with ongoing fees that can produce a comparable total cost over time.

No-load or low-load classes, usually D and I, have no upfront sales charge or a reduced one. They are generally available through fee-based advisory accounts, institutional channels, or higher-minimum investments. More capital can go to work immediately and ongoing fees can be lower, but access depends on the account and relationship. Every dollar paid as an upfront load is a dollar that never reaches the properties; two investors in the same REIT can therefore have different net returns.

## Ongoing Distribution Fees

Beyond any load, certain classes charge annual distribution and servicing fees for as long as the shares are held, often subject to a cap. These trailing charges compensate the selling broker. T and S shares often pair a smaller upfront load with a recurring annual fee. A may have a larger upfront load with a lower or no ongoing fee; D and I generally have the lowest ongoing fees.

The important point is compounding. A fraction of a percent deducted every year can materially reduce cumulative return over a long hold. A higher-load, lower-trailing-fee class can be cheaper than a lower-load, higher-trailing-fee class if the holding period is long enough; the reverse can be true for a shorter hold. Do not judge the cost from the upfront number alone.

## Which Class You Qualify For

You do not simply choose the cheapest class. Eligibility generally turns on three things: the advisor relationship—commission-based brokerage or fee-based advisory—the account type, and the investment minimum. Advisory accounts may have access to lower-cost D or I shares; the lowest-cost classes can require larger minimums or be reserved for institutions and advisory platforms.

An investor using a commission-based broker may be offered A, T, or S, where broker compensation is embedded through loads and trailing fees. An investor with a fee-based advisor or a larger account may qualify for D or I, where the advisor is paid separately. That is not arbitrary; it reflects the compensation model. Confirm the available classes before comparing their costs.

## Reading the Prospectus and Fee Tables

The prospectus is the authoritative source for share-class costs. Its fee tables disclose, by class, upfront sales loads—often including selling commissions and dealer-manager fees—ongoing distribution and servicing fees, any caps, the offering price, redemption terms and early-redemption discounts, organizational and offering costs, and other sponsor fees.

Read the all-in picture, not just the headline load. Look at how charges accrue over the expected holding period, whether ongoing fees stop once a cumulative cap is reached, the per-class offering price, and any conversion or liquidity-event treatment. The prospectus also provides risk factors and [redemption program terms](non-traded-reit-redemption-programs-explained.html), both [essential context for an illiquid, long-horizon investment](non-traded-reit-liquidity-fees-and-risks.html).

The fee table is where the full cost is disclosed. Verify loads, ongoing fees, caps, prices, and exit terms there before investing.

## Comparing Total Cost

Compare total cost over the expected holding period instead of fixating on one fee. A class with a high upfront load and low ongoing fees can cost less than a low-load class with higher trailing fees over a long hold; a short hold can change that result. Because non-traded REITs are illiquid, long-horizon investments, recurring fees often deserve substantial weight.

Add the upfront load to cumulative distribution and servicing fees for the years you expect to own the class, account for any caps, and weigh that total against the offering price and terms. If the channel only makes A, T, and S available, compare those. If D or I is available, those lower-cost classes often have the better total cost over a long hold. The purpose is to see how much of gross return the fees will consume.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are non-traded REIT share classes?

They are versions of the same REIT—typically A, T, S, D, and I—that own the same portfolio and receive income from it, but have different upfront loads and ongoing distribution and servicing fees. The sponsor uses classes for commission-based brokerage, fee-based advisory, and institutional or larger-minimum channels. The real estate is identical, but the cost wrapper differs and can affect net return over time. Use the prospectus to see each class's terms.

### Why do non-traded REITs have multiple share classes?

They accommodate different distribution channels and advisor-compensation models. A sponsor can sell one portfolio through brokerage, advisory, and institutional channels by issuing classes tailored to each. A, T, and S commonly compensate selling brokers through loads and/or trailing fees; D and I usually have lower or no loads because the advisor is compensated separately.

### What is the difference between Class A, T, S, D, and I shares?

Terms vary by offering. A has historically had the highest upfront load and lower or no ongoing fee. T and S commonly have a smaller load plus ongoing distribution fees. D is often a lower-cost advisory-account class, and I is commonly the lowest-cost class for institutions, advisory platforms, or higher-minimum investors. Confirm actual loads, fees, caps, and minimums in the prospectus rather than inferring them from a class letter.

### What is a sales load?

It is an upfront commission deducted at investment to compensate the selling broker. It reduces deployed capital and therefore the starting base on which return can be earned. A has historically carried the highest load; T and S can combine a smaller load with ongoing fees; D and I generally have little or no upfront charge. Check the prospectus for the exact amount.

### What are ongoing distribution and servicing fees?

They are recurring annual charges on some classes, often capped, paid while shares are held and used to compensate the selling broker. They are central to T and S; A can have a lower or no ongoing fee, and D and I generally have the lowest charges. Their compounding means apparently similar classes can diverge substantially over a multi-year hold.

### Which share class should I choose?

Choose the lowest total-cost class over the expected holding period among the classes you qualify for. Eligibility depends on the advisor relationship, account type, and minimum. Add the upfront load and cumulative ongoing fees, account for caps, and compare available A/T/S versus D/I as applicable. There is no single best class for everyone.

### Which share class do I qualify for?

Qualification depends on the advisor relationship, account type, and investment minimum. Commission-based brokerage usually provides A, T, or S. Fee-based advisory accounts can provide D, where advice is paid through a separate fee. I is often reserved for institutions, advisory platforms, or higher-minimum investors. Confirm eligibility in offering materials or with the advisor.

### Do higher fees mean lower returns?

All else equal, yes. The classes own the same assets and receive the same portfolio income; costs are the structural difference. A load reduces deployed capital and ongoing fees are deducted each year. The goal is to minimize total cost among eligible classes, while recognizing the lowest-fee class may not be available.

### Where can I find a non-traded REIT's share-class fees?

In the prospectus fee tables. They disclose loads, dealer-manager fees, ongoing distribution and servicing fees and caps, offering prices, redemption terms, early-redemption discounts, organizational and offering costs, sponsor fees, risk factors, and redemption-program terms. Read the full table rather than relying on a summary.

### How do I compare the total cost of share classes?

Add the upfront load to cumulative ongoing fees across the planned holding period, account for fee caps, and compare the result with each eligible class's offering price and terms. A high-load, low-trailing-fee class can be cheaper after a long hold than a low-load, high-trailing-fee class, and vice versa for a short hold. Net return depends on the all-in cost.

### Are share-class fees the only cost of a non-traded REIT?

No. Organizational and offering costs, sponsor or advisor asset-management fees, potential incentive fees, property-level expenses, and possible early-redemption discounts also matter. Share-class fees pay for distribution and servicing; other fees pay for management of the REIT and its properties. Read the entire prospectus fee section.

### Do share classes affect liquidity or redemptions?

The basic redemption program generally applies across classes because they own the same real estate. A class can still affect exit economics through early-redemption discounts, fee caps, conversion provisions, or treatment at a liquidity event. Read the redemption section and fee tables together. Every class remains illiquid, so plan for a long hold.

### Can my share class change over time?

Sometimes. Once cumulative trailing fees on a commission-based T or S class reach a stated cap, shares may convert automatically to a lower-fee class such as I. Not every REIT does this, and the cap, trigger, and conversion class vary. Check the prospectus and incorporate any conversion mechanics into total-cost analysis.

## Glossary

### Share Class

A version of the same REIT differing in loads and ongoing fees.

### Class A Shares

A class historically carrying a higher upfront load, lower ongoing fees.

### Class T Shares

A class with a smaller upfront load plus ongoing distribution fees.

### Class S Shares

A commission-based class with a load and ongoing distribution fees.

### Class D Shares

A lower-cost class for fee-based advisory accounts.

### Class I Shares

The lowest-cost class, for institutions and larger-minimum investors.

### Sales Load

An upfront commission deducted when you invest, reducing deployed capital.

### No-Load Class

A share class with little or no upfront sales charge.

### Distribution & Servicing Fee

A recurring annual fee compensating the selling broker.

### Trailing Fee

An ongoing fee paid each year a class is held.

### Fee Cap

A limit on cumulative ongoing fees, after which they stop.

### Dealer-Manager Fee

A fee paid to the firm managing a non-traded offering.

### Prospectus

The offering document disclosing each class's loads and fees.

### Total Cost

Upfront load plus compounded ongoing fees over the hold.

### Fee-Based Advisory

An account where the advisor is paid a separate fee, not commissions.

### Suitability Review

Assessing whether an illiquid non-traded REIT fits the investor.
