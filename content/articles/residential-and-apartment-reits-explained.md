# Residential & Apartment REITs Explained

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing investors focus on the rent they could collect next quarter while giving much less attention to the apartment supply scheduled to arrive after that. Housing is familiar. That can make it easy to mistake familiarity for safety.

Residential and apartment REITs own rental housing: apartment communities, single-family rentals, manufactured-housing communities, and student or senior housing. Their income comes from residents' rent. The important question is not simply whether people need a place to live. It is how demand, supply, lease resets, regulation, financing, and price interact over a full cycle.

The first-order view says that apartment leases are short, renters need homes, and high home prices or mortgage rates can support rent. The second-order view asks what has already been priced in, how much new product will compete for those renters, whether rent control limits repricing, and what leverage does when rates or cash flow move against expectations. Sector performance varies. Past performance does not guarantee future results. This is educational information, not investment advice, and REIT suitability depends on your circumstances.

Residential REITs are one part of the wider REIT market. For context, read the [complete guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## What Residential REITs Own

Residential REITs own income-producing rental property. Apartment or multifamily REITs are the largest and most familiar group. Their portfolios can run from garden-style suburban communities to high-rise urban towers. They collect [income from residents' rent](income-reits-explained-investing-for-cash-flow.html), manage leasing, maintenance, and amenities, and seek to grow income through occupancy, rent increases, and operating efficiency.

The sector is broader than apartments. Single-family rental REITs own portfolios of detached rental homes. Manufactured-housing REITs generally own the land and common infrastructure in manufactured-home communities and lease lots to residents who own their homes. Student-housing REITs own purpose-built housing near universities. Senior-housing REITs own independent- and assisted-living housing and can overlap with healthcare. Each segment has its own renters, lease characteristics, and demand drivers, but all are businesses that earn rent from where people live.

## Apartment Demand Drivers

Population growth and household formation are foundational. When young adults move out, form households, or start families, more households need housing. Job growth matters too: employment brings residents to a market and gives them income to pay rent. Apartment demand is usually firmer where local economies and employment are growing.

Homeownership affordability is another important driver. High home prices and elevated mortgage rates can make buying less affordable and keep people renting for longer. That may support apartment demand, but it is not a free pass for the owner. Local employment, the price of competing apartments, and the supply pipeline still determine whether the REIT can turn demand into rent growth.

## Rent Growth & Occupancy

Rent growth and occupancy are the two operating measures to watch most closely. Occupancy is the share of units leased and generating rent. Vacancy directly reduces revenue. Rent growth is the change in rent on renewals and new leases.

Apartment leases typically run about a year. That lets the owner reset rents frequently. In a strong market, it can capture higher market rents relatively quickly and gives the sector some inflation responsiveness. It also means soft demand shows up quickly. If supply outpaces demand or the economy weakens, rents can stall or fall with the next renewal cycle.

This differs from a [net-lease property locked into a decade-long lease](net-lease-reits-explained.html). A residential REIT can capture a rising market sooner, but it can also feel a weakening market sooner. Rising rents with high occupancy usually signal a healthy rental market; flat rents and rising vacancy signal pressure.

## Key Takeaways
- Residential REITs own apartments, single-family rentals, manufactured housing, and student or senior housing.
- Apartment demand reflects population and household formation, job growth, and homeownership affordability. High home prices and rates can push people to rent.
- Short annual leases create frequent rent resets: potential pricing power in a strong market and quick exposure when demand softens.
- New-supply oversupply, rent regulation, and economic downturns can pressure occupancy and rents.

## Sector Risks

New supply is often the central risk. Developers can add enough apartments in a market to outpace demand, increasing vacancy and pressing on rents even when the broader housing backdrop looks sound. Popular Sun Belt metros have at times had construction waves that temporarily softened rent growth. A serious review looks at the future supply pipeline, not just last quarter's occupancy.

Regulation is another risk. Rent control or rent stabilization can limit increases and cap pricing power, particularly in some coastal markets. A downturn is the third major concern. Renters can double up, move home, downsize, or fall behind after job losses. Housing is relatively defensive because people need a place to live, but residential REITs are not immune to recession.

General REIT risks also apply: sensitivity to interest rates and leverage. A rate move can affect property values, share prices, debt expense, and refinancing at the same time. Defensive is a relative word, not a guarantee.

## Notable Residential REIT Types

Coastal apartment REITs often operate in established, supply-constrained coastal markets. Limited land and strict zoning can support rents because less new supply is added. The trade-off can be heavier regulation, including rent control or stabilization. Sun Belt apartment REITs often operate in faster-growing southern and western markets, where population and job growth can be strong but new construction can be easier and cheaper. The trade-off is higher oversupply risk when building accelerates.

Single-family rental REITs serve renters who want a detached house, yard, and suburban setting without buying. Manufactured-housing REITs typically receive lot rent from residents who own their homes; costly moves can make occupancy and tenure relatively steady. Student housing depends on university enrollment. Senior housing depends on demographic aging and overlaps with healthcare. These are different businesses, not interchangeable labels.

## How Investors Evaluate Residential REITs

Begin with same-store rent growth and occupancy. Same-store figures compare properties owned in both periods, helping separate organic performance from acquisitions. Then examine the supply pipeline in the REIT's markets, the geographic mix—coastal versus Sun Belt, for example—and the staying power of local job growth.

On the financial side, funds from operations (FFO) and adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) are cash-earnings measures. The payout ratio relative to AFFO helps frame distribution sustainability. Balance-sheet leverage shows exposure to interest rates and refinancing. Net asset value (NAV) is an estimate of the underlying property value. The terms are defined in the [REIT glossary](reit-glossary-of-terms.html).

Frequent rent resets can make the sector more responsive to inflation than long-lease property types. They can also make it more exposed to demand swings. That is why it is better to weigh the operating facts and financing facts together than to call the sector an inflation hedge or a defensive holding in the abstract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do residential REITs own?

Residential REITs own rental housing where residents pay rent. Apartment REITs own communities ranging from garden-style suburban complexes to urban high-rises. The wider sector also includes portfolios of detached single-family rentals, manufactured-home communities where the REIT commonly owns the land and leases lots, purpose-built student housing, and independent or assisted senior housing. Each has different demand and lease patterns, but all earn income from residential rent.

### What drives demand for apartment REITs?

Population growth, household formation, and job growth create the basic demand. Homeownership affordability matters as well: when home prices and mortgage rates are high, some would-be buyers rent for longer. Strong, growing markets with expensive for-sale housing can have firm apartment demand, but local conditions and incoming new supply still matter.

### Why do residential REITs have short leases?

Apartment leases are typically about a year, which allows frequent rent resets. The owner can raise rent more quickly when market demand is strong and inflation pushes up rents. The same feature exposes it quickly when demand weakens, the economy softens, or supply increases. Short leases are a double-edged feature, not a guarantee of inflation protection.

### What is the difference between rent growth and occupancy?

Occupancy is the share of units leased and producing income. If a portfolio is 96% occupied, 4% of its units are vacant and earning no rent. Rent growth is the change in rent on new and renewing leases. A REIT can have full buildings and flat rents, or rising rents and slipping occupancy. Same-store figures compare the same properties across periods and help isolate organic trends from acquisitions. Income needs both filled units and sustainable rent growth.

### What are the main risks of residential REITs?

New supply can outpace demand and pressure rents and occupancy, including in fast-growing Sun Belt metros. Rent control or stabilization can cap increases, especially in some coastal markets. Job losses can cause renters to double up or fall behind, weakening occupancy and rent growth. Interest-rate sensitivity and leverage are further risks. Geographic diversification can help manage concentration, but it does not eliminate them.

### Are residential REITs a good inflation hedge?

Their short leases can make them inflation-responsive because rents may reset as costs and market rents rise. That is not a promise. Inflation can bring higher interest rates, pressure share prices, increase financing costs, and coincide with weak demand or new supply. Treat the ability to reset rents as one consideration, not a pure or guaranteed inflation hedge.

### What are coastal versus Sun Belt apartment REITs?

Coastal REITs often own in supply-constrained markets where limited land and strict zoning can support rents, while rent regulation can be heavier. Sun Belt REITs often own in faster-growing southern and western markets where population and jobs support demand but new supply can be easier to build. The trade-off is scarcity and regulation versus growth and supply risk. Some portfolios use both regions to balance those exposures.

### What is a single-family rental REIT?

A single-family rental, or SFR, REIT owns detached rental homes rather than apartment buildings. Often concentrated in suburban markets, it serves renters who want the space and yard of a house without purchasing one. The sector grew after the housing downturn as institutions assembled rental-home portfolios. Its operations differ from apartments: homes are more dispersed, management can be more complex, leases may be longer, and turnover can differ.

### What is a manufactured housing REIT?

It generally owns a manufactured-home community's land and shared infrastructure rather than each home. Residents usually own their homes and pay lot rent. Moving a manufactured home can be expensive and disruptive, which can support low turnover and steady occupancy; costs can be modest because the owner maintains land and common facilities rather than individual homes. Regulation of lot-rent increases, limited new community development, and economic pressure on residents remain real risks.

### How do residential REIT dividends get taxed?

Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income because the REIT generally did not pay corporate tax. Qualified REIT dividends can receive a 20% deduction under Section 199A; the 2025 OBBBA legislation made that deduction permanent. A distribution can also include return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than being taxed currently, or capital-gain distributions. The REIT reports the breakdown on Form 1099-DIV. Some investors use tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs. Tax treatment depends on the investor and current law, so consult a tax adviser; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How do rising interest rates affect residential REITs?

Higher rates can pressure REIT share prices, raise debt costs, squeeze cash flow, and complicate refinancing. They can also make homebuying less affordable and support rental demand and occupancy. The net effect depends on leverage, market demand, and the amount of fixed versus floating-rate debt. The sector has a demand-side offset that some property types do not, but no single rate relationship holds in every cycle.

### Are residential REITs defensive in a recession?

Housing demand does not disappear in a recession, so residential REITs are often relatively defensive. They are not recession-proof. Job loss can lead renters to double up, move home, downsize, or fall behind on rent. New supply arriving into a weaker market can compound the effect. Income, valuations, and share prices can still decline. Diversification and realistic expectations matter.

### What metrics should I look at for a residential REIT?

Use operating and financial measures together: same-store rent growth, occupancy, the local supply pipeline, and geographic mix; then FFO, AFFO, payout ratio, leverage, and NAV. Same-store rent growth and occupancy reveal the core portfolio's income trend. FFO and AFFO measure cash earnings, payout ratio frames distribution coverage, leverage indicates rate and refinancing sensitivity, and NAV estimates property value. No one metric tells the whole story.

### How can I invest in residential REITs?

Publicly traded apartment, single-family rental, and manufactured-housing REITs can be bought in an ordinary brokerage account. REIT mutual funds and ETFs offer baskets of residential and other REITs. Non-traded and private residential REITs are offered through a broker-dealer, typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors, and require a suitability review. Before committing capital, understand the segment, markets, rent and occupancy trends, fee structure, and liquidity terms.

## Glossary

- **Residential REIT:** A REIT that owns income-producing rental housing.
- **Apartment / Multifamily REIT:** A REIT that owns apartment communities.
- **Single-Family Rental REIT:** A REIT that owns portfolios of detached rental homes.
- **Manufactured Housing REIT:** A REIT leasing lots in manufactured-home communities.
- **Student Housing REIT:** A REIT owning purpose-built housing near universities.
- **Senior Housing REIT:** A REIT owning independent and assisted-living housing.
- **Occupancy:** The share of units leased and generating rent.
- **Rent Growth:** How much rents rise on renewals and new leases.
- **Same-Store Rent Growth:** Rent growth at properties owned across both comparison periods.
- **Rent Reset:** Re-pricing rents at lease renewal, frequent with short leases.
- **Household Formation:** New households forming and driving housing demand.
- **New Supply:** Newly built apartments that can pressure rents.
- **Rent Control:** Regulation limiting how much rents can be raised.
- **Coastal vs. Sun Belt:** Supply-constrained coastal versus growth-driven Sun Belt markets.
- **FFO / AFFO:** REIT cash-earnings measures used to assess income.
- **Payout Ratio:** The share of AFFO paid out as distributions.
