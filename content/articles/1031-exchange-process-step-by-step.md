# The 1031 Exchange Process, Step by Step

I keep seeing investors treat a 1031 exchange as a tax decision that becomes a logistics exercise later. In practice, it is a sequence decision from the start. A qualified intermediary must be in place before the sale, the proceeds must never reach the seller, and two hard clocks begin at closing. The tax deferral depends on getting that order right.

First-order thinking is, “I have 180 days to find something.” Second-order thinking is, “What needs to be finished before day 0, what can fail after day 45, and does the replacement still deserve my capital if the tax deadline disappeared?” The process is predictable. It rewards early preparation, a strong team, and an unwillingness to let calendar pressure replace judgment.

## Overview of the Process

A 1031 exchange continues an investment in real estate: an investor sells one investment property and reinvests the proceeds in another, deferring qualifying gain. The process exists to satisfy the rules behind that deferral. Most importantly, the investor cannot receive the proceeds, and the identification and closing deadlines must be met.

The practical sequence is: decide and plan; assemble the team; engage a [qualified intermediary](insights.html) before selling; sell the relinquished property; identify replacement property within 45 days; complete diligence and financing; close within 180 days; report the result; then decide whether to hold or exchange again. Each step makes the next one possible.

The active exchange usually occupies a few months, but the best work starts weeks before listing. Treat the deadlines as the fixed constraint and the pre-sale phase as the period when mistakes are cheapest to prevent.

## Step 1: Decide and Plan

First decide whether an exchange fits. It is often useful where embedded gain is meaningful, the investor wants to remain in real estate, and the deferral has a defined purpose: diversification, passive income, a trade-up, or estate planning. It may be less attractive where basis is high or the investor needs cash.

Estimate the taxes a qualifying exchange could defer: federal capital gains, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), [NIIT](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. The total can be larger than expected, which is why the planning effort and fees can be worthwhile.

Clarify what the replacement must accomplish—income, growth, passivity, or diversification—before listing. A replacement search begun before the sale has very different economics from one begun at day 30.

## Step 2: Assemble Your Team

Three roles should be in place early. The QI holds funds and documents the exchange. The CPA handles tax math, basis tracking, and Form 8824. An experienced advisor sources and vets replacement property and keeps the transaction aligned with the deadlines.

Early coordination gives the CPA time to confirm the exchange's value and flag the tax-return-date trap. It gives the advisor time to build a replacement shortlist and gives the QI time to prepare the sale-contract assignment. An independent, sponsor-agnostic advisor can compare options that fit the investor rather than offering only one sponsor's product.

The team's fees should be compared with the tax cost of a failed exchange, not with zero. The right professionals do not remove risk; they make the risk visible early enough to manage it.

## Step 3: Engage a Qualified Intermediary

Engage the QI before the relinquished-property sale closes, ideally during contract negotiation. The QI prepares the exchange agreement, takes assignment of the sale contract, and receives the proceeds directly at closing.

This is the most frequently missed step. If the sale closes, proceeds reach the seller, and only then the QI is engaged, the seller has constructive receipt. A QI cannot repair that after the fact. The exchange has failed and the sale is taxable.

Choose the QI for fund security as well as price: segregated qualified accounts, dual authorization, and bonding matter because the QI will hold the entire proceeds. Engaging early leaves time to do that diligence.

## Step 4: Sell the Relinquished Property

At closing, the QI receives the proceeds directly. That date is day 0, and it starts both the 45-day identification clock and the 180-day closing clock concurrently.

The exchange documents must be signed before closing, and the closing agent must know that funds go to the QI, not the seller. Note the day-45 and day-180 dates immediately. A late-year sale may require a tax-return extension so the filing date does not shorten the 180-day period.

## Step 5: Identify Replacement Property (45 Days)

Within 45 calendar days, deliver a written, signed identification notice to the QI. The usual methods are the **3-property rule** (up to three properties of any value), the **200% rule** (more than three, within 200% of value), and the less commonly used **95% exception**.

Identify backups rather than a single primary. After day 45, new properties cannot be added. A common structure is a primary direct property plus a fast-closing DST backup that can close in days if the primary fails. Begin searching before the sale, identify early rather than on day 44, and have the QI review descriptions and counts before delivery.

## Step 6: Due Diligence & Financing

Once a replacement is identified, complete diligence and financing inside the remaining window. For a direct property, order inspections, review title, and underwrite the asset. For a DST, review the [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html), sponsor, leverage, and projected distributions.

Financing is a common threat to the 180-day date. Start early and match the new financing to the debt-replacement target so a reduction in leverage does not create mortgage boot. A leveraged DST can provide pre-arranged non-recourse debt without a new loan application.

Diligence is still investment work. The replacement should stand on its own merits, not just as a vehicle for deferral. An identified fast-closing DST backup exists so the investor does not have to rush into a flawed direct deal.

## Step 7: Close Within 180 Days

Acquire identified replacement property within 180 days of the sale, with the QI transferring funds directly to closing. Full deferral generally requires the identified replacement to have equal or greater value, all equity to be reinvested, and any debt to be replaced.

Coordinate the seller, lender, title company, and QI well ahead of the deadline. A target closing around day 160–165 creates buffer for last-minute slippage. The seller still does not receive the exchange proceeds.

If the primary deal stalls late, an identified DST backup can close in days and provide the safety net needed to complete a fully deferred exchange.

## Key Takeaways

- Engage the QI before the sale; day 0 starts the 45-day and 180-day clocks.
- Identify in writing within 45 days, with backups; start financing early and close with buffer.
- Reinvest all equity, replace debt, and buy equal-or-greater value to avoid unintended boot.
- Report the exchange on Form 8824 and preserve the basis records with the CPA.

## Step 8: Report on Form 8824

Report the completed exchange on IRS Form 8824 with the tax return for the year the relinquished property was sold. The form records the exchanged properties, dates, values, any boot, and carryover basis in the replacement property.

This reporting establishes the deferred gain and the basis that will carry across years, future exchanges, and potentially a [step-up in basis at death](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html). Keep closing statements, QI agreements, the signed identification notice, and basis schedules. The CPA should handle the reporting, particularly where boot or a complex structure is involved.

## Step 9: Plan Your Next Move (or Hold)

The exchange can be one move in a longer plan. The investor may hold the replacement for income while gain remains deferred, or exchange again later to trade up, diversify, or become more passive.

The long-term capstone is repeated deferral followed by holding the final property until death, when a step-up in basis can eliminate the deferred gain for heirs. Passive, easily divisible property such as DST interests, or a 721 UPREIT into a REIT, may make that estate transition easier. The habits that made the first exchange work—planning, a team, and deadline discipline—remain useful in every later one.

## Common Process Pitfalls

The failure points are predictable: engaging the QI after closing; missing the 45-day identification; naming only one property without a backup; taking unplanned cash or debt relief; using vague descriptions; miscounting deadlines; letting financing consume the 180-day window; or skipping professional guidance.

The common thread is late, reactive work. The rules are not flexible just because a good deal fails late. Plan early, engage the QI before closing, identify backups, finance early, and leave buffer before day 180.

## Mapping the Process to a Realistic Calendar

Use the pre-sale weeks to decide whether a 1031 fits, estimate deferred tax, assemble the QI, CPA, and advisor, and build a replacement shortlist. There is no running deadline then, which is exactly why the work belongs there.

On day 0, the QI receives the funds and both clocks start. From roughly days 1–40, narrow the choices and deliver the signed identification by day 45, preferably by about day 30. From identification through roughly day 130, complete diligence and obtain financing; aim to have financing approved with about a month to spare.

Target closing around day 160–165. If the primary deal falters, use the DST backup that was identified on time. In the following tax season, report the exchange on Form 8824 for the sale year. Mapping milestones backward from day 180 converts an assumed “six months” into an actual plan with buffer.

## How Professionals Streamline the Process

Experienced exchangers front-load replacement search and team formation before listing. They coordinate the QI, CPA, lender, and closing agent from week one, set milestones backward from day 180, and surface trouble early rather than at day 160.

They also often identify a DST backup. That does not make the primary investment better; it makes a fragile one-deal plan more resilient. A sponsor-agnostic advisor can source and vet options that fit the dollar amount and goals while keeping the parties aligned.

## Why the Order Matters

Order is the whole process. Setup precedes the sale because the QI must receive proceeds. The sale starts the clocks. Identification constrains acquisition because the investor can only acquire what was identified. Reporting comes last because it records what happened.

The sequence is set up, sell, identify, acquire, report. Skipping or reordering steps typically defeats the exchange. The deadlines enforce timing; the order enforces the logic.

## The Step Investors Most Often Get Wrong

The critical setup error is closing before a QI has been engaged. The proceeds reach the seller, constructive receipt occurs, and the exchange is disqualified. A QI cannot retroactively undo receipt, and there is no IRS exception for not knowing the rule.

The result is a taxable sale, with capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax due. This is the most costly and most avoidable mistake: engage the QI before the sale closes.

## A Worked Example Across the Steps

Consider an investor selling a rental for $800,000 with $500,000 of equity and $300,000 of debt. Two weeks before closing, the investor engages a QI, signs the exchange agreement, and confirms the CPA and advisor. The sale closes May 1; the QI receives the $500,000, and both clocks start.

By May 25, day 24, the investor identifies an $800,000 net-lease property plus a diversified DST backup under the 3-property rule. The net-lease seller re-trades in June. The investor pivots to the DST, which closes in early July, well inside 180 days. The investment meets the $800,000 value target and the leveraged DST replaces the $300,000 debt, so there is no boot.

The following spring, the CPA reports the transaction on Form 8824 with the 2026 return and carries the original basis into the DST interest. The figures are illustrative, but the sequence shows how pre-sale setup, early identification, a backup, and debt matching can lead to a fully deferred, passive diversified replacement.

## The Process for Different Exchange Types

The steps above describe the delayed, or forward, exchange—the most common form. A reverse exchange acquires the replacement first and uses an exchange accommodation titleholder to park it; the acquire and sell steps switch order, while deadlines and reporting remain important.

An improvement or construction exchange expands acquisition to include completed and paid-for improvements within the 180-day period. A simultaneous exchange compresses sale and acquisition into one day and is now uncommon because the delayed structure is more practical. A DST exchange follows the same logic but can close in days, which is why it is a classic backup.

## How the Steps De-Risk the Exchange

Setup before sale eliminates the constructive-receipt risk. Direct QI fund flow keeps proceeds from reaching the seller. Identification with backups protects against a primary deal failing after day 45, when new properties cannot be named.

Acquisition planning protects against boot by matching value, reinvesting all equity, and replacing debt. A leveraged DST can supply non-recourse financing where that fits the facts. Accurate Form 8824 and basis tracking protect the future by preserving the deferred gain correctly through later exchanges and a possible estate step-up.

## From One Exchange to a Lifetime Strategy

Repeated exchanges can defer accumulated gain over a lifetime while the investor trades up, diversifies, and moves toward passive ownership. Holding the final replacement until death can pass a stepped-up basis to heirs and potentially eliminate the deferred gain.

The first exchange builds the reusable system: a trusted QI, a CPA who tracks basis, and an advisor who can source replacement property. The process becomes easier to execute when those relationships and habits already exist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the steps in a 1031 exchange?
Plan, assemble the QI/CPA/advisor team, engage the QI before sale, sell, identify within 45 days, perform diligence and financing, close within 180 days, file Form 8824, and plan the hold or next exchange.

### When do I engage a qualified intermediary?
Before the relinquished sale closes, ideally in contract negotiation. The QI needs to take assignment and receive proceeds; later engagement causes constructive receipt.

### How long does the 1031 process take?
The active exchange has a 180-day sale-to-closing limit, with identification in the first 45 days. Planning starts weeks earlier and reporting occurs in the following tax season.

### What form reports a 1031 exchange?
IRS Form 8824, filed with the return for the year the relinquished property sold. It reports the dates, values, boot, and carryover basis.

### What's the most common mistake in the 1031 process?
Engaging the QI after the sale closes. Receipt of proceeds causes constructive receipt and disqualifies the exchange.

### Do I need a CPA and an advisor, or just a QI?
The QI is required for funds and documents but does not provide tax advice or locate property. The CPA handles tax and Form 8824; an advisor sources and vets replacement property and coordinates deadlines.

### What happens at the 45-day mark?
The signed written replacement identification must have been delivered to the QI. After day 45, no new property can be added, so backups matter.

### How do I avoid boot during the process?
Acquire equal-or-greater value, reinvest all equity, and replace paid-off debt with new financing or, where suitable, a leveraged DST.

### Can I hold the replacement property indefinitely?
Yes. It can provide income while gain remains deferred, be exchanged again, or be held until death when a basis step-up may resolve the deferred gain.

### What if my financing is delayed?
Start early with a lender familiar with 1031 timing and identify a fast-closing DST backup. Loan delays are a common threat to day 180.

### Do I report the exchange the year I sell or the year I close?
Report it on Form 8824 with the return for the year the relinquished property was sold, even if replacement closes in the following calendar year.

### Can professionals make the process easier?
Yes. They front-load the team and shortlist, coordinate the QI, CPA, lender, and closing agent, use backups, and plan backward from day 180.

### Should I start before I list my property?
Yes. Use pre-listing time to decide whether a 1031 fits, estimate tax, assemble the team, and build a shortlist with a fast-closing DST backup.

### What documents do I need throughout the process?
Keep the exchange agreement and assignments, closing statements, signed identification notice, loan/debt information, and basis and depreciation schedules.

### How does the process change for a reverse or improvement exchange?
An exchange accommodation titleholder parks property under the safe harbor. Reverse exchanges acquire before sale; improvement exchanges count construction completed and paid for within 180 days. Both add cost and complexity.

### What happens to leftover cash at the end?
The QI releases unreinvested cash under the agreement, generally at the end of the period, and it is taxable boot up to the gain. Reinvest it, meet value, or consciously accept the known boot.

### How early should I engage each team member?
Engage the QI before sale, ideally in contract negotiation. Bring in the CPA and advisor earlier during planning, so the CPA can assess issues and the advisor can source property.

### Does the process work the same in every state?
Federal deadlines are uniform, but state QI requirements, nonresident withholding, filing, and rules such as California clawback can add obligations.

### Can I back out after starting?
Yes, but without replacement the sale is taxable and funds are released under the exchange agreement, commonly at the end of the period. Once the investor has received proceeds, the exchange cannot be restarted.

### How does the process end if everything goes right?
The investor owns equal-or-greater replacement property, has reinvested equity and replaced debt, has no boot, and reports fully deferred gain with carried basis. They can then hold, exchange again, or plan for an estate transition.

### Why does order matter?
The QI must be set up before sale, sale starts clocks, identification limits acquisition, acquisition completes exchange, and reporting documents it. The sequence cannot be freely rearranged.

### What's the single most important step?
Pre-sale QI setup. If the investor takes receipt of proceeds, later execution cannot save the exchange.

### Can the steps overlap?
Identification and acquisition overlap because the 45-day period sits inside the 180-day period. But identification must precede acquisition, while setup and sale come first and reporting follows completion.

### What if I complete some steps but not others?
Missing setup, the 45-day identification, or the 180-day acquisition generally makes the sale taxable. Partial deferral can remain where boot is intentionally recognized and the rest of a valid exchange qualifies.

### How long should I allow for each step?
Complete setup before sale, ideally a couple of weeks ahead; aim to identify around day 30; target closing around day 160–165; report in the following tax season.

### What's the difference between steps and 1031 rules?
Steps are the actions—set up, sell, identify, acquire, report. Rules are the substantive requirements, including like-kind property, same taxpayer, value and debt matching, QI, and deadlines. A successful exchange needs both.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** Independent party that holds proceeds and documents a deferred exchange.
- **Relinquished Property:** Property sold to begin the exchange; closing starts the clocks.
- **Replacement Property:** Like-kind property acquired to complete the exchange.
- **Identification Letter:** Signed written notice to the QI naming replacement property within 45 days.
- **45-Day Identification Period:** Sale-to-identification window.
- **180-Day Exchange Period:** Sale-to-replacement-acquisition window.
- **Form 8824:** IRS form reporting a like-kind exchange and carryover basis.
- **Carryover Basis:** Adjusted basis in the relinquished property carried to replacement.
- **Boot:** Taxable cash or unreplaced debt received in an exchange.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Access to or control over proceeds that disqualifies the exchange.
- **Backup Identification:** A fast-closing option, often a DST, named to protect the deadline.
- **Leveraged DST:** DST with pre-arranged non-recourse debt, avoiding a new loan application.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Basis reset at death that can eliminate deferred gain for heirs.
- **Exchange Agreement:** Pre-sale contract with the QI establishing the exchange.
- **Reverse Exchange:** Exchange where replacement is acquired before relinquished property sells.
- **Improvement Exchange:** Exchange using funds to build or renovate replacement property within 180 days.
