# REITs vs. Bonds for Income

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often an income discussion begins and ends with yield. The number is easy to compare. The claim on the cash flow is not.

Bonds and REITs are both common income holdings, but they are fundamentally different instruments. A bond is a contractual loan: it pays a fixed coupon and returns principal at maturity, subject to the issuer's credit risk. A REIT is an equity investment in real estate: it can offer higher yield, income growth, and an inflation consideration, but it has equity-like price risk, no maturity, and no guaranteed return of principal.

The first-order view says a higher REIT yield is preferable to a lower bond yield. The second-order view asks what supports the payment, whether it can grow or be cut, what happens to principal, and how the holding behaves when inflation or rates change. ## Yield and Risk Compared

REITs generally offer higher yield potential than bonds, plus a chance for income and value to grow. The REIT structure requires distributing at least 90% of taxable income, which can push its yield above the broad market. Because a REIT owns real estate, rents and property values can rise. If rents and income grow, the distribution can grow too.

Those features come with equity risk. A REIT's share price fluctuates, its distribution can be reduced or suspended, and it makes no promise to return principal. A higher yield is not a free addition to a portfolio; it reflects a different and less defined claim on cash flow.

Bonds offer the other side of the trade-off. A bond's coupon is contractual and paid on a set schedule. The issuer owes principal at maturity, provided it does not default. That makes a high-quality bond a more stable and predictable income instrument. The cost is usually lower yield, a payment that does not grow, and no usual inflation protection.

High-yield, or "junk," bonds can yield as much as or more than some REITs, but the higher income reflects higher default risk. The gap between REIT yields and high-quality bond yields also changes as rates change. Evaluate yield alongside the risk behind it, not in isolation.

## Interest-Rate Sensitivity

Both REITs and bonds are interest-rate sensitive, but the transmission is different. Bond prices move inversely to rates in a direct mathematical relationship: when newly issued bonds offer more, an existing fixed coupon is less attractive. Longer-duration bonds are more sensitive, falling more when rates rise and rising more when rates fall.

REITs can also come under pressure when rates rise. Higher discount rates lower the present value investors place on future property cash flows. Higher borrowing costs can squeeze REITs that use debt. Higher bond yields also compete with REIT distributions for income investors' dollars.

REITs have possible offsets that fixed coupons do not. Rents can rise through contractual escalations, market rent growth, and lease resets. Property values can benefit as replacement costs increase, and income may grow. None of these offsets is guaranteed, and the effect varies by property sector and lease structure. Still, they explain why the relationship between rates and REIT prices is less mechanical than it is for bonds.

In a rate shock, both holdings can fall together. Over longer periods, a REIT also responds to property fundamentals and behaves more like an equity, so the relationship is not constant.

## Inflation Protection

Inflation creates a sharp contrast. A conventional bond pays a fixed nominal coupon. When inflation rises, the real purchasing power of that coupon and the principal returned at maturity erodes. Inflation can also push rates higher, which pressures bond prices. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, or TIPS, are the principal exception: their principal adjusts with inflation.

REITs have an inflation consideration because they own real estate. Rents can rise through escalations, market rent growth, and lease resets; property values often rise as replacement costs increase. That can allow a REIT to grow income and value in a way a fixed-rate bond cannot.

This is not a guaranteed inflation hedge. It depends on sector, lease structure, pricing power, occupancy, and rate conditions. Rising rates can still pressure REIT prices. In general, short-lease, pricing-power sectors and TIPS have more direct inflation support than conventional fixed-rate bonds.

## Volatility Differences

REITs are generally more volatile than bonds. Public REIT prices trade continuously and reflect investor sentiment, rate expectations, and real-estate conditions. They can experience meaningful drawdowns during market sell-offs even when the underlying properties and rents remain stable. That equity-like volatility is part of the price paid for higher yield potential and possible growth.

High-quality bonds are generally steadier. Their contractual coupon and return of principal at maturity can anchor value and make them a portfolio stabilizer, especially during equity downturns. They are not immune to loss: rising rates, credit risk, and long duration can all move bond prices, and long-duration bonds can be quite volatile. Overall, though, bonds are usually the lower-volatility income holding.

## Key Takeaways

- REITs offer higher yield potential, possible income growth, and an inflation consideration, while bonds offer lower but stable contractual income and principal return at maturity.
- Both are interest-rate sensitive. Bond prices fall mechanically as rates rise; REITs face discount-rate, borrowing-cost, and yield-competition pressure.
- Conventional bonds usually have no inflation protection, except TIPS. REIT rents and values can rise with inflation, although the protection is imperfect.
- REITs have equity-like volatility and no principal guarantee. Bonds can provide a stabilizing core, while REITs can add growth and inflation awareness.

## Credit, Default, and Principal Risk

Beyond interest-rate risk, a bondholder faces credit risk: the chance that the issuer does not make interest payments or repay principal at maturity. High-quality government and investment-grade bonds generally have lower default risk. High-yield bonds pay more because their default risk is higher. If the issuer does not default, the contractual coupon and principal return give the bond a defined downside.

A REIT has no maturity date and no promise to return principal. The outcome depends on the value of the underlying real estate and the REIT's distributions. Property income can decline, share prices can fall, and a distribution can be cut. The bondholder is a lender with a contract; the REIT investor is an owner with an ongoing property-performance risk. That distinction is why one is usually steadier and the other can offer growth.

## Balancing REITs and Bonds

REITs and bonds are better viewed as complements than substitutes. Bonds can provide stability, contractual income, and principal return: a defensive anchor that supplies defined cash flow. REITs can provide higher yield potential, income growth, and an inflation-aware source of income, but with more volatility and no principal guarantee.

The mix should reflect goals, time horizon, income needs, and tolerance for price fluctuations. An investor focused on capital preservation and predictable cash flow may lean toward bonds. An investor seeking higher income and inflation resilience, and able to accept equity volatility, may include more REITs. Many investors use bonds as a stable core and REITs as a growth-and-income sleeve, diversified and sized so that REIT price swings do not threaten essential cash flow.

Inflationary or rising-rate periods can make the REIT sleeve's rent linkage especially relevant; calmer or deflationary periods can make the bond anchor especially valuable. This is a general framework, not a recommendation of a particular security or allocation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between REITs and bonds for income?

A bond is a contractual loan that pays a fixed coupon on a set schedule and returns principal at maturity, subject to the issuer's credit risk. It is generally lower-yielding but stable and predictable. A REIT is an equity investment in real estate that can offer higher income, distribution growth, and an inflation consideration, but it has no maturity, no principal guarantee, and equity-like volatility. Both are rate sensitive; REITs are usually more volatile. Neither is universally better, and many income investors use both. Verify current conditions with an advisor.

### Do REITs yield more than bonds?

As a general tendency, REITs offer higher yield potential than high-quality bonds. The 90% distribution rule and the equity risk borne by REIT investors both contribute. REIT distributions can also grow when rents and income grow. The comparison changes with the specific REIT, bond, and rate environment. High-yield bonds can offer competitive or higher yields with commensurate default risk. A higher yield always requires examination of volatility, credit or property risk, and principal protection.

### Are REITs and bonds both affected by interest rates?

Yes, through different channels. Bond prices move inversely to rates because a fixed coupon becomes less attractive when new bonds pay more; longer-duration bonds are more sensitive. Higher rates can lower REIT valuations through discount rates, raise borrowing costs, and make bond yields more competitive. REIT rent and property-value growth can partly offset that pressure, but not reliably. Shared rate sensitivity is why the two can fall together after a rate shock, even though REITs can diverge over time as property fundamentals change.

### Which is better for inflation, REITs or bonds?

REITs generally offer better inflation support than conventional bonds. A standard bond's fixed coupon and principal lose real value when inflation rises; TIPS are the important exception because their principal adjusts with inflation. REIT rents can rise through escalations, market rent growth, and lease resets, and property values can benefit from higher replacement costs. The effect varies by sector and lease structure, and rising rates can still hurt REIT prices. Short-lease, pricing-power REIT sectors and TIPS can provide more inflation support than conventional fixed-rate bonds.

### Are REITs more volatile than bonds?

Generally, yes. Public REIT prices move with market sentiment, rate expectations, and real-estate conditions and can have sharp drawdowns even when underlying properties are stable. High-quality bonds are anchored by contractual coupons and principal repayment and often stabilize a portfolio in equity downturns. Bonds can still decline from rising rates, credit risk, or long duration, but they are generally the steadier of the two. Holding both can moderate portfolio swings.

### Do bonds guarantee return of principal but REITs don't?

Essentially yes, with a credit-risk caveat. A bond issuer contractually promises coupons and principal at maturity. If the issuer defaults, full repayment may not occur; that is why high-quality bonds tend to yield less and high-yield bonds more. A REIT has no maturity and no promise to return principal. Its value and distributions depend on property performance and can decline. This is the structural difference between lending and owning.

### Can REITs replace bonds in a portfolio?

No. Bonds provide stability, contractual income, and principal return, which can steady a portfolio and support defined cash flow in an equity downturn. REITs can raise yield potential, offer income growth, and provide an inflation consideration, but they also add equity-like volatility and no principal guarantee. Replacing bonds entirely with REITs would remove the defensive role bonds play. Ignoring REITs entirely would forgo their possible growth and inflation benefits. The instruments usually complement rather than replace each other.

### How should I balance REITs and bonds for income?

There is no universal formula. The balance depends on goals, time horizon, income needs, and risk tolerance. Capital-preservation and predictable-income priorities can favor more bonds. A need for higher potential income and inflation resilience, coupled with tolerance for volatility, can support a REIT allocation. Many investors use bonds as the stable core and REITs as a growth and inflation sleeve. Conditions matter: inflation or rising rates can make the REIT linkage useful, while bonds remain an anchor. This is not a recommendation of a particular allocation; confirm suitability with an advisor.

### Why do REITs and bonds sometimes move together?

Both are rate sensitive. When rates rise, fixed-coupon bond prices fall. REIT valuations can also be pressured because higher discount rates can lower property values, borrowing costs can rise, and higher bond yields compete with REIT distributions. A rate shock can therefore push both down. The relationship is not fixed: REITs also respond to real-estate fundamentals and may grow income over time, so they generally behave more like equities than bonds over longer periods.

### Are REIT distributions as safe as bond coupons?

No. A high-quality bond coupon is contractual absent default. A REIT distribution depends on property income, which can fall when vacancies rise, tenants default, rents decline, costs increase, or rates increase. A REIT can reduce or suspend its distribution. Its higher yield and possible growth come with more income uncertainty. Diversification across REITs and sectors and attention to sustainable distributions can help manage risk, but they do not make the income contractual.

### What role do bonds play that REITs can't?

Bonds can provide a defensive anchor: defined coupon income, principal return at maturity subject to credit risk, and often relative stability in equity downturns. That can support capital preservation and reliable cash flow. REITs cannot reproduce that role because they have no maturity, no principal guarantee, variable distributions, and equity volatility. REITs can add yield, growth, and inflation consideration; bonds provide stability that remains useful even for investors who value REITs.

### Do REITs offer growth that bonds don't?

Yes, potentially. A conventional bond's coupon is fixed and its principal repayment is fixed, so nominal payments do not grow. A REIT can grow distributions through contractual rent escalations, market rent growth, and re-leasing. Property values can appreciate and potentially lift the share price. That growth can provide an inflation consideration. It is not guaranteed: distributions can remain flat or be cut and property values can fall over a real-estate cycle. The possible growth comes with more volatility and no principal guarantee.

### Are REITs or bonds better for a retiree?

Both can serve a retiree because they address different needs. Bonds support predictable income and capital preservation through contractual coupons and principal repayment subject to credit risk. REITs can offer higher yield potential, income growth, and inflation resilience over a long retirement, but with volatility and no principal guarantee. A retiree with essential near-term income needs may lean more on bonds; a retiree concerned about purchasing-power erosion may want a suitably sized REIT sleeve. The appropriate mix depends on income needs, horizon, and risk tolerance. This is general information, not advice.

### Do REITs and bonds belong in different accounts?

Both often produce ordinary-income-taxed cash flow, so some tax-aware investors place them in tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs and reserve taxable accounts for more tax-efficient qualified-dividend stocks. There are important exceptions. Municipal-bond interest is often federally tax-exempt and may suit a taxable account. The 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends applies in taxable accounts. Bond type, REIT distribution mix, account limits, and income needs can change the result. Confirm account placement with a tax advisor.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns or finances income-producing real estate.
- **Bond:** A contractual loan paying a fixed coupon and returning principal at maturity.
- **Coupon:** A bond's fixed periodic interest payment.
- **Yield:** Annual income as a percentage of price.
- **Duration:** A bond's sensitivity to interest-rate changes.
- **Interest-Rate Risk:** The risk that rising rates reduce a security's value.
- **Credit Risk:** The risk a bond issuer fails to pay interest or principal.
- **Principal:** The face amount a bond returns at maturity.
- **Inflation Hedge:** An asset whose income or value rises with inflation.
- **TIPS:** Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, whose principal adjusts with inflation.
- **Volatility:** The degree of price fluctuation over time.
- **Distribution:** A REIT's dividend paid from its income.
- **Distribution-Cut Risk:** The risk a REIT reduces or suspends its dividend.
- **Rent Escalation:** A contractual lease increase that can grow REIT income.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The REIT requirement driving high distributions.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across assets to reduce risk.

## REITs vs. bonds for income at a glance

| Factor | REITs | Bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income form | Dividends from real estate cash flow | Fixed coupon interest |
| Inflation protection | Rents and values can rise with inflation | Fixed coupons lose real value |
| Interest-rate sensitivity | Sensitive, with possible income growth | Prices fall when rates rise |
| Volatility | Higher, equity-like | Lower, especially high-grade |
| Principal risk | Market-priced and can decline | Credit/default risk; par at maturity |
| Income growth | Distributions can grow over time | Fixed until maturity |

## Which should you choose?

Bonds offer steadier income and lower volatility, with high-grade issues returning par at maturity subject to credit risk. Their fixed coupons have no usual inflation protection, and their prices fall when rates rise. REITs can offer growing income and inflation support through rents, at the cost of greater volatility and equity-like principal risk. Many income investors use both, balancing the bond anchor against the REIT growth and inflation sleeve.
