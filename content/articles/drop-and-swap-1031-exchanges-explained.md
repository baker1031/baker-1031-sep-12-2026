# Drop-and-Swap 1031 Exchanges Explained

I keep seeing the same partnership-sale tension: the property is ready to sell, but the owners are no longer pursuing the same outcome. One partner wants to keep capital in real estate; another wants liquidity; a third may want a different replacement asset altogether. The sale is one event. The owners’ goals are not.

That is where a drop-and-swap enters the conversation. In a partnership or multi-member LLC, the entity is ordinarily the taxpayer that owns the real estate. The partners cannot simply receive their shares at closing and each run a separate exchange. A drop-and-swap distributes direct tenant-in-common interests to the partners before the sale, so each can later exchange or cash out individually. It can solve a real ownership problem, but it brings meaningful timing, holding, documentation, and IRS-scrutiny risk.

First-order thinking is, “Split the ownership now so each partner gets what they want.” Second-order thinking is, “Will the partners’ ownership, timing, documentation, and actual conduct support that each held a direct interest for investment?” The technique can be legitimate. It is not a last-minute paperwork fix.

## The Partnership Exchange Problem

The starting point is the same-taxpayer rule. In a partnership or multi-member LLC that owns real estate, the entity—not each individual partner—owns the property and is the taxpayer for the exchange. The taxpayer that sells the relinquished property must acquire the replacement property. If the partnership sells, the partnership must complete the exchange and acquire the replacement. Individual partners cannot simply take their shares and exchange them because they were not the taxpayers that owned the relinquished real estate.

That becomes difficult when partner goals diverge. Some partners may want to defer gain through replacement property, while others want to take cash and pay tax. Even the exchanging partners may want different replacement properties. The partnership can complete an entity-level exchange into one replacement property or a set of replacements it will continue to own, but it cannot readily give each partner an independent path while remaining the single taxpayer.

An entity-level exchange can work when every partner wants to stay together in the new investment. It does not solve a separation. The drop-and-swap, and the related swap-and-drop, address this mismatch by changing the ownership before or after the exchange so that the relevant person owns a direct real-property interest rather than only a partnership interest.

## What “Drop-and-Swap” Means

The “drop” is a distribution of the partnership’s real property to the partners as tenant-in-common, or TIC, interests before a sale. Each partner moves from owning a partnership interest—which is not itself exchanged for real property under Section 1031—to owning a direct, undivided fractional interest in the real estate. The “swap” is the later transaction: each partner, now holding a direct property interest, can pursue an individual [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) or sell and take cash.

In practice, the partnership distributes the property as TIC interests and steps back from owning it directly. Once the property is sold, each partner handles that partner’s own TIC interest. A partner who wants deferral can exchange into a chosen replacement. A partner who wants liquidity can sell and pay the tax. This is how the technique permits divergent goals that the entity structure could not accommodate.

The ordering is the point: drop first, then swap. It differs from a [swap-and-drop](swap-and-drop-1031-exchanges-explained.html), in which the partnership exchanges first and later distributes interests in the replacement property. Drop-and-swap is widely used when partners want to separate, but each partner’s ability to claim investment holding after the distribution is the central issue.

## Timing and the Held-for-Investment Question

Each partner must hold the distributed TIC interest for investment to support that partner’s exchange. A last-minute distribution creates an obvious problem. If the partnership drops the property to the partners immediately before the sale, the IRS may argue that the partners did not really hold the interests for investment; they held them only long enough to facilitate what was, in substance, a partnership sale.

The shorter the time between the drop and the sale, the easier that argument becomes. A longer period between distribution and sale strengthens the position that the partners genuinely held their own interests for investment and then made their own sale or exchange decisions. That is why a distribution well before the sale is materially more defensible than a distribution made on the eve of closing.

There is no bright-line required holding period. Some practitioners suggest holding across a tax year or longer to demonstrate genuine investment holding, but that is not always workable when a sale is already imminent. The tension is structural: partners often consider a drop because a sale is near, while a near-sale drop is the fact that may weaken the position. Experienced counsel should plan the timing, holding, and documentation well in advance.

## IRS Scrutiny and Documentation

The IRS understands that a near-sale drop can convert what appears to be a partnership sale into separate individual exchanges. It can challenge the transaction on held-for-investment or substance grounds and potentially disallow the partners’ exchanges. That makes this a genuine audit-risk question, not just a filing detail.

Documentation needs to show a real distribution and real investment holding. The record should cover the distribution of TIC interests, the partners’ genuine holding of those interests for investment, meaningful separation between the distribution and the sale, and the partners’ independent decisions about whether to exchange or cash out. The more the record supports the reality of separate ownership, the better the position if examined.

Drop-and-swaps are common and are often respected when they are completed with adequate timing, genuine partner holding, and good documentation. Some states—notably California—require disclosure of drop-and-swap transactions on tax forms. A carefully planned early drop differs materially from a last-minute distribution with no support. Use experienced tax counsel to assess the facts, arrange the documentation, and explain the risk.

## When a Drop-and-Swap May Fit

This technique is designed for a partnership that owns real estate, expects to sell it, and has partners who want to go separate ways. Some may exchange into different replacements; some may cash out. By converting the partnership ownership into direct TIC interests before the sale, the partners can each pursue their own path.

It fits best when the partnership has time to plan. The partners can recognize their divergence early, distribute the interests well before a sale, and permit meaningful investment holding. A rushed, last-minute drop is the version to avoid because it intensifies the held-for-investment and substance risks.

Whether to use drop-and-swap or swap-and-drop depends on the partners’ goals, timing, and relative risks. The drop-and-swap distributes before sale. The swap-and-drop has the partnership exchange first and distribute later. Neither choice should be made from a template. For a partnership with divergent goals, these are sophisticated tools that need a fact-specific structure, adequate timing, thorough documentation, and experienced tax counsel.

## Key takeaways

- A partnership is the single taxpayer, so partners cannot simply run separate exchanges from partnership-owned property.
- A drop-and-swap distributes the property as TIC interests before sale, allowing each partner to exchange or cash out independently.
- The central concern is whether each partner held the distributed interest for investment; a last-minute drop is risky.
- Early timing, genuine holding, careful documentation, and experienced tax counsel are fundamental to a defensible approach.

## Alternatives and Broader Considerations

An entity-level exchange remains an alternative when the partners want to stay together in the replacement property. It is not a solution for partners who want to separate. For partners who expect to cash out, an installment arrangement or another approach may be considered alongside the transactions of partners who exchange.

Some partnerships restructure ownership long before a sale, converting to a TIC structure or otherwise giving partners direct interests well in advance. That can avoid the rushed-drop problem altogether. The earlier the restructuring, the stronger the factual case that the partners held their interests for investment before a later sale.

These are complex partnership-exchange decisions. An accountant and attorney experienced in partnership exchanges should assess whether a drop-and-swap, swap-and-drop, entity-level exchange, or a combination best fits the partners’ goals and facts. The risk is not avoided by calling the transaction something different; it is managed through real ownership, timing, documentation, and advice before the sale process becomes irreversible.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a drop-and-swap 1031 exchange?

It is a technique in which a partnership distributes its real property to the partners as tenant-in-common interests before a sale. Each partner then holds a direct property interest and may complete an individual 1031 exchange or cash out. It addresses the fact that the partnership is the single taxpayer while the partners may have divergent goals.

### Why can’t partners just do separate exchanges?

The partnership, not the individual partners, owns the property and is the taxpayer. The same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer that sold the relinquished property to acquire the replacement. A distribution changes who owns the property before the sale; without it, the partnership must complete the exchange.

### What do “drop” and “swap” refer to?

The drop is the distribution of real property from the partnership to the partners as TIC interests, converting partnership interests into direct real-property interests. The swap is each partner’s later 1031 exchange or sale. Drop comes first, which distinguishes this approach from swap-and-drop.

### What is the main risk of a drop-and-swap?

The held-for-investment requirement and timing are the main risks. If the distribution happens immediately before the sale, the IRS may argue the partners held the interests only momentarily to facilitate a partnership sale. That argument can disqualify the exchanges.

### How long should partners hold their interests before the sale?

There is no bright-line period. The longer the partners hold the distributed interests, the stronger the held-for-investment position. Some practitioners suggest holding across a tax year or longer. A last-minute drop is risky; an early distribution with meaningful holding is more defensible. Plan it well in advance with counsel.

### Does the IRS scrutinize drop-and-swaps?

Yes. The IRS knows that near-sale drops can turn a partnership sale into separate partner exchanges and can challenge them on held-for-investment or substance grounds. California also requires disclosure of drop-and-swap-type transactions. Timing, documentation, and experienced counsel matter.

### How do I document a drop-and-swap?

Document the TIC distribution, genuine investment holding by each partner, a meaningful separation between distribution and sale, and each partner’s independent decision to exchange or sell. Thorough support for real distribution and investment holding improves the position if the IRS examines it. Experienced counsel should handle the documents.

### When should I use a drop-and-swap?

Consider it when a partnership owns real estate that is being sold and the partners want different outcomes, such as separate replacements for some and a cash-out for others. It is best planned early enough for the distribution and investment holding to be credible.

### What is the difference between drop-and-swap and swap-and-drop?

In a drop-and-swap, the property is distributed to partners before sale so each sells or exchanges individually. In a swap-and-drop, the partnership exchanges first and later distributes interests in the replacement property. The right ordering depends on the partners’ goals, timing, and risks.

### Can partners use DSTs after a drop-and-swap?

Yes. Once each partner holds a direct TIC interest, each may pursue a separate 1031 exchange into a DST or another replacement property. DSTs can provide diversified, passive replacement options for individual partners, subject to suitability review.

### Is a drop-and-swap a do-it-yourself technique?

No. It is a sophisticated structure with real IRS-scrutiny risk. The timing, held-for-investment analysis, documentation, and choice among alternatives require an experienced tax attorney and CPA. Do not attempt it without that guidance.

### Should partnerships plan ahead for partner separation?

Yes. The most defensible path is to plan ownership well before an anticipated sale, possibly by converting to a TIC structure so partners already hold direct interests. Early planning strengthens the held-for-investment case and avoids the last-minute-drop problem.

### Can some partners exchange while others cash out?

Yes. After the TIC distribution, each partner handles that partner’s interest separately. Partners who want tax deferral can pursue a 1031; those who want liquidity can sell their interests and pay tax. This individual choice is a primary benefit of the approach.

### Does California specifically watch for drop-and-swaps?

Yes. California requires disclosure of drop-and-swap-type transactions on its tax forms. That reporting requirement reflects the state’s attention to the technique and reinforces the need for proper reporting, counsel, and documentation.

### Is a drop-and-swap respected by the IRS?

Often, when it has adequate timing, genuine partner investment holding, and good documentation. The approach is common and can be legitimate. A careless, last-minute drop with no documentation carries a real risk of challenge, so counsel should structure it carefully.

## Glossary

**Drop-and-Swap:** Distribution of partnership property to partners as TIC interests before a sale so each can exchange separately.

**Partnership Exchange Problem:** The difficulty caused by the partnership being the single taxpayer, preventing separate partner exchanges.

**Same-Taxpayer Rule:** The requirement that the taxpayer selling the relinquished property acquire the replacement property.

**Tenant-in-Common (TIC) Interest:** A direct, undivided fractional interest in real property that may be distributed to partners.

**Drop:** Distribution of property from the partnership to the partners as TIC interests.

**Swap:** Each partner’s later individual 1031 exchange, or sale, of that partner’s TIC interest.

**Held for Investment:** The requirement that a partner hold the distributed direct interest for investment to support an exchange.

**Swap-and-Drop:** An alternative in which the partnership exchanges first and distributes interests to partners afterward.

**Entity-Level Exchange:** A 1031 exchange completed by the partnership itself, keeping partners together in the replacement property.

**Substance Over Form:** The principle the IRS may use to argue that a purported partner sale was actually a partnership sale.

**Holding Period:** The time a partner owns a distributed interest before sale; a longer period is generally more defensible.

**IRS Scrutiny:** The attention the IRS gives these transactions because of timing and substance concerns.

**Disclosure Requirement:** A state reporting obligation, such as California’s reporting of drop-and-swap transactions.

**Multi-Member LLC:** An LLC taxed as a partnership and subject to the same partnership exchange problem.

**Cash-Out Partner:** A partner who prefers to sell and pay tax rather than exchange.

**Tax Counsel:** The experienced attorney needed to structure a defensible drop-and-swap.
