# How REITs Pay Dividends: The 90% Distribution Rule

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the yield figure read as the whole investment case. It is only the start. A REIT's dividend can be high because the structure channels income outward, but the more useful question is whether the cash flow actually supports it.

REITs are known for substantial dividend yields. The principal reason is a rule: to retain its tax-advantaged status and avoid corporate-level income tax, a qualifying REIT must distribute at least **90%** of its taxable income to shareholders each year. That required payout helps explain why yields are often higher than those of typical stocks. It does not make yields or distributions guaranteed.

This guide covers the **90%** distribution requirement, why yields are high, how distributions are funded, return of capital versus ordinary income, tax treatment, and dividend sustainability. REIT investments carry risk. This is educational information, not tax advice; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice, and you should verify your circumstances with a CPA. For the distinctions among listed, public non-traded, and private structures, see the [full primer on REITs and the 721 exchange](reits-guide.html).

The first-order view is that a high REIT yield means more income. The second-order view asks where the distribution comes from, whether it is covered by funds from operations, what its tax character is, and what a high yield may already be signaling about risk. Capital should be committed to the latter question, not only the former.

## The 90% Distribution Requirement

The **90%** distribution requirement is the central rule of REIT taxation. To qualify as a REIT and receive the tax benefit of avoiding corporate-level income tax, a company must distribute at least **90%** of its taxable income to shareholders each year as dividends. In exchange, the qualifying REIT avoids corporate tax on the distributed amount; the income is instead taxed at the shareholder level.

The payout test is part of a broader group of qualification requirements. A REIT must hold at least **75%** of its assets in real estate, cash, or government securities; derive at least **75%** of gross income from real estate; have at least **100** shareholders; and not be closely held under the **5/50** rule. Investors feel the distribution test most directly because it is the mechanism that creates the dividend.

The trade is straightforward: pay out most taxable income and receive the corporate-tax benefit. Unlike an ordinary corporation that can retain a larger share of earnings, the qualifying REIT has limited retained taxable income. That is why REITs are reliable high-dividend payers as a matter of structure, while still remaining subject to the risks of the underlying business.

## Why REIT Yields Are High

An ordinary corporation may retain earnings to reinvest, repurchase shares, or build cash. A REIT must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income, so it sends a larger share of what it earns to shareholders. That required payout, combined with income-producing real estate and its rental cash flow, is the structural source of a REIT's high yield.

This is why a high yield on a REIT is not automatically the same warning sign it can be on an ordinary stock. It can simply be the rule working as intended. Still, “not automatically” is important. A REIT's property income can weaken, its distribution can be uncovered, and an unusually high yield can reflect concern rather than opportunity.

> A REIT's payout structure explains why the yield can be high. It does not replace the work of testing whether the payout is sustainable.

## How Distributions Are Funded

Equity REIT distributions are funded mainly by property cash flow. An equity REIT collects rent from tenants in offices, apartments, warehouses, retail properties, and other real estate. After operating expenses, debt service, and reserves, the remaining operating cash flow supports dividends. In practical terms, tenant rent checks are the source behind investor distribution checks.

Funds from operations (FFO) and adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) are useful REIT-specific measures for this question. They approximate the cash flow available for dividends by adjusting net income for real-estate depreciation and other items. FFO adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization and excludes gains and losses on property sales because depreciation is a non-cash charge that can understate a REIT's cash generation. AFFO makes further adjustments for recurring capital expenditures and other items, moving closer to cash actually available to distribute.

Compare the dividend with FFO and AFFO. A payout below FFO/AFFO is funded by operations and is generally a healthier sign. A payout above those measures may rely on other sources. The comparison—often described as the payout ratio—does not answer every question, but it is an essential test of coverage.

## Return of Capital vs. Income

Not every dollar of a REIT distribution has the same tax character. Part can be ordinary income, and part can be a [return of capital](return-of-capital-in-reit-distributions.html). The ordinary-income portion represents taxable REIT earnings paid to the shareholder. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction.

Return of capital is not currently taxed. It reduces an investor's cost basis in the shares, deferring the tax until sale, generally as capital gain through the lower basis. This treatment often arises because real-estate depreciation reduces taxable income below actual cash flow. A REIT can distribute cash above taxable income, with the excess characterized as return of capital.

That can be tax-advantaged in the current year, but it should not be treated as automatically favorable in every respect. A large or rising return-of-capital component can indicate that a REIT is distributing capital rather than earnings. Verify the distribution's character, including through Form 1099-DIV and a CPA, and consider it beside the cash-flow coverage question.

## Sustainability of REIT Dividends

The central income question is whether the dividend can keep being paid. A sustainable distribution is covered by operating cash flow—FFO/AFFO—so rents support the payment and leave the REIT better positioned to continue, and potentially grow, the distribution. An unsustainable distribution exceeds cash flow and may instead be funded by borrowing, asset sales, or excessive return of capital. Those sources cannot support a payment indefinitely and can precede a cut.

Look for a payout ratio above **100%** of FFO/AFFO, a large or rising return-of-capital component, rising leverage used to fund distributions, or a [yield far above peers](evaluating-a-reits-dividend-yield.html). An outlier yield can be the market pricing concern about the REIT's dividend, portfolio quality, or financial health; a falling share price itself raises the yield.

This is where first-order and second-order thinking divide. First-order thinking chases the highest visible yield. Second-order thinking tests the coverage, the return-of-capital trend, the leverage, and the economics of the property portfolio. A moderate, sustainable and growing dividend can be preferable to a high payout that is at risk.

## Key Takeaways

- The **90%** distribution rule requires a qualifying REIT to pay out at least **90%** of taxable income in exchange for avoiding corporate-level tax, helping explain high yields.
- Distributions are primarily funded by operating cash flow from rents after expenses; FFO and AFFO help measure whether that cash flow covers the payout.
- A distribution can be part ordinary income, generally taxed at ordinary rates with a **20%** Section 199A deduction for qualified REIT dividends, and part return of capital, which is not currently taxed and reduces basis.
- A dividend covered by operating cash flow has a stronger foundation. Borrowing, asset sales, excessive return of capital, rising leverage, an outlier yield, or a payout above **100%** of FFO/AFFO all warrant scrutiny.

## How REIT Dividends Are Taxed

Most REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rates that apply to many corporate dividends. The reason is the same tax design: the REIT generally did not pay corporate income tax on the distributed income, so shareholders bear the tax at ordinary rates.

Qualified REIT dividends receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction. That deduction can lower the effective top federal rate on qualifying REIT dividend income to roughly **29.6%**, rather than the full top ordinary rate. The deduction was made permanent by the **2025** One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA). It applies to the ordinary-income portion of qualified REIT dividends, not to the return-of-capital portion, which is not currently taxed, or to capital-gain distributions.

The after-tax yield therefore depends on the distribution's character. Return of capital reduces cost basis and generally defers tax to a later sale as capital gain. These are general descriptions, not personal tax conclusions. Consult a CPA about the current rules and their application to your own facts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the 90% distribution rule for REITs?

A qualifying REIT must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income to shareholders each year as dividends to maintain REIT status and avoid corporate-level income tax. It must also satisfy the **75%** asset test, the **75%** gross-income test, the **100**-shareholder requirement, and the not-closely-held **5/50** rule. In exchange for distributing most income, it avoids corporate tax on the distributed amount and shareholders are taxed on the income.

### Why are REIT dividend yields so high?

The required payout explains much of it. While an ordinary company can retain earnings for reinvestment, buybacks, or cash, a REIT distributes at least **90%** of taxable income. Rental cash flow from income-producing property adds to the effect. The resulting high yield is often structural rather than evidence of distress, but it still must be checked for sustainability.

### How are REIT distributions funded?

For an equity REIT, rents from offices, apartments, warehouses, retail and other properties flow through operating expenses, debt service, and reserves; remaining operating cash flow funds dividends. FFO and AFFO are the key measures for testing whether that cash supports the payout. A dividend below FFO/AFFO is covered by operations; one above may be drawing on other sources.

### What is return of capital in a REIT distribution?

Return of capital is the part of a distribution that is not currently taxable. It reduces the shareholder's cost basis and generally defers tax until sale as capital gain through the reduced basis. It often arises when real-estate depreciation lowers taxable income below cash flow. A distribution can therefore be part return of capital and part ordinary income. The tax treatment can be favorable currently, but a large or rising return-of-capital share can be a sustainability warning. Confirm the character on Form 1099-DIV with a CPA.

### How are REIT dividends taxed?

Most are ordinary income because the REIT generally did not pay corporate tax on the underlying income. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction, which can bring the effective top federal rate to roughly **29.6%**. The **2025** OBBBA made the deduction permanent according to the source. Return of capital is not currently taxed and reduces basis. Tax results depend on the distribution's character and individual circumstances, so consult a CPA.

### How do I know if a REIT's dividend is sustainable?

Test coverage with FFO/AFFO. A payout below those measures is supported by operating cash flow and has a stronger foundation. A payout that exceeds cash flow, or is financed by borrowing, asset sales, or excessive return of capital, may not continue indefinitely. Warning signs include a payout ratio above **100%**, rising leverage, a large or growing return-of-capital component, and a yield far above peers. Look beyond the headline percentage.

### What is FFO and AFFO?

FFO, or funds from operations, starts with net income and adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization while excluding gains and losses on property sales. It helps address the non-cash depreciation charge that can understate real-estate cash flow. AFFO, or adjusted funds from operations, makes further adjustments for recurring capital expenditures and other items, getting closer to cash available to distribute. Comparing dividends with FFO/AFFO is generally more useful for coverage than using depreciation-distorted net income alone.

### Does the 90% rule mean REITs can't grow?

No, but it changes how they grow. Because a REIT distributes at least **90%** of taxable income, it retains less earnings for reinvestment. It often funds development and acquisitions with new equity or debt. The rule applies to taxable income, not cash flow, and depreciation can shelter some cash flow for reinvestment. Growth REITs can still emphasize reinvestment and appreciation; the structure makes external capital more important.

### Is a higher REIT yield always better?

No. A yield far above peers may show that the market is worried about dividend durability, property quality, or financial health. A falling share price also mechanically raises yield. The better questions are whether FFO/AFFO covers the dividend, whether the return-of-capital share is growing, how much leverage is present, and what total return—not just income—might be. Do not chase the highest number without testing its foundation.

### What is the 20% deduction on REIT dividends?

The Section 199A deduction allows a **20%** deduction for qualified REIT ordinary dividend income. Because REIT dividends are generally ordinary income, the deduction can reduce the effective top federal rate to roughly **29.6%**. The **2025** OBBBA made it permanent. It does not apply to return of capital, which is not currently taxed, or to capital-gain distributions. Ask a CPA how it applies to your own return.

### Can a REIT cut its dividend?

Yes. Dividends are not guaranteed. Falling rents, rising vacancies, higher interest costs, economic stress, or an unsustainable prior payout can cause a REIT to reduce or suspend its distribution. The **90%** rule applies to taxable income, and taxable income can decline. Dividend cuts have occurred, particularly during downturns or when REITs have overextended. Coverage, leverage, and the payout ratio should be examined before depending on the income.

### Do all REITs follow the 90% rule?

Any company that elects and qualifies to be a REIT must meet the **90%** distribution requirement to preserve REIT status and its corporate-tax benefit. That includes equity REITs, mortgage REITs (mREITs), and hybrid REITs, whether traded or non-traded. A company that failed the rule could lose REIT status and face corporate tax. REIT types differ in what they own and how they earn income—rents versus mortgage interest—not in the basic qualifying payout requirement.

### Why is REIT income mostly ordinary income, not qualified dividends?

Qualified dividends receive lower long-term-capital-gain rates because the corporation paid corporate tax on the underlying earnings and the lower shareholder rate helps avoid double taxation. A REIT generally avoids corporate income tax by distributing its income, so the shareholder receives ordinary-income treatment instead. The tax is imposed once at the shareholder level, at ordinary rates, with the **20%** Section 199A deduction providing partial relief for qualified REIT dividends. Consult a CPA for individual treatment.

### Are REIT dividends guaranteed?

No. The **90%** rule requires a qualifying REIT to distribute a share of its taxable income, but it does not guarantee the amount of taxable income or the size of the dividend. Rents, occupancy, expenses, interest costs, and market conditions all influence cash flow. A REIT can cut or suspend a distribution and its share price can decline. Treat the income as a variable, risk-bearing distribution and size reliance on it accordingly.

## Glossary

### 90% Distribution Rule

A REIT must distribute **>=90%** of taxable income annually.

### REIT

A trust that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.

### Dividend Yield

Annual dividends relative to the share price.

### Corporate-Level Tax

The tax a qualifying REIT avoids by distributing income.

### FFO

Funds from operations—a REIT cash-flow metric.

### AFFO

Adjusted funds from operations—cash available to distribute.

### Payout Ratio

The dividend as a percentage of FFO/AFFO.

### Return of Capital

A distribution portion that reduces basis and is not taxed now.

### Ordinary Income

The taxable portion of a REIT dividend, taxed at ordinary rates.

### Section 199A Deduction

The **20%** deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

### OBBBA

The **2025** law making the **20%** deduction permanent.

### Qualified REIT Dividend

An ordinary REIT dividend eligible for the **20%** deduction.

### Cost Basis

Your investment basis, reduced by return of capital.

### Sustainability

Whether cash flow covers the dividend over time.

### 5/50 Rule

A REIT cannot be closely held by five or fewer owners.

### Dividend Cut

A reduction or suspension of a REIT's distribution.
