# 721 Exchange Explained: How to Convert Property Into REIT Units

I keep seeing the 721 exchange described as a cousin of the 1031 exchange. That is useful as far as it goes: both can defer gain. But the destination is different. A 1031 keeps an owner in direct real estate. A 721 moves the owner into an operating partnership and, ultimately, a REIT structure.

Baker 1031 Research updated this 721 Exchange guide in June 2026. It is a 16 min read. Section 721 can let a property owner contribute real estate to a REIT’s operating partnership in return for partnership interests called OP units. The transaction can defer gain, but it also trades a particular property for professionally managed REIT exposure, passive ownership, a potential path to liquidity, and estate-planning considerations.

First-order thinking sees only the deferred tax. Second-order thinking asks whether the owner is comfortable losing direct control, accepting the REIT’s fees and management, holding a security with different liquidity, and making a move that is generally one-way. Those questions matter as much as the tax deferral.

## What is a 721 exchange?

A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) occurs when an owner contributes real estate to a REIT’s operating partnership and receives partnership interests in return. Under Section 721, gain or loss generally is not recognized when property is contributed to a partnership for an interest in that partnership. Rather than sell and pay tax, or complete a 1031 into another direct property, the owner receives tax-deferred OP units.

The structure is typically an UPREIT—an umbrella partnership REIT. The REIT owns real estate through its operating partnership. The property contributor becomes a partner in that operating partnership and receives [operating partnership units](op-units-explained.html), which represent the contributor’s economic interest in the portfolio.

The result is a transition from one direct property to exposure across the REIT’s holdings. This is fundamentally different from a 1031 exchange, which preserves direct real estate ownership. The 721 is a shift into a pooled, professionally managed structure and should be evaluated as such.

## How the conversion works

The path runs from property to OP units and, optionally, to REIT shares. First, the investor contributes property to the operating partnership. The partnership accepts it and issues OP units representing the agreed value. The contribution is tax-deferred under Section 721, so gain is not recognized at that moment.

Second, the investor holds the OP units. They represent a partnership stake in the portfolio and typically pay distributions comparable to REIT dividends. The holder has economic exposure to the real estate owned by the operating partnership without managing the properties.

Third, after a holding period—often around a year, depending on the REIT—the investor can typically convert the units to REIT shares, commonly on a 1:1 basis, or in some structures redeem them for cash. Conversion or cash redemption is generally taxable and can trigger the deferred gain. Holding the units continues the deferral and distributions; conversion creates liquidity, but it has a tax cost.

## Why convert property into REIT units

Diversification is one reason. A direct property concentrates exposure in one building, location, tenant group, and business plan. OP units can provide a stake in a REIT’s broader portfolio of properties, property types, and markets. The exchange does not remove real-estate risk, but it can reduce dependence on a single asset.

Liquidity is another. Direct real estate is generally illiquid and requires an all-or-nothing sale. OP units can be converted to REIT shares over time. A public REIT’s shares may be sold in the market, allowing an investor to access a portion of value rather than sell an entire property. Non-traded REIT liquidity is more limited and depends on its program.

The third is passivity. The REIT manages the assets while the unit holder receives distributions. Estate planning can also improve because units are easier to divide among heirs than a single property. If they are held until death, a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can potentially erase deferred gain under current law. These are possible benefits, not guarantees of distributions, price, liquidity, or tax results.

## OP units and REIT shares

OP units are partnership-level interests in the operating partnership that directly owns the REIT’s real estate. They entitle the holder to distributions comparable to REIT dividends and reflect the holder’s economic stake in the portfolio.

REIT shares are corporate-level interests in the REIT itself. For public REITs, they may trade in the market. OP units are commonly convertible to REIT shares after a holding period, often on a one-for-one basis. The two can be economically similar in their portfolio exposure, but they are different forms of ownership.

The important difference is tax. Holding OP units retains the deferral. Converting to shares or cash generally recognizes the deferred gain. The investor can hold units, earn distributions, and defer the tax; convert strategically when liquidity is needed and tax is acceptable; or, subject to current law, hold until death and seek the step-up. Timing the conversion is therefore part of the capital decision.

## The tax deferral

Section 721 defers gain at contribution. The property’s basis carries into the OP units as carryover basis, so the deferred gain remains embedded in those units. The tax is postponed rather than removed during the holder’s lifetime.

The deferral continues while the OP units are held. It can be triggered when units are converted to shares or cash, or if the operating partnership disposes of the contributed property in a taxable way, subject to any applicable tax protection. The investor has some timing choice through the decision of when, if ever, to convert.

Like a 1031 exchange, OP units held until death can receive a step-up in basis that may erase deferred gain for heirs under current law. That estate-planning possibility is important, but it is fact-specific and should be confirmed with the investor’s CPA and attorney.

## Key takeaways

- A 721 exchange contributes property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, with gain deferred under Section 721.
- OP units may convert to REIT shares, often 1:1 after a holding period, but conversion generally triggers deferred gain.
- Possible benefits include diversification, a path to liquidity, passive ownership, and estate planning.
- Deferral continues while units are held; a step-up at death can potentially erase deferred gain, similar to a 1031.

## Who a 721 exchange suits

A 721 exchange can suit an owner who wants to leave direct real estate for diversified, passive, and potentially more liquid REIT ownership. It can be relevant for a tired landlord, an owner concentrated in one asset, an investor seeking eventual liquidity, or someone focused on dividing wealth among heirs.

It may be especially useful for estate planning because OP units can be divided among several heirs more simply than a building. The possible step-up adds another reason estate-focused owners consider the structure.

The trade-offs are real. OP units are generally partnership interests, not like-kind real property, so an investor typically cannot complete a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) out of them. The REIT manages the portfolio, so the holder gives up direct control. The 721 is better viewed as a long-term endpoint for an owner comfortable with passive REIT ownership, not as another stop in a series of direct-property exchanges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 721 exchange?

It is a contribution of real estate to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, with gain generally deferred under Section 721. The investor moves from direct property ownership to REIT ownership rather than selling or acquiring another direct property through a 1031.

### How is a 721 exchange different from a 1031 exchange?

A 1031 exchange swaps like-kind real property for other real property and can preserve the ability to exchange again. A 721 exchange contributes property for a partnership interest in a REIT. Both can defer gain, but the 721 generally moves the investor into a one-way, passive REIT structure and can offer diversification and liquidity not present in a single direct property.

### What are OP units?

OP units are operating partnership units: the investor’s interest in the partnership that owns the REIT’s real estate. They represent the contributed property’s value, pay distributions comparable to dividends, and can generally be converted to REIT shares after a holding period.

### Can I convert OP units to REIT shares?

Usually, after a holding period often around a year, depending on the REIT. Conversion is commonly 1:1, although structures differ. The investor may receive shares or, in some cases, cash. Conversion or redemption is generally taxable and triggers deferred gain, so liquidity comes with a tax cost.

### Does a 721 exchange defer taxes like a 1031?

Yes, but under Section 721 rather than Section 1031. Gain is not recognized at contribution and basis carries into the OP units. The deferral continues until taxable conversion, redemption, or another trigger. Held until death, units may receive a step-up that erases deferred gain under current law.

### What are the benefits of converting property to REIT units?

Potential benefits are portfolio diversification, a path to liquidity through convertible units, passive ownership without daily management, and estate planning through divisible units and a possible step-up. These benefits depend on the specific REIT, its terms, and the owner’s circumstances.

### Is a 721 exchange a one-way move?

Generally, yes. OP units are partnership interests rather than like-kind real property, so investors typically cannot 1031 out of them. Returning to direct real estate usually requires a taxable conversion or sale and reinvestment of after-tax proceeds. The investor should treat the exchange as a long-term REIT commitment.

### What is an UPREIT?

An umbrella partnership REIT. It owns real estate through an operating partnership. That structure allows property owners to contribute real estate to the partnership for OP units in a tax-deferred Section 721 transaction.

### Do I get income from OP units?

OP units typically pay distributions comparable to REIT dividends, giving the owner passive income tied to the REIT portfolio. Distributions are not guaranteed and depend on the portfolio, REIT, and governing terms.

### Who should consider a 721 exchange?

Owners ready to move from direct property to diversified, passive, more liquid REIT ownership: tired landlords, concentrated owners, eventual-liquidity seekers, and estate planners. It is less suited to an investor who wants direct control or continued 1031 flexibility.

### Are OP units and REIT shares securities?

Yes. They are securities, so the transaction is offered through a broker-dealer to suitable investors after a suitability review. This is a key distinction from a 1031 into direct real estate.

### How do I start a 721 exchange?

Start by evaluating fit with a securities-licensed professional, CPA, and attorney. If it fits, identify a suitable UPREIT willing to accept the property or use a DST structured for a later 721 exit. The property is valued, contribution documents are negotiated, units are issued, and the investor holds or later converts them as appropriate.

## Glossary

### 721 Exchange

Contributing property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, with gain deferred under Section 721.

### Section 721

The tax-code section that generally defers gain when property is contributed to a partnership for an interest.

### UPREIT

An umbrella partnership REIT that owns real estate through an operating partnership.

### Operating Partnership (OP)

The partnership under a REIT that directly owns the properties.

### OP Units

Operating partnership units received in a 721 exchange and representing the holder’s stake in the partnership.

### REIT Shares

The corporate, often traded ownership interests in the REIT that can be converted from OP units.

### Conversion

Exchanging OP units for REIT shares or cash, generally a taxable event.

### Carryover Basis

The basis carried from contributed property into OP units, embedding deferred gain.

### Tax Deferral

Postponing gain through Section 721 until conversion, disposition, or another taxable trigger.

### Step-Up in Basis

The basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain on OP units under current law.

### Diversification

Exposure to the REIT’s overall portfolio instead of one property.

### Liquidity

The ability to convert units to tradable REIT shares over time, subject to REIT type and terms.

### Distributions

Income paid on OP units, comparable to REIT dividends.

### REIT

A real estate investment trust that owns a portfolio of properties.

### Holding Period

The time before OP units can typically be converted to shares.

### Passive Ownership

REIT ownership that requires no property management by the unit holder.
