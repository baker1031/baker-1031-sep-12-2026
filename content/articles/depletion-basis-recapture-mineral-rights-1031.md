# Depletion, Basis & Recapture in a Mineral Rights 1031 Exchange

I have been thinking about how often a mineral owner discovers the real tax question only after the sale is close. A long run of depletion deductions can feel remote until the basis is nearly gone, a buyer is at the table, and every dollar of boot begins to matter.

First-order thinking sees a 1031 exchange as a way to defer a large sale gain. Second-order thinking follows the low basis into the replacement property, separates depletion from depreciation recapture, and models how even modest boot can change the character and timing of recognized income. Depletion is what makes mineral 1031 exchanges technically tricky. This CPA-level walkthrough covers cost versus percentage depletion, adjusted basis, recapture, and carryover basis when gain is deferred.

## Key Takeaways

Most of what makes a mineral 1031 exchange technically harder than a real estate exchange comes down to depletion. Depletion lets mineral owners recover the value of a depleting asset, but it steadily lowers adjusted basis, enlarging the gain and tax being deferred. It also carries into the replacement property through basis and affects future deductions. Recapture can convert part of gain to ordinary income. Those moving parts are easy for a generalist to mishandle, which is why the mechanics of cost and percentage depletion, basis reduction, 1031 carryover basis, and recapture need coordination.

For how these basis and recapture mechanics fit into a full exchange, see [our in-depth mineral and royalty exchange guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html).

## Cost vs. percentage depletion basics

Depletion comes in two forms, and the distinction matters for basis. Cost depletion recovers actual basis in mineral property over time based on units of production extracted relative to total estimated recoverable reserves. Each year, the owner deducts the portion of basis attributable to that year's production. Cost depletion is limited to basis: it cannot exceed the amount invested, so it tracks recovery of actual cost.

Percentage depletion is different and often more generous. It permits a deduction equal to a fixed percentage of gross income from the property, commonly 15% for oil and gas for qualifying small producers and royalty owners, subject to limitations. Percentage depletion is not limited to basis. It can continue after basis has been fully recovered, which is part of its value: a royalty owner can take 15% of gross income year after year, sheltering income even when basis is zero.

Owners generally take the larger of cost or percentage depletion each year, subject to the rules and limitations, and percentage depletion wins for many royalty owners. The choice and mechanics belong with a CPA. For exchange planning, both forms reduce adjusted basis; percentage depletion reduces it until it reaches zero and then continues as a deduction without making basis negative. That basis reduction creates the larger gain and the carryover-basis issue that make exchanges tricky.

## How depletion lowers your basis

Adjusted basis in a mineral interest starts with cost, or [the stepped-up value if inherited](inherited-mineral-rights-step-up-basis-vs-1031.html), and is reduced over time by depletion claimed. Cost depletion reduces basis dollar for dollar as the investment is recovered. Percentage depletion reduces basis by the amount deducted until basis reaches zero; it can then continue as a deduction, but basis remains zero. A long-held interest can therefore have an adjusted basis that has been ground down to zero.

This is the hidden engine of mineral-sale taxation. Gain on sale is net sale price less adjusted basis, so a near-zero basis means nearly the whole sale price is gain. An owner who paid little, or inherited decades ago and has been depleting since, can find a substantial sale almost entirely taxable gain. Years of depletion deductions that sheltered income have quietly built the gain waiting at sale.

This is why deferral matters and how the exchange works. A low basis makes the gain and its four-layer tax large, giving deferral value. The low basis also carries into the replacement property in a 1031 and shapes future deductions. Depletion's effect on basis connects the size of gain, the value of deferral, and the carryover that follows.

## Carryover basis in a 1031 exchange

The defining tax feature of a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) is that basis carries from the relinquished property into the replacement, preserving deferred gain. The replacement does not receive a fresh, stepped-up basis equal to its purchase price. Instead, the old adjusted basis, with adjustments for any [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html) or additional investment, becomes the new property's starting basis. That carryover defers gain rather than eliminating it: the gain rides along in a low basis.

For minerals, carryover basis and depletion interact in important ways. Exchange a low-basis mineral interest into real estate and the low carryover basis becomes the real estate's basis, producing smaller depreciation deductions than a fresh purchase would. Exchange into other minerals and the low basis carries into them, affecting future cost depletion; percentage depletion, which is based on gross income rather than basis, is less affected. Carryover basis shapes deductions on whatever property replaces the relinquished interest.

The replacement choice has tax dimensions beyond deferral. A low carryover basis means modest basis-driven deductions such as depreciation, a factor in projecting after-tax returns. This does not reduce the value of deferral — keeping a third of the value invested is powerful regardless — but it makes carryover basis a real factor for a CPA comparing replacements and projecting the new asset's tax profile.

## Depreciation and depletion recapture rules

Recapture can convert part of gain into ordinary income or subject it to special rates. That is one reason mineral sales can be taxed more heavily than a simple capital-gains rate implies. Gain attributable to prior deductions — depletion on the mineral interest or depreciation on associated equipment — can be recaptured and taxed differently, often at higher ordinary rates for equipment depreciation or under special rules for depletion-driven gain, rather than entirely at favorable capital-gains rates.

In a fully deferred 1031, recapture defers with the rest of gain; it is not recognized at the exchange. It becomes relevant in two situations. First, cash or other non-like-kind value received as boot can trigger recapture on the recognized portion and sometimes accelerate ordinary-income recapture before capital gain. Second, if the exchange is disallowed or the owner ultimately sells without another exchange, recapture comes due in full. A clean 1031 defers recapture, but boot or failure brings it forward.

For a working interest with equipment, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) is a particular concern. The equipment is non-qualifying personal property carved out and taxed in the exchange, and its recapture is recognized then. For pure royalty and mineral interests, depletion-related gain is the main recapture consideration and defers in a clean exchange. The interplay with boot and carryover-basis mechanics is intricate, which is why mineral exchanges need a CPA who understands them.

## How boot interacts with recapture and basis

Boot — cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange — is where depletion, basis, and recapture tie together. To fully defer, the owner reinvests all proceeds in equal-or-greater-value like-kind property. Boot is recognized gain taxable up to total gain. Mineral interests often have a very low basis, so even a small amount of boot can represent a large share of gain and include recaptured amounts taxed at less favorable rates.

Ordering rules matter. When boot triggers recognition, tax law can require recapture with ordinary-income character to be recognized before capital gain. A partial exchange with boot may therefore produce more ordinary income than an owner expects. Intuition fails here: the same dollar of boot can have sharply different tax consequences depending on the shares of recapture and capital gain. CPA modeling is essential.

Carryover basis is also adjusted for boot and additional investment. Taking boot or adding cash changes the basis carried into the new property and, in turn, future depreciation or depletion. These adjustments are mechanical but intricate. Boot is not merely a little tax now; for a low-basis mineral interest, it interacts with recapture and basis in ways that should be modeled before deciding to take any.

## Coordinating with your CPA

Depletion, basis, and recapture interact closely, so the CPA's role should begin early. Before sale, the CPA establishes adjusted basis by tracking depletion history, estimates gain and the four-layer tax, quantifies equipment recapture for a working interest, and models how carryover basis will affect replacement-property deductions. That pre-sale analysis informs whether and how to exchange and what replacement property fits after-tax goals.

During the exchange, the CPA confirms value and debt matching to avoid unintended boot, models intended boot including recapture ordering, and computes carryover basis correctly. After the exchange, the CPA reports on Form 8824, establishes replacement basis, and sets up the new depreciation or depletion schedule. Each step requires the depletion-driven mechanics that distinguish mineral exchanges from real estate exchanges.

Mineral exchanges are not a place for a generalist or for going it alone. The depletion history, recapture rules, carryover basis, and their interaction with boot are technical issues where an experienced CPA earns the fee. Coordination from the planning stage, rather than raising the exchange at filing time, helps structure the exchange correctly, maximize deferral, and establish the replacement property's tax profile. Depletion makes mineral exchanges tricky; a good CPA helps them go smoothly.

## A worked example of the mechanics

Consider an owner who bought a royalty interest years ago for $50,000 and claimed percentage depletion against steady income. Suppose those deductions reduced adjusted basis to near zero, say $5,000. The interest is now worth $400,000. A straight sale would produce roughly $395,000 of gain, sale price less adjusted basis, most of it caused by years of basis erosion. The four-layer tax could approach or exceed a third of proceeds.

Now consider a 1031 into a real estate DST worth $400,000. The owner reinvests all proceeds, takes no boot, and defers the full gain. The $5,000 carryover basis moves into the DST interest, not a fresh $400,000 basis. Future DST depreciation is based on the low carryover figure, yielding modest basis-driven deductions. The deferred gain, including the depletion-driven portion, remains embedded in that low basis until a future taxable disposition that is not an exchange.

Contrast that with the same owner taking $50,000 of boot. The $50,000 is recognized gain. Because much of the gain reflects recaptured depletion, recapture-ordering rules can cause part of the $50,000 to be taxed as ordinary income instead of at favorable capital-gains rates, a harsher result than one might expect from “just taking a little cash.” The example shows why depletion-driven low basis makes even modest boot consequential and why a CPA should model carryover basis and recapture before any boot decision. The numbers are illustrative; a CPA should run actual figures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is depletion?

Depletion is the deduction that lets mineral owners recover the value of a depleting asset, analogous to depreciation for buildings. It has two forms: cost depletion, recovering actual basis based on production, and percentage depletion, a fixed percentage of gross income, often 15% for qualifying oil and gas owners. Owners generally take the larger each year.

### What's the difference between cost and percentage depletion?

Cost depletion recovers actual basis based on units produced relative to total reserves and is limited to basis. Percentage depletion deducts a fixed percentage of gross income, commonly 15%, and is not limited to basis; it can continue after basis reaches zero, making it especially valuable for royalty owners.

### How does depletion lower my basis?

Adjusted basis starts at cost, or stepped-up value if inherited, and is reduced by depletion claimed. Cost depletion reduces it dollar for dollar. Percentage depletion reduces it until it reaches zero, then continues as a deduction without making it negative. Long-held interests often have basis near zero.

### Why does a low basis matter for an exchange?

Gain is net sale price over adjusted basis. A near-zero basis means nearly the entire sale price is gain and a large gain to defer. The low basis also carries to the replacement property in a 1031 and shapes future deductions. Depletion's basis effect drives both the gain size and carryover mechanics.

### What is carryover basis in a 1031 exchange?

In a 1031, old adjusted basis carries into the replacement, with adjustments for boot or added investment, instead of resetting to the replacement purchase price. That preserves deferred gain: it rides along as a low basis. For minerals, low carryover basis means smaller basis-driven deductions on the replacement.

### How does carryover basis affect my replacement property?

A low carryover basis means modest basis-driven deductions on the replacement: smaller depreciation if exchanged into real estate or limited cost depletion if exchanged into other minerals. Percentage depletion, based on gross income, is less affected. The low basis does not reduce the value of deferral, but a CPA includes it in after-tax-return projections.

### What is depletion recapture?

Depletion recapture is the portion of gain attributable to prior depletion deductions that can be taxed differently than ordinary capital gain. It is part of why mineral sales are taxed more heavily than a simple capital-gains rate implies. In a fully deferred 1031, recapture defers with the rest of gain; boot or a failed exchange triggers it.

### Does recapture get deferred in a 1031?

Yes. In a fully deferred exchange, recapture is not recognized at the exchange; it carries forward with deferred gain. Recapture is triggered on a recognized portion if boot is taken and comes due in full if the exchange is disallowed or the owner later sells without exchanging. A clean 1031 defers it; boot or failure brings it back.

### How does boot interact with recapture?

Boot, cash or non-like-kind value received, is recognized gain. Tax law can require recapture with ordinary-income character before capital gain. Since mineral interests often have very low basis, even small boot can represent large gain and include recaptured amounts at less favorable rates. This deserves careful CPA modeling.

### Does equipment in a working interest trigger recapture?

Yes. Tangible equipment in a working interest is non-qualifying personal property carved out and taxed in the exchange, and its prior depreciation can be recaptured as ordinary income at that time. This makes working-interest exchanges more complex than pure royalty exchanges and requires careful allocation and CPA involvement.

### Will my future depletion change after an exchange?

It can. Exchanging into other minerals means low carryover basis affects future cost depletion, though percentage depletion based on gross income is less affected. Exchanging into real estate changes depletion to depreciation based on carryover basis. The CPA establishes the new schedule based on the replacement property.

### Why do mineral exchanges need a specialized CPA?

The depletion history, recapture rules, carryover basis, and interaction with boot are intricate and easy to mishandle. A generalist can miss percentage-depletion basis effects, recapture ordering with boot, or carryover mechanics. An experienced CPA establishes basis, models tax, avoids unintended boot, and reports the exchange correctly.

### When should I involve my CPA in a mineral exchange?

Involve the CPA at the planning stage, before sale, not at filing time. The CPA establishes adjusted basis, estimates gain and the four-layer tax, quantifies equipment recapture, and models how carryover basis affects replacement options. This analysis informs whether and how to exchange and which replacement best fits after-tax goals.

### Does inheriting minerals reset the basis and depletion?

Inheritance gives a stepped-up basis at the date of death, which can substantially reduce gain on later sale. Depletion begins anew from that stepped-up basis. Whether to exchange or sell inherited minerals depends on post-step-up gain; a CPA can quantify it, and sometimes the step-up makes gain small enough that an exchange is not worthwhile.

### Does the low basis reduce the benefit of exchanging?

No. Low basis is why exchanging is valuable: it means a large gain and a large tax to defer. That low basis carries to the replacement and means modest deductions there, which a CPA includes in projections, but deferring a large gain and keeping roughly a third of the value invested remains powerful regardless of the replacement's deductions.

### Can I take a little boot without much tax on a low-basis interest?

Be careful. With near-zero basis, even small boot is mostly gain, and recapture-ordering rules can tax part of it as ordinary income rather than capital gains. A little cash from a low-basis mineral interest can carry a harsher tax bill than expected. Model intended boot with a CPA before deciding to take it.

### Why is depletion called what makes mineral exchanges tricky?

Depletion does two complicating things: it grinds basis toward zero, enlarging the gain deferred, and that low basis carries into the replacement and shapes future deductions. Add recapture and its interaction with boot, and the moving parts are easy for a generalist to mishandle. That is why mineral exchanges need a CPA who understands depletion mechanics.

## Glossary

### Depletion

The deduction recovering the value of a depleting mineral asset; cost or percentage.

### Cost Depletion

Depletion recovering actual basis based on units produced relative to total reserves; limited to basis.

### Percentage Depletion

Depletion deducting a fixed percentage of gross income, often 15%, not limited to basis.

### Adjusted Basis

Original cost, or stepped-up value, reduced by depletion; a low basis means a larger gain.

### Carryover Basis

The relinquished property's adjusted basis that transfers to the replacement in a 1031, preserving deferred gain.

### Depletion Recapture

Gain on sale attributable to prior depletion, potentially taxed at less favorable rates.

### Depreciation Recapture

Ordinary-income tax on prior depreciation of equipment, recognized on the equipment portion of a sale.

### Boot

Cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange; recognized gain that can trigger recapture.

### Recapture Ordering

Rules that can require recognizing ordinary-income recapture before capital gain when boot is taken.

### Stepped-Up Basis

The reset of basis to fair market value at death, which reduces gain and restarts depletion.

### Form 8824

The IRS form reporting a like-kind exchange and computing deferred gain and carryover basis.

### Gross Income (mineral)

The income base for percentage depletion, independent of basis.

### Working Interest Equipment

Tangible operating assets; non-qualifying personal property whose depreciation is recaptured in an exchange.

### Four-Layer Tax Stack

Capital gains, depletion recapture, NIIT, and state tax — the combined cost a 1031 defers.

### Net Sale Price

Gross sale price minus selling costs; the basis for computing gain.

### 1031 Exchange

A transaction deferring gain on investment real property reinvested into like-kind real property.
