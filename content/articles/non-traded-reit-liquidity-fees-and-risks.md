# Non-Traded REIT Liquidity, Fees & Risks

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing the absence of a daily quote described as stability. It can feel that way. But a price you do not see move every day is not the same thing as an investment without risk.

Non-traded REITs own income-producing real estate and distribute most of their taxable income, but their shares are not listed on a public exchange. They can offer real estate exposure without daily public-market moves. In exchange, investors accept limited liquidity, redemption caps, substantial upfront and ongoing fees, and a periodic NAV that is an estimate rather than a live market price.

First-order thinking focuses on the smoother reported value or the stated yield. Second-order thinking asks when capital can actually be accessed, what fees take out before and during the hold, whether the NAV would hold up in a sale, and whether the structure belongs with the investor’s time horizon. This is educational information, not investment advice. Verify current terms in each offering’s documents.

For a broader look at the structure, see our [in-depth guide to non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## Limited liquidity explained

Limited liquidity is the defining feature. A listed REIT can be bought or sold on an exchange on a trading day at the market price. A non-traded REIT has no continuous public market. An investor generally cannot sell shares at will; the practical routes are the REIT's redemption program, if one is available, or an eventual ['liquidity event' such as a listing, sale, or merger](non-traded-reit-liquidity-events-ipo-merger.html).

That is a structural trade-off, not a drafting footnote. The missing daily market is why the shares do not register public-market volatility in real time. It also means capital is largely committed until the REIT supplies a path out. A non-traded REIT should be treated as a multi-year, illiquid hold, not as a place for money that may be needed on short notice.

## Redemption program caps

Most non-traded REITs offer a share-redemption program. It lets an investor request that the REIT repurchase shares, often at current NAV and sometimes at a discount for a shorter holding period. That is limited, conditional liquidity—not a guaranteed exit.

Programs normally restrict how much can be redeemed in a quarter or year, sometimes as a percentage of net assets. If requests exceed the cap, the REIT can prorate them or leave part of a request queued for a later period. The program may also be modified, suspended, or terminated, and pressure periods are often when investors most want to use it.

Read the [redemption-program terms](non-traded-reit-redemption-programs-explained.html) for the actual caps, pricing, holding-period conditions, and the REIT’s discretion to suspend. Do not plan around a redemption request as if it were a dependable sale order. It may provide some access in normal conditions, then be capped, delayed, or unavailable when conditions turn difficult.

## Upfront & ongoing fees

Fees can materially change the economic result. Upfront costs can include selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, and organization and offering expenses. Historically, upfront loads could consume a meaningful percentage of capital, meaning some money goes to costs before it begins working in real estate.

The drag can continue. Asset-management fees, possible sponsor performance or promote fees, and property-level expenses reduce the income and growth that reach investors over time. The point is not merely that fees exist; it is that they affect both the amount initially invested and the net return through the hold. Compare every charge to the economics of available alternatives.

## Valuation uncertainty

A non-traded REIT’s share value is generally a periodic net asset value, or NAV: an appraisal- and model-based estimate of property and other assets less liabilities, stated per share. A traded REIT has a price continuously set by buyers and sellers. The non-traded REIT instead reports an estimate prepared through its valuation process and appraisers.

That estimate can lag a downturn or recovery. It requires assumptions and judgment, and it may differ from what the properties would fetch in a sale. The smoothness of reported NAV can therefore reflect less-frequent valuation rather than the absence of volatility in the underlying real estate. Treat NAV as an informed estimate, not a guaranteed realizable price.

## Who non-traded REITs suit

The structure can fit an investor who seeks real estate income and exposure without daily public-market movement, can commit capital for years, does not need ready liquidity, and understands the fees, redemption limits, and valuation uncertainty. Non-traded REITs are typically offered to accredited or otherwise suitable investors after a suitability review, so the review should test whether an illiquid, fee-bearing position fits the individual situation.

It is a poor fit for emergency reserves, capital with a near-term purpose, an investor who needs transparent daily pricing, or someone who is not prepared for the cost and liquidity terms. Liquidity needs, time horizon, fee sensitivity, and understanding of the risks are decisive.

## Key Takeaways

- Limited liquidity is the defining feature: shares are not exchange-listed, so investors rely on a capped redemption program or an eventual liquidity event.
- Redemption programs are conditional and can be prorated, suspended, or terminated—often when investors most want out—so they are not a guaranteed exit.
- Fees may be significant upfront, including commissions and offering expenses that reduce invested capital, and ongoing, including management and performance fees that reduce net returns.
- Value is a periodic estimated NAV, not a live market price. Non-traded REITs fit long-term, suitable investors who can accept those trade-offs.

## Non-traded vs. traded REITs

Listed REITs offer daily liquidity, transparent live pricing, generally lower direct costs, and ordinary brokerage-account convenience. Their prices can also move with public-market sentiment every day, even when that sentiment does not map neatly to the value of the underlying real estate.

Non-traded REITs offer reduced visible daily volatility and access to certain private real estate strategies. The exchange is illiquidity, capped redemptions, potentially higher fees, and valuation uncertainty. Neither structure is universally better. One gives liquidity and transparency with market volatility; the other offers a smoother private format with higher costs and less control over the timing of an exit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a non-traded REIT?

A non-traded REIT owns income-producing real estate but does not list its shares on a public exchange. Like other REITs, it must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income, which can support a high stated yield. Its shares do not show daily exchange-price moves; instead, it reports periodic NAV. The trade is real estate exposure without daily public quotes for illiquidity, capped redemption access, fees, and an estimated—not market-set—value. Review each offering’s documents.

### How liquid is a non-traded REIT?

Not very. There is no continuous market, so an investor typically relies on a capped redemption program or a listing, sale, or merger. Programs are conditional and may be modified, suspended, or terminated, particularly during heavy redemption demand or market stress. Plan to hold for years and do not treat the investment as a source of on-demand capital.

### How do redemption programs work?

They allow an investor to request that the REIT repurchase shares, often at NAV and sometimes at a discount during a shorter holding period. Caps can limit the total amount redeemed in a quarter or year. If requests exceed a cap, the REIT may prorate an investor’s request or queue the remainder. The REIT can modify, suspend, or terminate the program. It is a partial, revocable liquidity feature, not a substitute for daily liquidity. Review the caps, pricing, holding-period rules, and suspension authority.

### What fees do non-traded REITs charge?

Upfront costs can include selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, and organization and offering expenses. Ongoing costs can include asset-management fees, sponsor performance or promote fees, and property-level expenses. Upfront fees reduce capital initially working in real estate; ongoing fees reduce income and growth over the hold. Read the full fee table before comparing a stated yield with a low-cost alternative.

### Why is valuing a non-traded REIT uncertain?

The reported value is periodic NAV, based on appraisals and valuation models, rather than a continuous price set by an exchange. NAV can lag a downturn or recovery, reflects assumptions and judgment, and may not equal a sale price. A smooth NAV path can conceal changing property values. It is an estimate, not a guaranteed value.

### Who should invest in a non-traded REIT?

Potentially an investor who wants income and real estate exposure without daily public-market volatility, can commit capital for years, does not need ready liquidity, and accepts the fees, redemption limits, and valuation uncertainty. They are often offered to accredited or otherwise suitable investors after review. They are generally unsuitable for emergency funds, short-horizon capital, investors who need daily pricing, or anyone unable to accept the cost and liquidity restrictions.

### How are non-traded REITs different from traded REITs?

Listed REITs trade daily with live prices, liquidity, generally lower direct costs, and brokerage-account access, but their prices move with public markets. Non-traded REITs have no exchange quote and may offer private real estate access with less visible daily volatility, but investors give up liquidity and accept capped redemptions, fees, and NAV uncertainty. The appropriate choice depends on which trade-offs fit the investor.

### Can I lose money in a non-traded REIT?

Yes. Property values or income can fall, tenants can be lost, leverage can amplify losses, distributions can be reduced or suspended, and a listing, sale, or merger can realize less than the purchase price or stated NAV. Fees compound the problem. Illiquidity can keep an investor in a declining position when redemptions are capped or suspended, while appraisal-based NAV may reveal losses late. Principal, distributions, and value are not guaranteed.

### Are non-traded REIT distributions guaranteed?

No. The **90%** taxable-income distribution rule does not guarantee a particular distribution. Payments depend on the REIT’s income and can be reduced, suspended, or partly funded from sources other than current earnings. A portion may be return of capital—your own money returned, reducing basis—rather than operating income. Review funding sources, including operations, return of capital, and borrowing; a stated yield is not a promise.

### What is a liquidity event for a non-traded REIT?

It is an event that supplies a path out, such as a public listing, a portfolio sale and distribution of proceeds, or a merger or acquisition. Until one occurs, the main exit is the capped and conditional redemption program. Management and the board control timing based on market conditions and strategy; neither the timing nor value realized is certain or controlled by the investor.

### How long should I plan to hold a non-traded REIT?

Typically several years or more. The lack of a market, limited redemptions, possible suspension, multi-year paths to a liquidity event, holding-period requirements, and early-redemption discounts all point to patient capital. If the horizon is short or uncertain, or the capital may be needed soon, a non-traded REIT is likely unsuitable and a liquid vehicle may fit better.

### Do non-traded REITs reduce volatility?

They reduce visible daily volatility because there is no live exchange price and NAV is updated periodically. That can improve the investor experience for someone who dislikes watching public-market moves. It does not remove economic risk: the properties remain exposed to market forces, and appraisal-based NAV can be slow to reflect them. Do not equate smoother reported values with lower fundamental risk.

### Is the high yield on a non-traded REIT worth the trade-offs?

It depends on the investor and a full assessment. The **90%** distribution requirement can support an attractive stated yield, but fees, illiquidity, capped and revocable redemptions, valuation uncertainty, and the possibility of return of capital all matter. A headline yield can be reduced by fees and is not a promise. It may suit a long-term, suitable investor who values income and accepts the structure; for another investor, the cost and liquidity limits may outweigh it.

### Should I read the offering documents carefully?

Yes. The prospectus and related materials set the actual fee schedule, redemption caps, pricing, holding-period requirements, suspension authority, NAV methodology and frequency, distribution policy and funding, leverage, liquidity-event expectations, transfer restrictions, and risk factors. Those terms determine liquidity, costs, and risk more than a sales description or stated yield. A financial professional can help assess suitability, but the documents contain the real terms.

## Glossary

- **Non-Traded REIT:** A REIT whose shares are not listed on a public exchange.
- **Traded REIT:** A REIT listed on an exchange with daily liquidity.
- **Liquidity:** The ability to convert an investment to cash readily.
- **Illiquidity:** The inability to easily sell a non-traded REIT interest.
- **Redemption Program:** A REIT's limited, conditional share-buyback mechanism.
- **Redemption Cap:** A limit on the amount redeemable per period.
- **Proration:** Filling redemption requests partially when over the cap.
- **Liquidity Event:** A listing, sale, or merger providing an exit.
- **Upfront Fees:** Commissions and offering costs reducing invested capital.
- **Ongoing Fees:** Asset-management and performance fees reducing returns.
- **NAV:** Net asset value — the estimated per-share value.
- **Valuation Uncertainty:** Doubt that the estimated NAV reflects true value.
- **Return of Capital:** A distribution returning your own money, reducing basis.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to distribute most taxable income.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds.
- **Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a non-traded REIT fits the investor.
