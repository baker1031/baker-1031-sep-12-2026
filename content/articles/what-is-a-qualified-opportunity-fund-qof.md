# What Is a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF)?

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Opportunity Zone Funds Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how often an Opportunity Zone fund gets treated as a tax label rather than a living investment structure. It is both an investment vehicle and a compliance machine. First-order thinking sees a potential 10-year exclusion. Second-order thinking asks whether the vehicle can meet its 90% test, what sits beneath it, how long capital will be tied up, and whether the project risk justifies the tax framing.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">A Qualified Opportunity Fund, usually called a QOF or opportunity fund, is the vehicle through which an investor reinvests eligible capital gain in <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">Opportunity Zone</a> property and pursues the associated tax benefits. It connects the gain to the investment, but it also has strict structure, deployment, reporting, suitability, and holding-period requirements. The rules are time-sensitive and continue to evolve under the 2025 OZ 2.0 legislation, so confirm the current rules with your tax advisor.</p>

<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>A Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) is the investment vehicle formed to invest in OZ property and deliver the OZ tax benefits to investors.</li>

<li>Investors access OZs via sponsor-managed QOFs (passive, professional) or their own QOFs (hands-on); most individuals use funds.</li>

<h2 id="definition">QOF definition and purpose</h2>

<p>A QOF is an investment vehicle organized as a corporation, partnership, or an LLC taxed as one, formed to invest in Qualified Opportunity Zone property. It channels capital into zone real estate or businesses and, if the program requirements are met, provides its investors the OZ benefits: deferral and potential tax-free growth after a 10-year hold.</p>

<p>The fund self-certifies by filing the appropriate form with its tax return. It then has to meet the program requirements, most notably holding at least 90% of its assets in OZ property. Investors put their eligible capital gains into the QOF; the QOF deploys the capital into OZ property or businesses. That is why the QOF is central to the structure: it is where capital enters the zones and where the investor's potential benefits attach.</p>

<h2 id="asset-test">The 90% asset test explained</h2>

<p>The <a href="the-opportunity-zone-90-percent-asset-test-deep-dive.html">90% asset test</a> is the core QOF requirement. At least 90% of a QOF's assets must be Qualified Opportunity Zone property. The test is measured periodically, generally at two points each year, and a shortfall can trigger penalties. A QOF therefore has to keep the bulk of its assets in qualifying zone property, not merely describe itself as an OZ fund.</p>

<p>Qualifying property can include OZ business property, such as tangible property used in a zone and developed or improved there, as well as OZ stock and OZ partnership interests in zone businesses. The rules include useful but technical timing relief. A <a href="the-opportunity-zone-working-capital-safe-harbor.html">working-capital safe harbor</a> can give funds time to deploy capital into projects, which matters when construction or substantial improvement takes time. It does not remove the test; it provides structured flexibility around deployment.</p>

<h2 id="direct">Fund vs. direct OZ investment</h2>

<p>An investor can enter the program through a sponsor-managed QOF or by forming a QOF personally. A <a href="opportunity-zone-funds-explained-complete-investor-guide.html">sponsor-managed QOF</a> is a professionally managed fund, offered as a security, that invests in OZ projects. It offers passive exposure to projects and the related tax benefits without requiring the investor to source, execute, or manage the projects.</p>

<p>A direct QOF means forming an entity and undertaking the OZ project yourself, whether by developing or improving property or operating an OZ business. That route offers control, but it also means sourcing, executing, managing, and complying with the requirements. Most individual investors use sponsor-managed funds; developers and active investors with their own projects may choose the direct route. The right comparison is not fund versus no fund, but passive professional management versus hands-on control and responsibility.</p>

<h2 id="types">Types of QOFs</h2>

<p>Real-estate development QOFs are the most common type. They develop or substantially improve property in Opportunity Zones, through ground-up development or major renovation, with the aim that future appreciation may qualify for the 10-year exclusion. Operating-business QOFs invest in businesses that operate in zones instead of, or in addition to, real estate. They are less common but valid and can have different risk and return profiles.</p>

<p>QOFs also differ by concentration. A single-asset fund is concentrated in one OZ project; a multi-asset fund spreads exposure across multiple projects. Sponsor, strategy, geography, and legal structure can differ as well. Those choices affect the risk and return profile, so type and concentration are part of the investment analysis rather than secondary marketing labels.</p>

<h2 id="invest">How to invest in a QOF</h2>

<p>The process begins with a capital gain from stock, a business, real estate, or another capital asset. The OZ benefits apply to the reinvested gain, and the gain generally has to be invested in a QOF within 180 days of realization. In the common sponsor-managed route, the investor then reviews the fund's strategy, projects, risks, and terms.</p>

<p>Because sponsor-managed QOFs are securities, an investor typically needs to be an <a href="accredited-investor-requirements.html">accredited investor</a> and complete a suitability review through a broker-dealer. If the fund is suitable, the investor invests and holds the QOF interest, ideally for 10 or more years to pursue the tax-free exclusion, while reporting the investment with CPA assistance. The steps sound sequential because they are: identify the gain, meet the 180-day window, qualify, review, invest, hold long term, and report.</p>

<h2 id="risks">Key QOF considerations and risks</h2>

<p>QOFs are long-term and generally illiquid. The 10-year exclusion requires a decade-long hold, and the interest is generally not easily sold before the underlying project resolves. Many funds also carry development risk, including construction, lease-up, and market risks. The underlying projects can underperform or fail.</p>

<p>They are securities, so the investment can lose value. They typically require accredited-investor status and involve sponsor and management fees. The tax benefits also depend on the fund and investment meeting program requirements, including the 90% test and substantial-improvement rules, under rules that are time-sensitive and evolving. The deferred gain is recognized at a set date, creating a future tax bill. Those risks, fees, and the long holding period have to be weighed alongside the potential benefits.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq">
<details open="">
<summary>What is a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF)?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A Qualified Opportunity Fund is an investment vehicle — organized as a corporation or partnership (or an LLC taxed as one) — formed to invest in Qualified Opportunity Zone property (OZ real estate or businesses). Its purpose is to channel investment capital into Opportunity Zones and deliver the OZ tax benefits (deferral and tax-free growth after a 10-year hold) to its investors. The QOF self-certifies (by filing the appropriate form) and must meet the program's requirements (notably the 90% asset test). Investors reinvest their capital gains into the QOF, and the QOF deploys that capital into OZ property/businesses. So the QOF is the central vehicle for OZ investing — the conduit for both the capital and the benefits.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What is the 90% asset test?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>The 90% asset test is a core QOF requirement: a QOF must hold at least 90% of its assets in Qualified Opportunity Zone property (OZ business property, OZ stock, or OZ partnership interests). This ensures the fund genuinely invests in the zones. The test is measured periodically (generally at two points each year), and falling short can trigger penalties. There are nuances (like a working-capital safe harbor giving funds time to deploy capital into development projects). So the 90% asset test requires the QOF to keep the bulk of its assets in qualifying OZ property — the core requirement making it a genuine OZ vehicle. It ensures the capital actually reaches the zones.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I form my own QOF, or do I invest in a fund?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Both are possible. You can invest in a sponsor-managed QOF (a professionally-managed fund offered as a security) — the passive route most individual investors use, getting OZ exposure without managing projects. Or you can form your own QOF (creating your own entity and undertaking your own OZ project) — the hands-on route, giving control but requiring you to source, execute, and manage the project and meet the QOF requirements yourself. So most individual investors use sponsor-managed funds (passive, professional, diversified), while developers or active investors with their own projects may form their own QOFs. The choice depends on whether you want passive fund exposure or to undertake your own OZ project.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What types of QOFs are there?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Several types: real estate development QOFs (the most common — investing in developing or substantially improving OZ real estate), operating business QOFs (investing in operating businesses located in zones), and single-asset vs. multi-asset QOFs (concentrated in one project vs. diversified across several). QOFs also vary by sponsor, strategy, geography, and structure. So when evaluating QOFs, consider the type (real estate vs. business), the concentration (single vs. multi-asset), and the specific strategy — these affect the risk/return profile. The most common QOFs are real estate development funds, aiming for the appreciation the 10-year exclusion can make tax-free, but other types exist for different objectives.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>How do I invest in a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>First, have a capital gain to reinvest (from selling stock, a business, real estate, etc.). Second, within 180 days of realizing the gain, invest the gain amount into a QOF (typically a sponsor-managed fund). Third, because sponsor-managed QOFs are securities, you typically need accredited-investor status and go through a suitability review (the fund is offered through a broker-dealer); review the offering documents and, if suitable, invest. Fourth, hold the QOF investment (ideally 10+ years for the tax-free exclusion), earning the benefits, and report it on your taxes (with your CPA). So the process connects your gain to the OZ benefits via a suitable fund within the 180-day window.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Do I need to be an accredited investor to invest in a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>For sponsor-managed QOFs offered as securities, typically yes — they're generally offered to accredited investors (meeting income or net-worth thresholds) through a broker-dealer, with a suitability review. So most QOF investments require accredited-investor status. (If you form your own QOF for your own project, the accreditation requirement is about accessing securities offerings, which may not apply to your own entity — but that's the hands-on route.) So for the typical route (investing in a sponsor-managed fund), you generally need to be accredited. Confirm the specific fund's requirements; most OZ fund offerings are limited to accredited investors via the securities framework.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>How long do I have to invest my gain in a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>180 days from realizing the capital gain. So you have a 180-day window from the date you realize a gain to invest it into a QOF and earn the OZ benefits. For some pass-through gains (from partnerships, S corps, etc.), the 180-day window can start at different dates (offering flexibility on timing). Missing the window means you can't get the OZ benefits for that gain. So timing matters — reinvest within 180 days. This is more flexible than a 1031 (no separate 45-day identification deadline); you simply invest the gain into a QOF within 180 days. Track the deadline carefully to preserve the OZ benefits for your gain.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What are the risks of investing in a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>QOF investments are long-term (the 10-year exclusion requires a decade-long hold) and illiquid (generally not easily sold before the project resolves). Many involve development risk (construction, lease-up, and market risks). They're securities (carrying the risk of loss), require accredited status, and involve fees. The OZ tax benefits depend on the fund meeting the program's requirements and on the evolving rules (compliance and rule-change risk), and the deferred gain is recognized at a set date (a future tax bill). So QOF investing carries real risks (the long hold, illiquidity, development and securities risk, fees, evolving rules) to weigh against the tax benefits. Understand these risks before investing.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What happens if the QOF fails the 90% asset test?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>If a QOF fails to meet the 90% asset test (holding less than 90% of assets in qualifying OZ property at a measurement date), it can face penalties (a monthly penalty based on the shortfall). Persistent failure could jeopardize the fund's QOF status (and thus the OZ benefits). The working-capital safe harbor gives funds time to deploy capital (so holding cash temporarily for a development project can be acceptable). So the 90% test is enforced with penalties, and a well-run QOF manages its assets to meet it. As an investor, you rely on the fund's management to maintain compliance — a reason to evaluate the sponsor's track record and the fund's structure. Compliance with the 90% test protects the OZ benefits.</p></div>
</details>

<summary>What is 'substantial improvement' for OZ real estate?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>For a QOF developing existing OZ real estate, the substantial improvement requirement generally means the fund must improve the property by at least an amount equal to its basis in the building (roughly, double the investment in the building) within a set period — ensuring the fund genuinely develops or rehabilitates the property (not just holds it). For raw land or new construction, different rules apply. So substantial improvement is a core OZ requirement ensuring real development activity in the zone (the program's economic-development goal). As an investor, you rely on the fund to meet this requirement; it's part of how the QOF qualifies its assets under the 90% test. The specifics are technical — your CPA and the fund's structure address them, and the rules continue under the permanent program.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I move my investment between QOFs?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>In some cases, yes — if you sell or exit one QOF investment before the recognition date, reinvesting the proceeds into another QOF within the applicable window can continue the OZ treatment (a QOF-to-QOF move), though the rules are technical and an exit can be an inclusion event triggering the deferred gain. So moving between QOFs is possible but requires care to preserve the benefits (and may affect the 10-year clock). So don't assume you can freely move between funds without tax consequences — consult your CPA before exiting or reinvesting, as the timing and mechanics affect whether the OZ benefits continue. The 10-year exclusion generally requires a continuous hold, so moving funds can complicate it. Plan QOF changes carefully with professional guidance.</p></div>
</details>

I am managing QOF exposure by matching the hold period, liquidity needs, development risk, and compliance burden to the benefit before committing capital. What would you need to see in a fund's structure before the tax benefit felt earned rather than assumed?
