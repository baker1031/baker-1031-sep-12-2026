# Private REIT vs. Syndication vs. DST

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** REITs  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **15 min read**

I keep hearing these structures described as interchangeable passive real estate. They are not. The wrapper decides whether an investor owns breadth, a single deal, or a tax-deferred interest in specific property.

A private REIT, a syndication, and a Delaware Statutory Trust can all let an investor own real estate without managing it. They differ materially in diversification, information, control, liquidity, tax treatment, fees, and the life of the investment. The right choice begins with the job: diversified cash investment, conviction in a particular deal, or a need to defer a gain through a [1031](1031-exchange-guide.html) exchange.

First-order thinking is to choose the most familiar label or the highest stated return. Second-order thinking asks what property is owned, which risk is concentrated, whether a gain must be deferred, and how capital returns. These are educational comparisons, not investment, legal, or tax advice.

## Key Takeaways

- A private REIT is a diversified portfolio company; a syndication is usually a single-deal partnership; a DST is a fractional interest in specific property that qualifies for a 1031 exchange.
- Only a DST is directly 1031-eligible. REIT shares are securities and most syndication interests are partnership interests, neither of which is like-kind real property.
- A REIT diversifies, a syndication concentrates, and a DST usually owns one property per offering but can be diversified across several offerings.
- A syndication commonly offers the greatest view into a specific asset; a REIT and a DST demand the least operational involvement and show less about any individual building.
- All three are illiquid and generally require capital that can remain invested for years. Lifespan and redemption terms must be read deal by deal.
- Match the structure to the work: diversification points to a REIT, a specific property to a syndication, and real-estate gain deferral to a DST.

## Three shapes of passive ownership

A private REIT is a company that owns a diversified portfolio. The investor buys shares and owns a slice of the entire portfolio, often across sectors and markets. A syndication is usually a partnership or LLC created to buy one property or a small group of properties; the sponsor manages it and passive investors own limited-partner interests. A DST is a trust holding particular property that sells fractional interests. It is structured to satisfy the IRS conditions for 1031 replacement property.

All three are passive and pooled, but the container controls everything downstream: how many buildings are owned, how much detail an investor can review, whether a gain can be deferred, and when capital may return. None is inherently superior. Each was built for a different purpose.

## Diversification versus concentration

A private REIT is the most diversified by design. It spreads capital across many properties, sectors, and markets, with professional allocation across the cycle. That reduces the impact of a single failed asset, but it also means an investor does not get the full benefit of one outstanding property.

A syndication is usually a concentrated view on one asset and one business plan. That creates more idiosyncratic risk and the possibility of greater upside from a well-chosen property, but it also gives the investor a clearer, targeted thesis to evaluate before committing. The full investment remains exposed if that property, local market, debt structure, or sponsor plan underperforms.

A DST lies between the two. One DST offering is commonly a single property or small portfolio, so it is concentrated on its own. A 1031 investor can assemble diversification by owning several DSTs across property types and markets. A REIT fits a primary need for broad diversification; a syndication fits a desire to underwrite a particular asset; a DST fits gain deferral and a build-your-own set of interests.

## Control and information

None of the three gives operational control. The difference is information and proximity. In a syndication, an investor is often closest to the specific asset. The operating agreement defines limited-partner rights, and the sponsor may provide detailed reporting and periodic calls. That can suit someone who wants to follow an individual building.

A private REIT is more arm’s-length. An investor underwrites the manager and portfolio strategy and receives aggregate reporting, rather than detailed review of every property. A DST is the most passive by rule. The IRS requirements in Revenue Ruling 2004-86 restrict active trust actions in order to preserve 1031 treatment; an investor has essentially no operating say.

Due diligence follows the same shape. Underwrite a syndication’s local market, rent roll, business plan, debt, and sponsor record. With a private REIT, underwrite management and portfolio strategy. With a DST, examine the specific property and sponsor but recognize the static nature of the trust.

## The decisive tax difference: 1031 eligibility

For proceeds from a real-estate sale, this can settle the choice. Only a DST is 1031 replacement property. A qualifying DST exchange can defer capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, and the net investment income surtax, while continuing the 1031 chain when the DST property sells. REIT shares are securities, and most syndication interests are partnership interests, so they are not like-kind real property.

The direct comparison is covered in [DST vs. REIT](dst-vs-reit.html). There can be a later [721 exchange](1031-to-721-exchange.html), where DST property is contributed to a REIT operating partnership for units, but that follows the 1031 rather than replacing it. For new cash without a gain to defer, 1031 eligibility does not decide among the structures.

## Liquidity, minimums, and lifespan

All three are illiquid relative to publicly traded stock. A private REIT is an ongoing entity and may offer a limited redemption program that is conditional, capped, and subject to gating during stress. A syndication is normally finite and returns capital when its property is sold, often after a few years or more, with little or no interim liquidity. A DST is also finite, ending at a sponsor’s full-cycle sale, often **5–10 years** out, with no redemption mechanism during the hold. None is a place for money that may be needed soon.

Minimums vary. Private REITs and syndications often require tens of thousands of dollars. DST minimums for 1031 investors often run roughly **$25,000 to $100,000**—also expressed as **$25 to $100 thousand**—while cash investors can face higher floors. In every case, read the specific liquidity terms and prepare to commit capital for years.

## Risk and return profiles

Diversification drives much of the risk-return distinction. A diversified private REIT reduces the chance that a single property creates a catastrophic result, while also blending away the upside from a single winner. A single-asset syndication is sharper in both directions; a strong value-add plan can perform well, but there is no portfolio to cushion a weak property.

A DST is commonly based on stabilized, leased property, which can favor current income and relative stability over aggressive appreciation. It still bears interest-rate and property-market risk. None of these is low risk. They are illiquid private placements, generally sold to accredited investors, and principal can be lost. The honest comparison is among different risk profiles, not safe versus risky options; a DST’s tax benefit or a REIT’s diversification never replaces underwriting the underlying real estate. Read the private placement memorandum and weigh the sponsor in every case.

## Fees and how each pays you

A private REIT may charge ongoing asset-management fees and may also have selling commissions, acquisition fees, and a performance promote. It generally pays regular portfolio-income distributions reported on Form 1099-DIV. Payments can include ordinary income, capital gain, and return of capital; the ordinary component can generally be eligible for the permanent **20%** Section 199A deduction. See [REIT dividend taxation](private-reit-dividends-tax.html) for the mechanics.

A syndication normally pays a preferred return to limited partners before splitting profits above a hurdle through a promote or carried interest. It reports on a Schedule K-1 and passes through depreciation that can shelter some income. A DST has sponsor and offering fees built into the deal, commonly pays monthly rental-income distributions, and reports on a 1099 with a grantor letter to preserve its grantor-trust structure. In all three, compare results net of every fee and tax feature, ask how the sponsor is compensated, and examine the incentive structure because it says much about how the deal will be run.

## Using more than one

These structures can coexist. A common sequence uses a DST to defer a real-estate gain, then later pursues a 721 exchange into a REIT operating partnership for broader diversification and a possible path to liquidity. See the [1031-to-721](1031-to-721-exchange.html) guide. An investor with sale proceeds and fresh cash might use a DST for the gain and a private REIT for the cash.

Another investor may hold a diversified private REIT as a core, one or more syndications for concentrated opportunities that were independently underwritten, and a DST when an exchange is required. The decision need not be permanent or exclusive. Start with the gain, desired diversification, information needs, control expectations, and liquidity tolerance.

## Which structure fits your goal

Choose a private REIT when broad diversification is the priority, the capital is new cash rather than 1031 proceeds, and limited redemption access is acceptable. Choose a syndication when backing a particular property or sponsor, seeing the single-asset details, and accepting concentrated risk are the priorities. Choose a DST when completing a 1031 exchange and needing a passive qualifying replacement property.

Three profiles make the contrast clear: a cash investor seeking income without thinking about one building may lean to a REIT; an experienced investor who has studied a specific apartment value-add plan may lean to a syndication; a landlord selling a fully depreciated rental to defer a six-figure gain and stop managing tenants may lean to a DST. The structures are tools for different situations, not direct substitutes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a private REIT and a syndication?

A private REIT is a diversified portfolio company, so its shares spread capital across many properties. A syndication is normally a partnership formed for one property, with a sponsor as general partner and passive investors as limited partners. The REIT diversifies; the syndication concentrates in one deal.

### Can I use a 1031 exchange with a private REIT or syndication?

Generally no. REIT shares are securities and most syndication interests are partnership interests, not like-kind real property. Of the three structures, a DST is the qualifying 1031 replacement property and can defer a real-estate gain directly.

### Which is most diversified?

A private REIT, which may own many properties in multiple markets and sectors. A syndication is usually the most concentrated because it owns one asset. A DST often holds one property per offering, although investors can diversify across several DSTs.

### Which gives me the most insight into the deal?

Usually a syndication. It offers proximity to the property, detailed information, and defined limited-partner rights. A private REIT reports portfolio results, while a DST is most passive by rule and has IRS restrictions that preserve its static 1031 form.

### How liquid are these structures?

All are illiquid. A private REIT may offer capped redemptions that can be gated; a syndication commonly returns capital when its property sells after a few years; a DST normally ends at a sponsor full-cycle sale, often **5–10 years** away. Plan to leave the money committed for years.

### Which is riskiest?

A syndication has the most concentrated risk because the result rides on one property and principal can be fully lost if it fails. A private REIT spreads risk across many assets, and a DST is often stabilized income property. None is low risk; each is an illiquid private placement that can lose principal.

### How do I choose among the three?

Begin with the purpose: private REIT for broad diversification with new cash, syndication for a specific deal that can be underwritten, DST for a qualifying passive 1031 replacement. Determine first whether there is a gain to defer, because that can eliminate two choices immediately. For the next operational step, see [how to invest in a private REIT](how-to-invest-in-a-private-reit.html).

## Glossary

- **Private REIT:** A Regulation D REIT owning a diversified portfolio of properties, offered to verified accredited investors.
- **Syndication:** A partnership or LLC, usually formed to acquire a single property, with a sponsor as general partner and passive limited-partner investors.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding specific property that sells fractional interests and qualifies as 1031 replacement property.
- **Limited Partner:** A passive investor in a syndication with defined rights spelled out in the operating agreement and no operational control.
- **General Partner (Sponsor):** The party that organizes and manages a syndication, sources the deal, and operates the property on behalf of the limited partners.
- **1031 Eligibility:** Whether an investment qualifies as like-kind replacement property for a tax-deferred exchange; true for a DST, generally not for a REIT or syndication.
- **Full-Cycle Sale:** The point at which a DST's or syndication's property is sold and proceeds, plus any gain, are returned to investors.
- **Redemption Program:** A private REIT's mechanism for buying back shares, typically capped per period and subject to suspension under stress.
