# Donating OP Units: 721 Exchanges and Charitable Giving

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I sometimes see charitable intent and tax intent arrive at the same point: an OP-unit holder wants to support a cause, yet also recognizes that converting or selling appreciated units can trigger substantial deferred gain. Donating the units may appear to offer a direct answer.

It can be a valid planning question, but it is not as simple as donating appreciated stock. A long-term gift of [OP units](op-units-explained.html) may potentially avoid the donor’s recognition of embedded deferred gain and support a charitable deduction. OP units, however, are partnership interests rather than corporate stock. That feature can pass unrelated business taxable income, or UBTI, to a charity and can make a charity reluctant to accept the gift.

First-order thinking focuses on avoiding gain and obtaining a deduction. Second-order thinking asks whether the recipient can accept and manage the partnership interest, whether the gift belongs in the donor’s larger estate, income, and charitable plan, and whether the donor is genuinely prepared to give the value away. This is highly technical planning that requires experienced tax and estate professionals. This article provides an educational framework, not tax, legal, or charitable-planning advice.

## The appeal of giving appreciated OP units

Like other appreciated assets, OP units can potentially be used for charitable giving. The general appeal of giving appreciated property rather than cash is that the donor may avoid recognizing embedded gain while receiving a charitable deduction, subject to rules governing the asset type and the donor’s facts.

An OP unit can carry deferred gain from a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), as well as later appreciation. Converting or selling the units can trigger that gain. A charitable gift of the units, rather than a conversion or sale, may avoid the donor’s recognition of the gain in a way that a sale would not. The charity receives the units, and the donor may support the charitable purpose with their value.

This resembles the familiar idea of donating appreciated stock instead of selling stock and donating cash. Stock donations can avoid [capital-gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html) and create a deduction. But the analogy is limited. An OP unit is a partnership interest, and that legal character changes the administration and tax analysis.

The potential combination of gain avoidance and a charitable deduction is appealing. The specific treatment of gain and the deduction for a partnership interest must be analyzed by the donor’s advisers. It cannot be assumed from the rules that apply to publicly traded corporate stock.

> Donating appreciated OP units may avoid the donor’s recognition of embedded deferred gain that conversion or sale could trigger and may produce a charitable deduction. The partnership-interest analysis is technical and cannot be treated like a routine stock donation.

## Avoiding the embedded gain: potential, not automatic

The principal potential tax benefit arises because OP units contain embedded deferred gain. That is generally the gain deferred in the original 721 exchange plus any appreciation. If an owner [converts or sells the units](tax-consequences-of-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html), the transaction can trigger the gain.

Giving the units to charity instead may avoid the donor’s recognition of that gain, while allowing a charitable deduction for the gift’s value, subject to the deduction rules. The donor supports a charity with the value of the asset rather than first selling the asset, paying tax, and donating the remaining cash.

The qualification is critical. Tax treatment of a gift of a partnership interest is technical. Its value, holding period, gain treatment, deduction rules, the charity’s status, and the surrounding transaction facts all matter. The potential benefit is not a shortcut for an owner who simply wants to avoid conversion tax. It is a charitable strategy in which the owner gives the asset’s value away and needs appropriate structuring.

## Why OP units create a UBTI complication

The central difference from stock is unrelated business taxable income. When a tax-exempt charity holds a partnership interest, partnership income can flow through to the charity as UBTI. UBTI is income on which even a tax-exempt organization may owe tax when the income arises from an unrelated business or debt-financed property.

This can be especially relevant to real estate because a partnership may own debt-financed property. If a charity receives OP units, its share of partnership income may create UBTI and associated tax and administrative obligations.

Corporate stock does not ordinarily create this same issue: dividends and gains on stock are not UBTI in the same manner. A partnership interest can. This is why a direct charity recipient may be reluctant to accept OP units even if it enthusiastically accepts stock or cash.

The UBTI question does not automatically prevent a gift. It does mean the charity’s willingness, capacity, tax position, and handling arrangements must be confirmed. The partnership-interest nature of OP units is the central reason that charitable giving with them requires more planning than a typical appreciated-stock gift.

## Charitable remainder trusts

A charitable remainder trust, or CRT, is one structure sometimes considered for appreciated assets. It is an irrevocable trust to which a donor contributes property. The trust pays an income stream to the donor or other beneficiaries for a term, and the remainder goes to charity.

For appreciated assets, a CRT can defer or avoid immediate gain when the trust sells the asset, provide an income stream, and generate a charitable deduction for the remainder interest. A CRT may therefore be considered when a donor wants both charitable giving and continued income.

For OP units, it may offer a possible structure for holding the units and managing income. It does not eliminate the key difficulty. A CRT that holds a partnership interest can still face UBTI, potentially with significant consequences. The donor and advisers should analyze the UBTI and partnership-interest effects within the CRT itself rather than assuming the CRT solves them.

A CRT is one possible approach, not a default. Its suitability depends on the donor’s charitable objectives, income needs, estate plan, and the specific facts of the OP units. Sophisticated estate and tax planning is required.

## Donor-advised funds and direct gifts

A donor-advised fund, or DAF, is an account at a sponsoring charity. A donor contributes assets, receives a deduction, and may recommend grants to charities over time. A DAF can be useful for appreciated assets, but a DAF receiving OP units faces the same partnership-interest and UBTI issues.

Some DAFs may be reluctant to accept OP units or may require special handling. The donor needs to ask whether the particular sponsoring organization can accept and manage the interest. A DAF is a possible vehicle, not an assumption that removes the complexity.

A direct gift gives the OP units to the charity itself. This places the UBTI and administrative questions squarely with the charity. The charity must be willing to accept the units and able to handle any resulting partnership-income reporting. Many charities may decline a direct gift of OP units for those reasons.

The possible vehicles—CRT, DAF, direct gift, or another structure—share the same foundational issue. The recipient or structure has to be able to accept and manage the partnership interest. The fact that the donor wants to make a gift does not require a charity to take an asset that does not fit its administrative capacity.

## Integrate the gift with the full plan

A decision to give OP units should fit the donor’s broader charitable, estate, tax, and income plan. It is not a stand-alone transaction. The donor should consider charitable goals, estate objectives, income needs, tax position, other available assets, and the potential role of the OP units in a step-up-at-death strategy.

The professional team should include an experienced estate attorney, a CPA, and, where useful, a charitable-giving specialist. The estate attorney addresses gift structures, bequests, trusts, and estate integration. The CPA analyzes gain, UBTI, deduction treatment, and other tax effects. A charitable specialist may help match the asset to a suitable recipient or vehicle.

The team should also compare donating OP units with other choices. Appreciated stock or other assets may be much simpler to donate. An owner may prefer to give simpler assets to charity and retain OP units for income and a possible step-up, or decide that a properly structured OP-unit gift better serves a particularly important charitable goal. Lifetime giving and giving through an estate plan at death have different consequences. The appropriate choice depends on the whole plan.

## Key Takeaways

- A gift of appreciated OP units may potentially avoid the donor’s recognition of embedded deferred gain and may support a charitable deduction.
- OP units are partnership interests, not stock. Their partnership income can create UBTI for a charity, particularly where real estate is debt-financed.
- CRTs, DAFs, and direct gifts are possible vehicles, but none automatically removes the partnership-interest and UBTI issues.
- The gift must be coordinated with the donor’s estate, tax, charitable, and income plan by an experienced estate attorney, CPA, and, if needed, charitable-giving specialist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I donate OP units to charity?

Potentially. A donation of appreciated OP units may avoid the embedded deferred gain that a sale could trigger and may provide a charitable deduction. But OP units are partnership interests, not corporate stock, and can create UBTI for a charity. The gift is therefore more complex than donating appreciated stock and requires experienced tax and estate professionals.

### What is the benefit of donating OP units instead of selling them?

The potential benefit is avoiding the donor’s recognition of embedded deferred gain while supporting a charitable purpose with the asset’s value and receiving a possible charitable deduction. A conversion or sale could trigger the gain. The specific benefit depends on the partnership-interest rules, valuation, deduction rules, and structure. The gift must be motivated by charitable intent; it transfers the value to charity, not merely the tax obligation.

### What is UBTI, and why does it matter for OP units?

UBTI is unrelated business taxable income, which can be taxable even to a tax-exempt organization. A charity holding an OP unit is holding a partnership interest, and partnership income can flow through to it as UBTI, especially if partnership real estate is debt-financed. The prospect of tax and administration may make a charity reluctant to accept the units. This distinguishes an OP-unit gift from a typical stock donation.

### Will a charity accept my OP units?

It depends on the charity. Many may be reluctant because partnership interests can create UBTI and administrative burdens. Some charities or specialized structures may be able to accept and manage them. The donor must confirm the recipient’s willingness and capacity before planning the gift. If no suitable recipient exists, donating other assets may be simpler.

### What is a charitable remainder trust?

A CRT is an irrevocable trust funded with appreciated assets. It pays an income stream to the donor or other beneficiaries for a term, with the remainder going to charity. It can defer or avoid immediate gain on a trust sale, provide income, and create a charitable deduction for the remainder value. A CRT may be considered for OP units, but it can still face UBTI and partnership-interest issues with significant consequences. It requires careful planning.

### Can I use a donor-advised fund for OP units?

Possibly. A DAF lets a donor contribute assets, receive a deduction, and recommend grants over time. However, a DAF receiving OP units faces the same UBTI and partnership-interest concerns. Some DAFs may decline the units or require special handling. The donor should confirm whether the sponsoring charity can accept and administer the interest.

### Is donating OP units like donating stock?

The tax concept can be similar: donating an appreciated asset may avoid gain and produce a deduction. The execution is very different. Charities commonly accept stock, and stock does not create UBTI in the same way. OP units are partnership interests and can create UBTI and administrative burdens. Donating other appreciated assets may be far simpler when simple charitable giving is the goal.

### Should I donate OP units or other assets to charity?

It depends on the charitable and tax objectives. Appreciated stock and other assets may be simpler because they do not bring the same partnership-interest and UBTI issues. An owner may choose to donate those assets and retain OP units for income and a potential step-up. If the OP units carry a large embedded gain and the charitable objective supports a gift, they may still be considered with proper structuring. Advisors should compare the choices.

### Do I need professionals to donate OP units?

Yes. This is not a do-it-yourself gift. An experienced estate attorney should address the estate structure and integration; a CPA should analyze UBTI, gain, and deduction treatment; and a charitable-giving specialist may be useful. The partnership-interest complexities can have significant consequences if handled improperly.

### Can I leave OP units to charity in my will?

Yes. OP units can be bequeathed to a charity through an estate plan and may support an estate-tax charitable deduction that reduces the taxable estate. The recipient still faces the partnership-interest and UBTI considerations. An estate attorney should structure the bequest around the charity’s ability to handle the units, including whether conversion is part of the bequest.

### Is donating during life or at death better for OP units?

It depends on the donor’s goals. Lifetime giving may provide an income-tax charitable deduction and may avoid gain. A charitable bequest may provide an estate-tax deduction and lets the donor keep unit income during life. UBTI and partnership-interest issues apply either way. Some owners hold units for income and a possible step-up for non-charitable heirs while donating other assets or using a charitable bequest. The decision belongs in the broader estate and charitable plan.

### Could a charitable strategy avoid conversion tax?

Potentially, in concept. Donating appreciated units rather than converting and selling may avoid the donor’s recognition of tax on the donated units and may provide a deduction. The strategy must be a genuine charitable gift: the donor gives the asset value to charity. UBTI, charity acceptance, and partnership-interest mechanics still require proper structure. It is not a simple tax-avoidance device.

## Glossary

**Charitable Giving:** Donating assets, including OP units, to support a charity with possible tax effects.

**Appreciated Asset:** An asset with embedded gain, such as an OP unit.

**Embedded Gain:** Deferred gain and later appreciation contained in OP units.

**Charitable Deduction:** Potential tax deduction for a qualifying charitable gift.

**Partnership Interest:** The legal character of OP units, adding complexity to a donation.

**UBTI:** Unrelated business taxable income that can be taxable to a charity holding a partnership interest.

**Debt-Financed Property:** Real estate with debt, which can be a source of UBTI.

**Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT):** A trust that provides income for a term and a charitable remainder.

**Donor-Advised Fund (DAF):** A charitable account that accepts contributions and makes grants over time.

**Direct Gift:** A direct transfer of units to a charity, exposing the recipient to the partnership-interest issues.

**Tax-Exempt Charity:** A charitable recipient that may still owe UBTI tax on partnership income.

**Gain Avoidance:** Potential avoidance of gain recognition by donating rather than selling.

**Income Stream:** Payments a CRT can make to a donor or beneficiaries.

**Estate Integration:** Coordinating the charitable gift with the estate plan.

**Charitable Specialist:** A professional who advises on sophisticated charitable giving.

**Deduction Rules:** Rules that govern deduction treatment for a gift of a particular asset type.
