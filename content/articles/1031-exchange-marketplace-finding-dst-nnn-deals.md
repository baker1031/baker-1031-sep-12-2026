# 1031 Exchange Marketplace: Finding DST & NNN Deals

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing the 45-day identification period turn a deliberate 1031 decision into a deadline problem. An exchanger has six weeks to find, evaluate, and identify replacement property. Missing the window can cause the exchange to fail.

For passive replacements such as Delaware Statutory Trusts and net-lease properties, discovery is its own problem. These offerings are securities and specialized real estate, not a public inventory that an owner can scan on a general property portal. A 1031 marketplace can aggregate screened DST and NNN offerings, allow rapid comparison, and make a tight identification process more orderly. It is a search and comparison tool, not a substitute for diligence.

## What a 1031 marketplace provides

A 1031 marketplace aggregates current replacement offerings, principally DSTs and net-lease properties, so an exchanger can see a range of choices in one place. Its value is aggregation and curation: it brings a fragmented and opaque group of replacement products into one organized view instead of requiring an investor to contact multiple sponsors and brokers before knowing what is available.

The curation matters. A reputable platform screens the sponsors and offerings it presents, reviewing sponsor history, offering structure, fees, and underlying real estate. That initial filter can save time and reduce exposure to offerings that have not met the platform’s standards. It is not an endorsement or a guarantee of quality.

Because DSTs and many NNN offerings are securities, marketplace access is within the securities framework. Offerings are provided through a broker-dealer to [accredited investors](insights.html), with disclosure and suitability requirements. It is not a public free-for-all listing site.

## Browsing DST and NNN offerings efficiently

The useful fields on an offering include property type and location—apartments, industrial, medical office, or [net-lease retail](1031-exchange-for-retail-nnn-owners.html)—the sponsor, minimum investment, projected distribution rate, leverage or LTV, offering size and availability, fees, holding period, and exit strategy.

Those inputs let an exchanger narrow the field. An owner focused on stable income may study stabilized DST and net-lease assets. An owner concerned about debt replacement can filter for LTV that supports debt matching. An owner seeking diversification can look across sectors and markets. An owner should look beyond a headline distribution rate to sponsor quality, fees or load, leverage, real-estate fundamentals, and the proposed holding and exit plan.

First-order thinking sees the highest distribution rate. Second-order thinking asks whether a higher rate is paired with higher leverage, a weaker sponsor, riskier real estate, or a different expected holding period. The marketplace makes the alternatives visible; the investor and adviser still decide what genuinely fits.

## Compare offerings side by side

The strongest use of a marketplace is side-by-side comparison. Distribution rate, LTV, fees, property type, sponsor, investment minimum, and expected holding period can be compared across several offerings at once.

This makes trade-offs easier to see. A higher projected distribution may accompany higher leverage or a less proven sponsor. A lower-fee offering may have less compelling underlying property. The goal is not to name one offering “best” in the abstract. It is to identify the replacement or group of replacements that meets the exchanger’s income, diversification, value, debt, timing, and risk objectives.

The same comparison process supports a diversified replacement portfolio. An investor may select several DSTs across markets and asset types, possibly including net lease, with each serving a particular role. Seeing the building blocks together can make portfolio construction practical inside the identification deadline.

> Side by side, trade-offs become visible: a higher rate may mean higher leverage or a less established sponsor. That is information a one-at-a-time search can obscure.

## Why speed matters in the 45-day window

The time advantage is not merely convenient. In a traditional search, an exchanger may spend much of the 45-day period finding out what exists before meaningful comparison begins. A marketplace compresses discovery by providing aggregated, screened options immediately.

This is especially useful for a backup property. An exchanger pursuing a direct replacement can identify a suitable, fast-closing DST as a fallback. If the primary acquisition stalls, the backup can protect the exchange from failure. A marketplace helps identify that contingency quickly.

Speed also reduces bad decisions made from panic. An owner racing toward day 45 may choose any available replacement. An owner able to compare multiple screened offerings sooner has more time to make a considered selection. The platform does not remove deadline pressure, but it can turn a frantic search into a manageable process.

## Access and preparation

Marketplace access generally begins with the adviser or broker-dealer that provides the offerings. The investor establishes a relationship, confirms accredited-investor status, and undergoes the suitability process required for securities.

The adviser then helps translate the investor’s exchange requirements into the relevant options, compare terms, and review offering documents. The platform supplies access; advice and diligence support the selection.

Preparation should occur before the property sale where possible. An investor who has already established the relationship and completed accreditation can move directly to comparison and identification when the 45-day period starts. Waiting until after the sale gives up part of the speed advantage.

## Ground rules when using a marketplace

Vetting standards differ between platforms. Understand what “curated” actually means and do not treat a listing as a guarantee. The underlying DST or NNN security remains illiquid, subject to sponsor and property risk, and capable of underperforming or losing value.

The platform is a means to find suitable replacements efficiently, not the end itself. Suitability review, offering-document review, and the investor’s own risk evaluation remain necessary. Used with diligence, a marketplace expands and organizes choice; used as a replacement for analysis, it can facilitate a hasty decision.

## Marketplace versus a single sponsor or narrow shelf

A single sponsor can show only its own offerings. A single broker may have a limited shelf. That restricts the investor to what one relationship happens to carry.

A marketplace that aggregates multiple sponsors can provide a wider comparison set and reduce the risk of being steered solely toward one issuer’s inventory. Breadth matters for matching debt, diversification, income needs, and timing. But breadth by itself is not enough: an enormous unfiltered list simply puts the vetting burden back on the investor under time pressure.

The practical advantage is the combination of broad sponsor coverage, curation, and advice. That combination can provide a wider field of screened offerings and help in selecting one that fits the exchange, rather than merely presenting more choices.

## Key Takeaways

- A 1031 marketplace aggregates screened DST and NNN replacement offerings to help with the difficult 45-day search.
- Side-by-side comparison surfaces the real trade-offs among rate, leverage, sponsor, fees, property type, and holding period.
- Speed helps build a fast-closing DST backup and reduces rushed-choice risk, but does not replace diligence.
- Access is through a broker-dealer and adviser for accredited investors; establishing it before sale preserves the time advantage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 1031 exchange marketplace?

It is a curated platform aggregating screened replacement offerings, mainly DSTs and net-lease properties, so an exchanger can discover, compare, and identify them efficiently. It organizes a fragmented field and is particularly useful under the 45-day deadline.

### What offerings are on a marketplace?

Typically DSTs and NNN properties across apartments, industrial, medical office, retail, and other property types. Because they are securities, accredited investors receive them through a broker-dealer with disclosure and suitability requirements.

### How does it help with the 45-day deadline?

It reduces discovery time. Offerings are already aggregated and screened, allowing the exchanger to move sooner to comparison and selection. It can also make a fast-closing DST backup easier to identify if a primary direct deal is uncertain.

### What information can I compare?

Compare property type and location, sponsor, minimum investment, projected distribution rate, LTV, fees, offering size and remaining availability, holding period, and exit approach. Compare rate alongside its leverage, sponsor, and real-estate support.

### Are marketplace offerings vetted?

Reputable platforms screen sponsors and offerings for track record, structure, fees, and underlying real estate. Vetting standards vary and screening is not a guarantee or substitute for the investor’s and adviser’s due diligence.

### Who can access a marketplace?

Generally accredited investors who meet SEC income or net-worth thresholds, since DSTs and many NNN offerings are securities. Access comes through the adviser or broker-dealer, which confirms status and performs suitability review.

### How do I get access?

Establish a relationship with the adviser or broker-dealer, confirm accredited status, and use the adviser to identify and compare offerings that fit. Do this before sale where possible so access is ready when the identification window begins.

### Can I build a diversified portfolio through a marketplace?

Yes. Multiple DSTs across markets and property types, perhaps with a net-lease component, can be selected together. The marketplace lets the investor compare those building blocks within the deadline and give each a role in the portfolio.

### Is a marketplace better than finding deals on my own?

For passive securitized DST and NNN replacements, it is often more efficient because the securities can be difficult to discover and compare individually. It is less relevant for a direct property the investor sources personally. Many exchangers use it for the passive portion or backup of a replacement plan.

### Does it replace diligence?

No. DST and NNN securities remain illiquid and exposed to property and sponsor risk. Curation is a useful initial filter, not a substitute for offering-document review, suitability, and a decision about fit and risk tolerance.

### Should I arrange access before selling?

Yes. Confirming the relationship and accreditation in advance allows immediate comparison and identification at the start of the 45-day window. Waiting until after sale can sacrifice the platform’s time advantage.

### Are marketplace DSTs guaranteed investments?

No. They can underperform or lose value, are illiquid, and depend on underlying real estate and sponsor management. Marketplace access makes them discoverable and comparable; it does not change their investment risks.

### How is it better than a single broker?

A single sponsor has only its own products and a broker may have a narrow shelf. A multi-sponsor marketplace can compare across the field instead of one inventory, reducing a captive-channel conflict and improving the chance of finding a fit.

### Does it compare sponsors?

Yes. Multi-sponsor access allows comparisons based on exchange and investment requirements rather than availability from one firm. Sponsor breadth is most useful when paired with curation and an adviser who can assess the alternatives.

### Is breadth alone enough?

No. More offerings without screening would simply place more vetting work on the investor. The useful combination is breadth, curation, and advice.

## Glossary

**1031 Marketplace:** A curated platform of DST and NNN replacement offerings.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A securitized fractional interest in institutional real estate that can qualify as 1031 replacement property.

**Net-Lease (NNN) Property:** Property where the tenant generally pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

**Curation:** Sponsor and offering screening used to present a vetted selection.

**Accredited Investor:** Investor meeting applicable SEC income or net-worth thresholds.

**Distribution Rate:** Projected income paid by an offering.

**Loan-to-Value (LTV):** Leverage level relevant to debt matching.

**Load:** Fees and selling costs deducted before capital reaches real estate.

**Sponsor:** Firm organizing and managing an offering.

**Holding Period:** Expected DST duration before a full-cycle event.

**Side-by-Side Comparison:** Evaluating offerings together to see rate, leverage, fee, and quality trade-offs.

**Backup Property:** A fast-closing replacement, often a DST, identified to protect an exchange.

**Suitability Review:** Assessment that a security fits an investor.

**Broker-Dealer:** Registered firm through which securities offerings are presented; Baker 1031’s is Aurora Securities.

**45-Day Identification Period:** The post-sale period for identifying replacement property.

**Diversified Portfolio:** Multiple offerings selected together across types and markets.
