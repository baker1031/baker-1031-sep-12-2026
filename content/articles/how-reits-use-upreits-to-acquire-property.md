# How REITs Use UPREITs to Acquire Property

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › How REITs Use UPREITs to Acquire Property

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I start with the low-basis owner, because that owner can make a simple cash bid surprisingly weak. The price may be right, but a cash sale can push a substantial portion of the gain into tax. The [UPREIT](721-exchange-guide.html) gives a REIT another form of acquisition currency: operating-partnership units rather than cash.

First-order thinking calls it tax deferral for the owner. Second-order thinking sees why it also matters to the REIT: it can reach desirable properties that a cash offer cannot. The same 721 transaction is an acquisition strategy from the REIT's side and a tax-deferred exit from the owner's side.

## Why REITs need an acquisition tool

REITs grow by acquiring property. The hurdle is that long-term owners of attractive property often face significant tax in a cash sale. The four-layer tax can consume a third or more of gain, making an otherwise generous cash offer unattractive.

This constraint is most acute for exactly the real estate a REIT may want: established, well-located, income-producing properties held for years by owners with very low basis and large embedded gain. A cash buyer may not reach those assets because the owner would rather keep the property than sell and recognize tax.

The UPREIT addresses that obstacle. It lets the REIT acquire through [OP units](op-units-explained.html), providing a tax-deferred route for owners who will not sell for cash. It is an acquisition tool created to reach gain-laden property without forcing a taxable sale.

## OP units as acquisition currency

The REIT offers OP units instead of cash for the property. Because [contributing property for OP units is tax-deferred under Section 721](upreit-conversion-and-capital-gains-deferral.html), the owner can receive partnership units without the current tax that a cash sale would create.

For a gain-laden owner, that is a meaningful distinction. Rather than lose roughly a third of gain to current tax in a cash sale, the owner may defer tax and exchange direct ownership for diversified REIT ownership, distributions, and a possible route to liquidity. The unit currency can therefore entice sellers that cash cannot.

For the REIT, the result is an expanded acquisition universe: desirable owner-held properties that would remain unavailable to a cash buyer. OP units are tax-advantaged acquisition currency, and that is their core strategic value.

## Where the REIT and owner interests can align

When structured well, an UPREIT acquisition can benefit both sides. The REIT receives an established property it could not obtain for cash and adds it to the portfolio. In exchange, it assumes operating, market, and financing risk associated with that property.

The owner receives a tax-deferred exit into passive REIT ownership rather than a taxable cash sale. Potential benefits include diversification, distributions, a possible limited future liquidity path, and estate planning features such as a potential step-up in basis. Distributions are projected, not guaranteed, and can be reduced or suspended. Liquidity is generally subject to a holding period and sponsor discretion, and conversion can itself be taxable. The [estate planning with a 721 exchange](721-exchange-estate-planning.html) overview explains the step-up consideration.

The owner also gives up direct control. The REIT, as general partner, controls the property. The owner assumes REIT, property-market, interest-rate, dilution, and potential principal-loss risk. Mutual benefit does not eliminate those risks; whether the arrangement fits depends on the owner's goals, tax position, and ability to accept them.

## How the acquisition works

The process runs through the operating partnership. The REIT identifies a property it wants and an owner willing to receive OP units. The operating partnership and owner agree on property value, which determines the number of units issued. The owner contributes the property; the partnership issues OP units in a tax-deferred Section 721 contribution.

Afterward, the property sits in the REIT portfolio through the operating partnership. The former owner holds a limited-partner economic interest through the units. The REIT, as general partner, controls the property; the former owner is now a passive unit holder.

The parties may also negotiate [tax protection](721-exchange-closing-documents-explained.html): an agreement that the REIT will not sell the contributed property in a taxable manner for a period, protecting the owner's deferred gain. Valuation, contribution, unit issuance, and tax protection are the usual components. It is the 721 exchange viewed from the REIT perspective.

## Growing the REIT's portfolio

UPREIT acquisitions can be an important growth engine. By issuing units, the REIT can access low-basis, established property whose owners would not sell for cash. It preserves cash for other uses and converts the former owner into a stakeholder whose economic interest is linked to REIT performance.

Over time, a REIT can build a larger, more diversified portfolio by accumulating property from many contributors. That growth is not guaranteed, and the value of the OP units still depends on how the REIT and portfolio perform. The [UPREIT structure](what-is-an-upreit-umbrella-partnership-reit-basics.html) is therefore both a growth mechanism for REITs and an investment outcome owners must assess carefully.

## Key Takeaways

- REITs need an acquisition tool because cash-sale tax can deter low-basis, long-term owners of desirable property.
- OP units are tax-deferred acquisition currency, reaching gain-laden owners that cash cannot.
- The acquisition can serve both sides: the REIT receives property; the owner receives a tax-deferred exit into REIT ownership.
- UPREIT acquisitions can grow portfolios, conserve REIT cash, and align the contributor with the REIT's performance.

## What this means for property owners

For an owner of desirable property, a REIT's acquisition interest can create an option beyond an ordinary cash sale: contribute for OP units and defer tax. REITs actively seek appropriate properties, so an owner whose asset fits can have an attractive tax-deferred alternative.

It can also create leverage. REITs compete for desirable property, and an owner may be able to negotiate value and tax protection. The extent of that leverage depends on the REIT's interest and the transaction facts.

The [721 exchange (from the owner's side)](721-exchange-explained-convert-property-into-reit-units.html) and the UPREIT acquisition are the same transaction. Seeing both sides helps an owner understand the full deal: a REIT needs the property; the owner may have a tax-deferred exit. Whether that exit is appropriate turns on objectives, tax situation, and OP-unit risk tolerance.

## Risks an owner should weigh

Contributing property for OP units is a securities transaction with risks a cash sale does not carry. The owner transfers control to the REIT general partner. Unit value depends on REIT performance, real-estate markets, and interest rates, and is exposed to market risk, dilution, and possible loss of principal.

Distributions are projected, not guaranteed, and may be reduced or suspended. Liquidity is limited. Unit conversion to REIT shares is generally subject to a holding period and sponsor discretion, and conversion can trigger tax. OP units are illiquid securities available only to verified accredited investors through the sponsor's PPM, which contains the complete terms and risks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do REITs use the UPREIT structure to acquire property?

It permits tax-deferred acquisition, overcoming the tax obstacle that keeps low-basis owners from taking a cash sale. OP units let an owner contribute without triggering the tax, expanding REIT acquisitions to property cash could not reach.

### How do OP units work as acquisition currency?

The REIT offers OP units for property. Section 721 permits tax-deferred contribution for those units, unlike a cash sale. Owners with large gains may prefer the deferral and REIT ownership benefits, so units can attract sellers cash cannot.

### Why is the UPREIT acquisition a win-win?

The REIT can acquire established property unavailable for cash. The owner can exit tax-deferred into passive diversified REIT ownership, with potential income, liquidity, and estate benefits. Proper value and terms matter, but mutual benefit is why the acquisition format became widespread.

### How does an UPREIT acquisition work?

The REIT and owner agree on property value, which sets the OP-unit number. The owner contributes property to the operating partnership, receives units under Section 721, and becomes a limited partner. The property enters the REIT portfolio, and tax protection may be negotiated.

### Does the UPREIT acquisition help REITs grow?

Yes. It reaches gain-laden property cash cannot buy, conserves cash, and aligns contributors as unit-holding stakeholders. REITs can build larger and more diversified portfolios over time through property contributed by many owners.

### What does this mean for me as a property owner?

If a REIT wants the property, an owner may contribute for tax-deferred OP units instead of selling for taxable cash. REIT demand can provide negotiating leverage on value and tax protection. The owner should still evaluate the REIT and fit of the resulting units.

### Will a REIT want my property?

It depends on sector fit, institutional quality, location, size and value thresholds, and financial profile. REITs generally seek well-located, well-maintained properties in target markets and sectors. If a property does not fit, alternatives such as a DST bridge may still lead to REIT ownership.

### Can I negotiate the terms of an UPREIT acquisition?

Often to some degree. Property value determines the units received, and tax protection determines how well the deferred gain is protected. A desirable property can support negotiation, but the result depends on REIT interest and transaction facts. Advisors and counsel help negotiate terms.

### What is tax protection in an UPREIT acquisition?

It is an agreement by the REIT or operating partnership not to sell contributed property taxably for a period, or to compensate the owner if it does. It protects against a partnership sale unexpectedly triggering deferred gain and is an important negotiated term.

### Is contributing to an UPREIT the same as a 721 exchange?

Yes. The UPREIT acquisition is the REIT perspective; the 721 exchange is the owner's perspective. Both describe contribution of property to the operating partnership for OP units under Section 721.

### How does Baker 1031 help with UPREIT acquisitions?

Baker 1031 helps identify REIT interest, assess the REIT, valuation, and tax protection, negotiate terms, execute the 721 contribution, and coordinate with CPA and counsel. OP units are securities offered through Aurora Securities after suitability review.

### Why would a REIT prefer OP units over paying cash?

OP units reach owners who will not sell and pay tax, preserve REIT cash for other uses, and align the contributor with REIT performance. The strategic benefit is access to desirable gain-laden property and cash efficiency.

### Does the REIT benefit more than the owner?

Not necessarily. The REIT gains a property; the owner gains a tax-deferred exit into passive REIT ownership that a cash sale cannot provide. Owners can negotiate value and tax protection. A well-negotiated acquisition can serve both sides.

## Glossary

**UPREIT Acquisition**  
A REIT acquiring property via OP units, the 721 exchange from the REIT's side.

**Acquisition Currency**  
OP units used by the REIT to acquire property tax-deferred.

**Tax Obstacle**  
The tax deterring owners from selling for cash, solved by the UPREIT.

**Low Basis**  
The small basis (large gain) of long-term owners REITs want.

**Operating Partnership**  
The vehicle through which the REIT acquires and holds property.

**OP Units**  
The tax-deferred consideration the REIT offers for property.

**Section 721**  
The provision making the contribution tax-deferred.

**Win-Win**  
The mutual benefit to the REIT (acquisition) and owner (deferred exit).

**Tax Protection**  
The REIT's agreement shielding the owner's gain from triggering.

**Portfolio Growth**  
REITs expanding via UPREIT acquisitions.

**General Partner**  
The REIT's controlling role in the operating partnership.

**Limited Partner**  
The contributing owner's unit-holding role.

**Cash Preservation**  
The REIT acquiring without cash via OP units.

**Interest Alignment**  
The contributing owner becoming a REIT stakeholder.

**Institutional Quality**  
The property standard REITs seek to acquire.

**Owner Opportunity**  
The tax-deferred exit available when a REIT wants your property.
