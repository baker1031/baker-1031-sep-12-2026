# 1031 Exchange Costs & Fees: What You’ll Actually Pay

I have been thinking about how often the fee question gets reduced to one line: “What does the exchange cost?” The honest answer has two parts. A standard delayed exchange adds a modest qualified-intermediary fee to the normal costs of selling and buying property. A reverse or improvement exchange can cost far more because the structure itself is more involved.

That difference is not a reason to choose the cheapest option. First-order thinking says, “Find the lowest QI quote.” Second-order thinking asks whether the intermediary can protect the proceeds, whether the exchange structure fits the deal, which closing charges can create boot, and whether the all-in cost is small relative to the tax actually deferred. Price matters. Risk, fund security, and the quality of the replacement decision matter more.

## Key Takeaways

- The fee unique to a 1031 exchange is the qualified intermediary (QI) fee, and it is generally modest for a standard delayed exchange.
- Reverse and improvement exchanges are considerably more costly because they require a holding entity and additional legal work.
- You still pay ordinary transaction costs: commissions, title, escrow, recording, and transfer taxes.
- Customary exchange expenses can generally be paid from exchange proceeds without creating boot. Paying non-exchange costs from those proceeds can create taxable boot.

## The Qualified Intermediary Fee

The charge that exists only because an investor is doing a 1031 exchange is the QI fee. The qualified intermediary is the independent party that holds the proceeds so the investor does not have constructive receipt. For a standard delayed exchange, the charge is generally a modest base administrative fee, sometimes with a small additional charge for each extra replacement property.

Many intermediaries earn interest on funds held during the exchange. Ask how that interest is treated and whether it offsets the stated fee. The headline price alone does not answer the economic question.

It also does not answer the safety question. Intermediaries are lightly regulated in many states while holding large sums of client money. Ask how proceeds are secured, including whether accounts are segregated and what bonding or insurance is in place. The cheapest intermediary that mishandles the money can be the most expensive mistake in the transaction. For an overview of the role, see the [main 1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html).

## Cost by Exchange Structure

The structure of the exchange is the largest driver of cost.

| Exchange type | Relative cost | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard (delayed) | Lowest | The QI holds proceeds and prepares the documents. |
| Reverse | Several times higher | An Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT) must be formed and operated while a property is parked. |
| Improvement / construction | Several times higher | Construction administration and a holding entity are required while work is completed. |

Actual fees vary by provider, deal size, and complexity.

The move from a delayed exchange to a [reverse exchange](reverse-1031-exchange.html) shows why cost rises. Once a property must be parked with a holding entity, entity formation, legal work, and carrying costs enter the picture. Those costs may be appropriate when the transaction requires that structure. They are not a reason to use reverse or improvement arrangements when a standard delayed exchange will do.

## The Ordinary Costs of Buying and Selling

An exchange also carries the normal expenses of any real-estate sale and purchase. They are not unique to a 1031, but there are two transactions to complete: real-estate commissions, title insurance, escrow or settlement charges, recording fees, and state or local transfer taxes. Attorney and tax-advisor fees may apply as well. In reverse or improvement exchanges, carrying costs for property held by the accommodation entity can be added.

When investors are surprised by exchange cost, these ordinary transaction expenses usually account for more of the total than the QI fee itself.

## How Fees Can Create Boot

The way a cost is paid can have tax consequences. Customary **exchange expenses**—broker commissions, the QI fee, title and escrow charges, and transfer taxes—can generally be paid from exchange proceeds without creating taxable [boot](1031-exchange-boot.html).

Other costs are generally not exchange expenses. Loan-acquisition fees and points, prorated rents, security-deposit transfers, and property-tax or insurance prorations can create boot when paid from exchange funds. The practical discipline is to pay non-exchange costs with funds outside the exchange and review the settlement statement line by line with the QI and tax advisor before closing.

## Is a 1031 Exchange Worth the Cost?

For an investor with meaningful gain, the cost comparison can be straightforward. Federal capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, net investment income tax, and state tax can take a large share of gain on a long-held property. Against that, the charge for a standard exchange is small.

The answer becomes less clear at the margins: modest gain, little appreciation or depreciation recapture, or a reverse or improvement structure whose cost is not justified by the tax deferred. Run the actual figures. Estimate the tax from an outright sale, compare it with the all-in exchange cost, and then weigh the exchange’s deadlines, reinvestment requirement, and like-kind replacement constraint.

For a sizable gain, the comparison is rarely close. For a small gain, it should be tested rather than assumed.

## A Worked Comparison

Consider two simplified cases. In the first, an outright sale produces a large taxable gain, and combined federal and state taxes take a substantial amount from the capital available for the next investment. A standard delayed exchange defers that amount for a comparatively small incremental fee. Even after the ordinary costs that any sale would incur, more capital remains invested than in an outright sale.

In the second, the gain is small, prior depreciation is limited, and a reverse structure multiplies the fee. The advantage may narrow or disappear. The figures are hypothetical, but the shape of the decision is useful: exchange cost usually scales modestly, while the tax deferred rises or falls with the gain.

## What Moves the QI Fee

A standard delayed exchange with one relinquished property and one replacement property generally sits at the bottom of the fee range. Costs can rise with:

- Additional replacement properties, often through a modest per-property charge.
- Multiple relinquished properties.
- A rushed timeline that compresses the work.
- The structure itself. Reverse and improvement exchanges can cost several times a delayed exchange because a holding entity must be formed and operated.

Ask whether a quote is all-in or whether wiring, extra-property, and document charges appear later. Also ask how interest on held funds is handled, since that interest may meaningfully offset the stated fee at some firms.

## Choosing an Intermediary: Cost and Safety

The QI may hold a large sum of money for months in an industry that is lightly regulated. Selecting only on price is how an investor can lose proceeds to an intermediary that fails or misuses funds—and lose the exchange along with them.

Before engaging a QI, ask about segregated **qualified escrow or qualified trust accounts**, **fidelity bonding and errors-and-omissions insurance**, the firm’s financial strength and track record, and whether dual authorization is required to move funds. Saving a few hundred dollars with a cut-rate provider is a poor trade against the safety of the entire proceeds. Pay for safety; negotiate the rest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a 1031 exchange cost?

For a standard delayed exchange, the QI fee is usually modest, in addition to ordinary costs of a sale. Reverse and construction exchanges can cost several times more. Exact fees vary by provider and complexity.

### Why are reverse exchanges more expensive?

They require an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder to park a property, plus the added legal work and carrying costs that a standard delayed exchange does not require.

### Can I pay exchange fees from my proceeds?

Customary exchange expenses such as commissions, the QI fee, and title charges can generally be paid from exchange funds without creating boot. Paying non-exchange costs, such as loan points and prorated rents, that way can create taxable boot.

### Is a 1031 exchange worth the cost?

For most investors with meaningful gain, yes: fees are small relative to the tax deferred. The case is weaker when the gain and resulting tax are small, or when an expensive reverse structure is not justified.

### What hidden costs should I watch for?

Watch the ordinary costs of two transactions—commissions, title, escrow, recording, and transfer taxes—plus entity and carrying costs in reverse and improvement exchanges. Those expenses often exceed the QI fee.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Intermediary Fee:** The charge paid to the independent party that holds proceeds and facilitates the exchange.
- **Exchange Expenses:** Customary transaction costs that can generally be paid from exchange funds without creating boot.
- **Improvement Exchange:** An exchange in which proceeds fund construction or renovation on the replacement property while it is parked.
- **Transfer Tax:** A state or local tax on a real-estate conveyance, payable on the sale and purchase.

## Hidden and Future Costs to Budget For

Complex structures, including reverse and improvement exchanges, carry higher setup costs. Some intermediaries charge expedited fees when the calendar is compressed. Replacement financing adds loan-origination, appraisal, and related charges; some cannot be paid from exchange funds without boot implications. With a [DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html), the upfront load is only part of the picture: ongoing management fees can affect returns during the hold.

The most overlooked cost may be the carryover-basis effect. A 1031 exchange carries the old, often low basis into the replacement property rather than resetting basis to the new purchase price. Future depreciation deductions can therefore be smaller than on a fresh purchase. It is not a closing fee, but it is a real economic effect of deferral.

A useful budget includes the QI fee, closing and title costs on both legs—often the largest category—advisory or sponsor fees, and these less-visible expenses. Compare the total with the four layers of tax the exchange defers. For a meaningful gain, the deferred tax will often dwarf the cost, but the replacement should still earn its place on its own terms.
