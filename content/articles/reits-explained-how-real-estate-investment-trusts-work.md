# REITs Explained: How Real Estate Investment Trusts Work

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep returning to the same basic question with REITs: what, exactly, is the investor being paid for? A REIT lets investors own a piece of income-producing real estate without buying, financing, or managing property directly. This guide explains the structure, income sources, 90% distribution rule, REIT types, traded versus non-traded structure, and comparisons with direct property and DSTs. Tax and suitability rules vary; verify current rules with a tax advisor. This is educational information, not investment advice.

First-order thinking sees a REIT as a dividend stock. Second-order thinking asks whether it owns property or holds mortgages, how it pays income, what risks sit behind the yield, whether liquidity is genuine, and whether the structure fits the investor’s purpose.

## Key Takeaways

- A REIT owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and distributes most income to shareholders as dividends.
- The 90% distribution rule supports REITs’ high payout model and corporate-tax treatment, but it also means growth often requires new equity or debt.
- Equity REITs own property and earn rents; mortgage REITs finance property and earn interest; hybrid REITs do both.
- Public REITs offer exchange liquidity and market pricing. Non-traded REITs are NAV-valued, illiquid, and commonly use capped redemption programs.

## What Is a REIT?

A [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html), or REIT, is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and distributes most of its income to shareholders. It pools investor capital into real estate or real-estate debt so an investor can gain exposure through shares instead of buying, financing, and managing a building directly. REIT portfolios can include apartments, warehouses, data centers, shopping centers, cell towers, offices, retail properties, and other assets.

To qualify under the tax code, a REIT must distribute at least 90% of taxable income annually; hold at least 75% of assets in real estate, cash, or government securities; derive at least 75% of gross income from real-estate sources such as rents, mortgage interest, and property gains; have at least 100 shareholders; and comply with the 5/50 rule, under which five or fewer individuals cannot own more than 50% of its shares. A qualifying REIT avoids corporate-level income tax, so its income is principally taxed at the shareholder level rather than being taxed twice.

## How REITs Make Money

Equity REITs, the most common type, own and operate income-producing properties. They earn rent from tenants, pay expenses, and distribute much of the remaining income as dividends. Property appreciation can be another source of value over time.

A [mortgage REIT (mREIT)](mortgage-reits-mreits-explained.html) does not own property in the same way. It holds mortgages or mortgage-backed securities and earns the spread between interest received and its own borrowing cost. That makes mortgage REITs highly interest-rate-sensitive. The core engines are rents for equity REITs and mortgage interest for mREITs, with property appreciation as an additional source for equity REITs. Most of the income flows through as dividends, which is why REIT yields tend to run higher than the broad stock market.

## The 90% Distribution Rule

The 90% distribution rule requires a REIT to distribute at least 90% of taxable income each year as dividends. Most distribute close to 100% to eliminate corporate tax entirely. The rule is why income is such a central part of the REIT structure: most earnings pass to shareholders rather than remaining inside the company.

There is a trade-off. An ordinary company can retain earnings for internal reinvestment. A REIT pays most earnings out, so it often raises capital by issuing shares or debt to acquire or develop property. The rule produces high, regular distributions and shapes how REITs grow; it is not a promise that distributions will remain unchanged.

## Types of REITs

Equity REITs own and operate property, earning rents and potentially benefiting from property appreciation. They operate across residential, industrial, office, retail, healthcare, data-center, self-storage, and other sectors. This is the classic own-the-buildings, collect-the-rent model.

Mortgage REITs finance property through mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. Their interest-rate spread can produce high yields, but it also makes them more rate-sensitive and often higher risk. Hybrid REITs combine both approaches by owning property and holding mortgage debt. Each type has a different risk-and-return profile, so type is an important part of understanding what an investor owns.

## Publicly Traded vs. Non-Traded REITs

Public REITs are exchange-listed. Shares trade throughout the day at transparent market prices, with daily liquidity and generally lower upfront fees. Their prices can be volatile in the short term with the broader market even when underlying properties are stable.

Non-traded REITs are SEC-registered but unlisted. They are valued periodically at net asset value, or NAV, rather than trading daily on an exchange. Liquidity usually comes through periodic redemption programs, commonly capped around 2% of NAV per month and 5% per quarter and subject to suspension. Non-traded REITs have historically carried higher loads and upfront fees and are typically designed for longer-term income investors who do not need ready liquidity. Non-traded and private REITs are generally offered through a broker-dealer and require accredited or otherwise suitable investors after a suitability review.

Two questions define the structure: does the REIT own property, hold mortgages, or both; and does the investor hold a liquid, exchange-listed share or an illiquid, NAV-priced non-traded interest?

## REITs vs. Other Real Estate Investments

Compared with direct ownership, REITs are passive, diversified, and accessible with low minimums. Public shares are liquid. The investor does not handle tenants, repairs, or financing. Direct ownership offers hands-on control, potential operating leverage, and direct benefits such as depreciation, but it is active, illiquid, and concentrated in one or a few properties.

Compared with a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html), the central difference is 1031 treatment. A DST is passive, fractional real estate that can qualify as replacement property in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), while [a REIT share is a security that is not 1031-eligible](reits-and-1031-exchanges-why-reit-shares-dont-qualify.html). A possible bridge is a 1031 exchange into a DST followed by a later 721 or [UPREIT](721-exchange-guide.html) transaction if the DST property is acquired by a REIT, which can convert the interest to operating-partnership units while preserving deferral. REITs trade direct control and 1031 eligibility for passivity, liquidity, diversification, and low minimums.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a REIT?

A REIT is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and distributes most income to shareholders as dividends. It pools capital into real estate so investors can buy shares rather than directly buy, finance, and manage property. Qualification requires the 90% distribution rule, 75% asset and income tests, at least 100 shareholders, and the 5/50 ownership rule. The qualified structure avoids corporate-level income tax, so income is principally taxed at the shareholder level.

### How do REITs make money?

Equity REITs own apartments, warehouses, offices, retail assets, data centers, and other property, earning rent and potentially benefiting from appreciation. Mortgage REITs hold mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, earning interest spread over their borrowing cost and carrying substantial rate sensitivity. After expenses, REITs distribute most underlying rent, mortgage interest, and property-income gains as dividends.

### What is the 90% distribution rule?

To qualify as a REIT and avoid corporate-level income tax, a company must distribute at least 90% of taxable income each year as dividends; most distribute close to 100%. That makes dividends structurally important and often supports higher yields. The trade-off is limited retained earnings, so REITs often issue shares or debt to fund acquisitions and development.

### What are the three types of REITs?

Equity REITs own property and earn rents and possible appreciation. Mortgage REITs finance property with mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, earning interest spread and carrying higher rate sensitivity and risk. Hybrid REITs own some property and hold some mortgage debt. The type determines how the REIT participates in real estate and shapes its risk-and-return profile.

### Are REIT dividends taxed?

Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at qualified-dividend rates because the REIT did not pay corporate tax. Qualified REIT dividends can receive a 20% Section 199A deduction, reducing the effective top federal rate to roughly 29.6%; the 2025 OBBBA legislation made the deduction permanent. Distributions may also include return of capital, which reduces basis rather than being currently taxed, or capital-gain distributions taxed at capital-gains rates. Form 1099-DIV reports the components. Confirm treatment with a tax advisor.

### Are REITs liquid?

It depends on the structure. Public REITs trade on exchanges throughout the day at market prices and are generally liquid through a brokerage account. Non-traded REITs are not exchange-listed, have no daily trading, and generally rely on periodic redemption programs commonly capped around 2% of NAV per month and 5% per quarter and subject to suspension. They are intended for longer-term investors who do not need ready access to capital.

### How are REITs different from owning property directly?

REITs provide passive ownership, diversification across many properties, low minimums, and liquidity for public shares. Direct owners receive hands-on control, may use leverage, and receive direct depreciation benefits, but also take on management, illiquidity, and concentration. REIT shares are securities and cannot serve as 1031 replacement property, while direct real estate can. The choice is between passive, liquid, diversified exposure and hands-on, controllable, 1031-eligible property.

### Can I use a REIT in a 1031 exchange?

No. A 1031 exchange requires like-kind real property held for investment or business use, and REIT shares are securities. An investor cannot exchange directly from investment real estate to REIT shares. A DST is 1031-eligible and may later participate in a 721 or UPREIT transaction, potentially converting the interest to operating-partnership units and later REIT shares while preserving deferral. Confirm the specifics with a tax advisor.

### What is the difference between a traded and a non-traded REIT?

Public REITs are exchange-listed, daily liquid, market-priced, generally lower-fee, and more exposed to short-term market volatility. Non-traded REITs are SEC-registered but unlisted, NAV-valued periodically, illiquid, and rely on capped redemption programs that can be suspended. They historically have had higher loads and upfront fees and are generally designed for longer-term income investors. The differences are liquidity, pricing, fees, and volatility.

### Are REITs a good source of income?

They can be, because the 90% rule requires high distributions and REIT yields tend to exceed the broad stock market. Stable equity-REIT sectors such as net lease, healthcare, and some residential property can provide relatively steady distributions. Mortgage REITs can offer higher yields but carry more interest-rate risk. No distribution is guaranteed: property income can decline, dividends can be cut, and REIT shares fluctuate. REITs are not guaranteed or bond-like income investments, so size and diversify the allocation appropriately.

### What property sectors do REITs invest in?

Equity REITs cover residential properties, industrial warehouses and logistics facilities, retail and net lease, office, healthcare, data centers, self-storage, hospitality, cell towers, timberland, farmland, and other specialized sectors. Each has different demand drivers. Net lease and healthcare are often associated with stable income; data centers and industrial are often associated with growth. Mortgage REITs invest in real-estate debt rather than a specific property sector. Sector helps define income and risk.

### How do I invest in a REIT?

Public REITs can be purchased through an ordinary brokerage account, either as individual shares or as REIT mutual funds and ETFs holding a basket of REITs. Non-traded and private REITs are offered through a broker-dealer, usually require accredited or otherwise suitable investors, and involve a subscription and suitability process. Before investing, understand the REIT’s type, sector, fees, and liquidity terms and choose the access route that fits goals, horizon, and liquidity needs.

### What are the main risks of investing in REITs?

Risks include market volatility, interest-rate sensitivity, property and sector weakness, distribution cuts, illiquidity in non-traded REITs, and leverage, which can amplify gains and losses. Public share prices can fall even when properties are stable. Rising rates can pressure prices and mREIT interest spreads. Rents, occupancy, and property values can decline. Diversification and appropriate position sizing can help manage risk but cannot eliminate it. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Do REITs offer diversification?

Yes. A single REIT commonly owns many properties across markets, and a REIT fund or ETF adds diversification across many REITs. Real estate has historically behaved somewhat differently from stocks and bonds, so REITs can diversify an equity and fixed-income portfolio while providing real-estate-linked income. Public REITs still trade as equities and can move with the stock market, particularly in the short term, so the benefit is partial rather than complete.

## Glossary

### REIT

A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.

### Equity REIT

A REIT that owns property and earns income from rents.

### Mortgage REIT (mREIT)

A REIT that finances real estate and earns mortgage interest.

### Hybrid REIT

A REIT that both owns property and holds mortgage debt.

### 90% Distribution Rule

The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

### 75% Asset Test

At least 75% of assets in real estate, cash, or government securities.

### 75% Income Test

At least 75% of gross income from real-estate sources.

### 5/50 Rule

Five or fewer individuals cannot own more than 50% of shares.

### Dividend

The distribution a REIT pays shareholders from income.

### Publicly Traded REIT

An exchange-listed, liquid, market-priced REIT.

### Non-Traded REIT

An SEC-registered but unlisted, illiquid, NAV-valued REIT.

### Net Asset Value (NAV)

The per-share value used to price a non-traded REIT.

### Redemption Program

A non-traded REIT’s limited, often-capped liquidity feature.

### Section 199A Deduction

The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

### Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)

1031-eligible fractional real estate, unlike a REIT share.

### 721 / UPREIT Exchange

Contributing property to a REIT for OP units while preserving deferral.
