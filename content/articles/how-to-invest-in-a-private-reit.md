# How to Invest in a Private REIT: Minimums, Yield & Liquidity

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REITs · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 14 min read

I keep seeing private REIT liquidity described as though a redemption program makes the capital readily available. It does not. A private REIT can offer diversified, income-focused real estate for accredited investors, but it does so at the cost of liquidity. Here is who can invest, what to expect, and what to check first.

First-order thinking sees a diversified slice of institutional real estate, regular income, no property management, and no daily public-market swings. Second-order thinking asks about the requirements, economics, and the terms that govern getting your money back. Those terms separate a sound private REIT from a weak one.

## Key Takeaways

- Private REITs are generally limited to accredited investors and sold through Regulation D private placements, with status verified at subscription.
- Minimums usually run higher than public or non-traded REITs, often tens of thousands of dollars, so the position should sit inside a diversified portfolio.
- Distributions are a target, not a guarantee, can move with the portfolio, and may include return of capital that reduces your basis rather than true income.
- Liquidity is conditional. You exit through a redemption program with lock-ups, periodic windows, caps, and the sponsor's discretion to slow or suspend buybacks.
- The fee load and how net asset value is set deserve as much scrutiny as the yield, because both quietly shape what you actually keep.
- The private placement memorandum is the document that governs the deal. Read it in full, and weigh the sponsor's track record before you fund.

If you are still working out the terrain, our [complete guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html) frames where this vehicle fits before you commit capital to one.

## What a private REIT actually is

A private REIT is a real estate investment trust that does not register its shares for public sale and does not list on any exchange. Like every REIT, it owns income-producing property and must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders each year, which lets it avoid corporate-level tax and pass the income through to you. Shares are offered under Regulation D, the same private-placement framework used for DSTs and many funds, and sold to accredited and institutional investors rather than the general public. That structural choice drives the higher minimums, limited liquidity, and lighter public-reporting profile.

It helps to place a private REIT beside its cousins. A publicly traded REIT lists on an exchange, trades daily at a market price, and is open to anyone with a brokerage account. A non-traded REIT is SEC-registered but not listed, priced at periodic net asset value and sold through advisors. A private REIT is the least liquid and least public of the three, available only to accredited investors and built for a multi-year hold. Our [private vs. public vs. non-traded comparison](private-reit-vs-public-reit.html) sets the three side by side.

## Who can invest: the accredited standard

Because a private REIT is sold under Regulation D and not registered for public sale, it is generally available only to accredited investors. The federal definition offers a few ways to qualify. On income, you need more than $200,000 individually, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse, in each of the two most recent years, with a reasonable expectation of the same in the current year. On net worth, you need more than $1 million excluding the value of your primary residence. Since 2020 there are also credential-based paths: holding a Series 7, 65, or 82 license in good standing qualifies you regardless of income or net worth. Our [guide to accredited-investor requirements](insights.html) walks through each test in detail.

You do not simply check a box. As part of subscribing, your status is verified, typically through tax documents, brokerage and account statements, a letter from your CPA or attorney, or a third-party verification service. The sponsor and broker-dealer placing the offering are obligated to confirm you qualify before accepting your money. Investors who are not accredited but want REIT exposure generally use public or non-traded REITs instead, which carry no accreditation requirement. The accredited gate exists because a private placement carries less mandated disclosure than a registered offering, and the rule presumes a qualifying investor can absorb the risk and evaluate the deal.

## Typical minimum investment

Private REIT minimums sit at the high end of the REIT spectrum. A public REIT can be bought one share at a time and a non-traded REIT often starts in the low thousands; a private REIT frequently asks for a minimum in the tens of thousands of dollars, and some institutional-style offerings ask for more. The higher floor reflects both the private nature of the offering and the sophistication the structure presumes of its accredited participants. It also reflects the economics of a private placement, which is not built to process thousands of small tickets.

The planning consequence is straightforward. Because the position is sizable and illiquid, a private REIT should generally be one holding within a diversified portfolio, not a concentrated bet. The committed dollars should be money you can leave invested for years without touching. If losing access to this capital for five years or more would strain your plan, the amount is too large or the vehicle is the wrong fit. Size it so an extended lock-up is an inconvenience rather than a crisis.

## Distributions and yield expectations

Private REITs are typically income-oriented, aiming to pay regular distributions funded by portfolio rental income. Headline yield varies widely with strategy, leverage, and property type. Like any real-estate return, it is a target, not a guarantee. A distribution can be cut if occupancy slips, financing costs rise, or a sale environment softens. Treat any quoted yield as a projection that depends on the business plan working, not as a coupon.

Two cautions carry over from how we evaluate DSTs. Be skeptical of a distribution that appears to exceed the portfolio's actual cash flow, because the gap may be funded by return of capital: part of your own investment returned rather than income the properties earned. Return of capital lowers cost basis and defers tax; a payout propped up by it can mask a portfolio that is not covering its distribution. Also evaluate yield net of fees. A transparent sponsor will show what funds the distribution and what coverage looks like; the absence of clarity is information. Our memo on [how private REIT dividends are taxed](private-reit-dividends-tax.html) explains how one distribution can split into ordinary income, capital gain, and return of capital at tax time.

## Liquidity, redemptions, and lock-ups

Illiquidity is the defining constraint of a private REIT. There is no public market, so you cannot sell shares to another investor. You access capital through the sponsor's redemption program, and those programs have conditions. Most include an initial lock-up, often a year or more, during which you cannot redeem at all. After that, redemptions occur in periodic monthly or quarterly windows and are usually capped at a small share of net assets per period. When requests exceed the cap, they are prorated. The sponsor typically retains discretion to limit or suspend the program entirely, often in the stressed markets when investors most want their money.

The ability to exit is real but conditional, and least reliable precisely when it matters most. Read redemption terms as carefully as return projections. Confirm the lock-up length, redemption frequency, per-period cap, whether early redemptions carry a discount to NAV, and the exact language on the sponsor's right to gate. A program that looks generous can still leave you waiting several quarters to fully exit if requests hit the cap. Plan to hold through the sponsor's full-cycle event, and treat the redemption program as a relief valve, not a checking account.

## Fees and how NAV is set

Fees are where a private REIT's economics quietly diverge from a low-cost public one. Depending on the offering, you may encounter an upfront selling commission or dealer-manager fee, an ongoing asset-management fee, acquisition and disposition fees on properties, financing fees, and in some structures a performance or promote that pays the sponsor a share of profits above a hurdle. None is automatically disqualifying: active management and deal sourcing cost money. But the all-in load erodes return, so add the layers rather than judging any one in isolation.

Net asset value matters just as much because NAV is both the entry price and the basis for redemptions. Ask how often NAV is recalculated, who performs appraisals, and what methodology and assumptions sit behind it. An NAV updated infrequently or set with optimistic assumptions can overstate what shares are worth, which matters most on the way out. A sponsor that explains its valuation process plainly and uses independent third-party appraisals shows the transparency a private, illiquid vehicle demands.

The table below sets out the terms that most shape a private REIT investment and the question each should prompt before you commit.

| Term | What it covers | What to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimum investment | The smallest amount accepted | Can I size this as one position, not a concentration? |
| Lock-up | Initial period with no redemptions | How long before I can request any exit? |
| Redemption cap | Limit on buybacks per period | What share of NAV per quarter, and is there proration? |
| Gating rights | Sponsor's right to slow or suspend | Under what conditions can redemptions be halted? |
| Fee load | Upfront and ongoing charges | What is my projected return net of all fees? |
| NAV process | How shares are priced | How often, by whom, and on what assumptions? |
| Distribution coverage | What funds the payout | Is the distribution covered by cash flow, or return of capital? |

## The subscription process, step by step

Investing in a private REIT runs through a structured subscription, usually with a broker-dealer or adviser involved because the offering requires suitability review. The sequence is straightforward once you know the pieces.

1. **Confirm suitability and accredited status**, and that illiquidity genuinely fits your timeline and your need for capital.
2. **Review the offering materials**: the strategy, portfolio, leverage, fees, and redemption terms, before paperwork.
3. **Read the private placement memorandum (PPM)** in full. It holds the complete risk and fee disclosure the marketing summary does not.
4. **Complete the subscription agreement** and verify accreditation, typically with supporting documents or a verification letter.
5. **Fund the investment**, then **hold and monitor**: collect distributions, review statements, and track NAV updates and any redemption-program changes.

Private REITs are sold through financial professionals who conduct suitability review and often rely on third-party due-diligence reports, so an adviser is usually part of the process. Understand how that adviser is compensated, whether through a commission embedded in the offering or a separate fee, because compensation can shape what gets recommended. The same point applies across private real estate; our [how to invest in a DST](how-to-invest-in-a-dst.html) memo makes it directly.

## Where a private REIT fits in a portfolio

A private REIT is a tool, not a default. It suits someone who wants diversified, professionally managed real-estate income, can meet the accredited standard, and genuinely does not need the committed capital for years. Rather than owning one building through a single-asset deal, you own a fractional slice of a portfolio that spreads tenant, property, and sometimes geographic risk across many holdings. For someone who has sold an active rental and wants to remain in real estate without managing anything, that pooled exposure can fit.

It is a poor fit for money needed on short notice, for an investor uncomfortable with a multi-year lock-up, or for anyone who would have to concentrate to meet the minimum. It differs from a Delaware Statutory Trust in a way that matters for 1031 investors: a private REIT is not 1031 replacement property, so a real-estate gain cannot be exchanged directly into one. If deferring gain is the goal, a DST is the direct route, and a private REIT may enter later through a 721 roll-up. Our [private REIT vs. syndication vs. DST](private-reit-vs-syndication.html) memo compares the passive structures side by side.

## A due-diligence checklist

Before committing, answer these questions in concrete terms, not generalities. What is the sponsor's track record, and how have prior offerings actually performed through a full cycle, including any that disappointed? What does the portfolio hold, and how diversified and leveraged is it, since leverage amplifies both returns and losses? What is the full fee load, and what is the projected return net of it? Is the distribution supported by real cash flow, or partly by return of capital? Most important for a private vehicle, what are the redemption terms and lock-up, and how and how often is NAV determined?

A sponsor who answers clearly is showing the transparency a private, illiquid vehicle demands. Evasiveness on any one is a reason to slow down, not a detail to wave past. Confirm who custodies assets, how often reporting arrives, what tax documents look like, and what happens at the end of the fund's life. Our companion piece, [are private REITs safe](are-private-reits-safe.html), walks through the risk factors behind these questions. Review specifics with your own financial, tax, and legal advisors before investing; the right answer depends on your facts, not an offering's pitch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I have to be accredited to invest in a private REIT?

Generally yes. Private REITs are sold under Regulation D and limited to accredited investors, and status is verified at subscription. You can qualify by income (over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly for two years), by net worth (over $1 million excluding your primary residence), or by a Series 7, 65, or 82 license. Non-accredited investors typically use public or non-traded REITs.

### How much do I need to invest in a private REIT?

Minimums are typically higher than public or non-traded REITs, often in the tens of thousands of dollars and sometimes more for institutional offerings. Because the position is sizable and illiquid, treat it as one holding in a diversified portfolio rather than a place to concentrate.

### What yield do private REITs pay?

It varies by strategy, leverage, and property type. It is a target rather than a guarantee and can be cut if the portfolio underperforms. Evaluate yield net of fees, and be wary of distributions that exceed cash flow and may be funded partly by return of capital rather than income.

### How do I get my money out of a private REIT?

Through the sponsor's redemption program, which typically has an initial lock-up, periodic windows, caps on how much may be redeemed each period, and the sponsor's discretion to limit or suspend redemptions in stressed markets. Liquidity is real but conditional, so read those terms before investing and plan to hold through the full-cycle event.

### What fees should I expect?

Depending on the offering, you may see an upfront selling commission, ongoing asset-management fee, acquisition and disposition fees, financing fees, and sometimes a performance promote. None is automatically disqualifying, but add layers and judge projected return net of the all-in load, not gross yield.

### How is the share price set if it does not trade?

By net asset value: an appraisal-based per-share value of the portfolio net of debt, recalculated on a schedule. NAV is both the entry price and redemption basis, so ask how often it is updated, who performs appraisals, and what assumptions underlie it. An infrequent or optimistic NAV can overstate what shares are worth.

### What should I check before investing in a private REIT?

Check the sponsor's track record through a full cycle, the portfolio's diversification and leverage, the full fee load and net return, whether distributions are supported by cash flow, and the redemption, lock-up, and NAV terms. Clear answers signal transparency; evasiveness on any is a reason to step back. Review specifics with your own advisors.

## Glossary

### Private REIT

A Regulation D real estate investment trust offered only to accredited investors, the least liquid and least public of the REIT types.

### Accredited Investor

An investor meeting SEC income, net-worth, or licensing thresholds who may participate in private placements such as a private REIT.

### Regulation D

The SEC framework that allows securities to be sold privately, without full public registration, to qualifying investors.

### Redemption Program

A private or non-traded REIT's mechanism for buying back shares, typically with lock-ups, periodic windows, caps, and sponsor discretion to gate.

### Lock-Up

An initial period after investing during which shares cannot be redeemed at all.

### Return of Capital

A distribution that returns part of your own investment rather than income earned; it reduces your cost basis and defers tax.

### Net Asset Value (NAV)

The appraisal-based per-share value used to price private and non-traded REITs, serving as both the entry price and redemption basis.

### Private Placement Memorandum (PPM)

The legal offering document that discloses a private REIT's strategy, risks, fees, and terms in full; the controlling document for the deal.
