# Retail REITs: Malls, Strip Centers & Net-Lease

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about how often the word “retail” gets used as if it describes one investment. It does not. A grocery-anchored center, a trophy mall, a commodity mall, and a single-tenant net-lease building may all appear in the same sector screen, yet their cash flow and risk can be very different.

The first-order view is easy: retail property collects rent and REITs pay dividends. Second-order thinking starts with the format, the tenant roster, the trade area, the lease structure, the price paid, and what can happen when an anchor fails or a lease rolls. The label says much less than those particulars.

Retail REITs own shopping property where consumers buy goods and services in person. The sector includes enclosed regional malls, open-air strip and shopping centers, and freestanding buildings leased to a single tenant on long net leases. E-commerce has pressured some formats, especially commodity malls and discretionary apparel centers, while necessity, convenience, and experiential uses have generally been more resilient. These are general sector observations, not recommendations to buy any REIT. Demand and outlook differ by property and market; past performance does not guarantee future results, and current conditions should be verified with an adviser.

Retail is part of the wider REIT picture. See the [private REIT pillar guide](reits-guide.html) for broader context.

## Types of Retail REITs

Retail REITs are best understood by their format. The first group is the regional mall: an enclosed destination built around department-store or large-format anchors with inline specialty tenants, food, and increasingly entertainment. It has been the most exposed to e-commerce and department-store decline, though high-quality trophy or “A” malls in strong locations have been more resilient than commodity “B” and “C” malls.

The second group is the strip or shopping center. These are open-air properties, often neighborhood centers anchored by a grocer or another necessity retailer, with smaller inline tenants. Groceries, pharmacies, services, quick-service food, fitness, and salons can produce recurring, needs-based traffic that has generally been harder to replace online.

The third group is net-lease retail. It consists of single-tenant, freestanding properties such as pharmacies, convenience stores, dollar stores, auto-parts stores, and quick-service restaurants. Their income depends largely on the tenant's credit and the lease terms rather than day-to-day foot traffic.

## Malls vs. Strip Centers

A regional mall is an enclosed destination property. It depends on its anchors, inline specialty shops, and discretionary foot traffic. E-commerce and the decline of department stores have hurt many malls. Yet a strong “A” mall in an affluent, growing, supply-constrained market, with healthy sales per square foot, can operate differently from a lower-quality mall in a weaker market.

A grocery-anchored strip center is open-air and convenience-oriented. A grocer can draw regular traffic for necessities. Pharmacies, salons, fitness businesses, and quick-service restaurants benefit from being beside that anchor. Because the trips are frequent and needs-based rather than discretionary and destination-driven, these centers have generally proved more resilient to online competition. Their smaller, lower-cost spaces can be easier to re-tenant when a store closes.

The useful contrast is not “malls good” or “retail bad.” It is anchor-dependent discretionary traffic versus recurring necessity traffic, and trophy quality versus commodity quality. A property label is only the beginning of the analysis.

## Net-Lease Retail REITs

Net-lease retail REITs own one-tenant freestanding buildings. A triple-net, or NNN, lease usually requires the tenant to pay property taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to rent. That shifts most operating expenses to the tenant and can make landlord income relatively passive and predictable.

Leases commonly run 10 to 20 years and often include contractual rent escalations. The result can look more like a fixed-income instrument than a mall: tenant creditworthiness, the remaining lease term, and escalation terms are the central variables. It is still real estate, not a bond. Each property has single-tenant concentration risk, and tenant distress or a bad re-leasing outcome can matter greatly. Diversification across tenants, industries, and geographies helps manage that risk but does not erase it.

## E-Commerce Pressure & Resilience

E-commerce is the defining structural force in retail, but it is selective. Discretionary, easily shipped goods have faced the heaviest online competition, which has pressured commodity malls and weaker apparel-heavy centers through store closures, anchor failures, and less traffic. The change resembles the effect of hybrid work on [office REITs](office-reits-in-a-hybrid-work-world.html): a broad label can conceal a large difference in use and demand.

Necessity and convenience retail—groceries, pharmacies, and daily services—require recurring in-person trips. Experiential retail, including dining, entertainment, fitness, and personal services, provides something that cannot be shipped. A property that functions as a fulfillment or pickup point can also benefit from omnichannel shopping. Format, tenant quality, location, and the actual use of the space matter more than a blanket view of “retail.”

### Key Takeaways

- Retail REITs come in three formats—regional malls, grocery-anchored strip centers, and single-tenant net-lease properties—each with a distinct profile.
- Malls are anchor-dependent and most e-commerce-exposed, except for trophy “A” malls; grocery-anchored strip centers draw recurring necessity traffic and tend to be more resilient.
- Net-lease retail can produce predictable, bond-like income from long triple-net leases, driven by tenant credit and lease term rather than foot traffic.
- E-commerce pressures discretionary retail hardest, while necessity, experiential, and omnichannel retail are more resilient. Format and quality matter more than the “retail” label.

## Tenant Mix and Anchor Quality

An anchor tenant sets the tone at many malls and shopping centers. A healthy grocer, discount retailer, or department store brings traffic that supports inline tenants. A failing or vacant anchor can do the opposite. Co-tenancy clauses can allow inline tenants to reduce rent, switch to percentage rent, or terminate a lease when an anchor closes or occupancy falls below a stated threshold. The loss can spread through the center rather than stop at the empty anchor space.

The mix of inline tenants matters as much as the anchor. Necessity and service tenants—pharmacies, medical users, fitness, food, and salons—are generally more resistant to online substitution than discretionary apparel. Credit quality, lease terms, rents relative to market, and diversification across industries all affect income stability. A strong grocery anchor with needs-based inline tenants is usually more defensive than a troubled department-store anchor with discretionary shops. “Usually” is not a guarantee.

## Evaluating Retail REITs

Start with format and quality tier: trophy versus commodity mall, grocery-anchored versus discretionary center, or net-lease versus traffic-dependent property. Then examine location and demographics. Affluent, growing, supply-constrained trade areas can support traffic and rents, while declining or oversupplied markets can work in the other direction.

Look at occupancy and leasing trends, including leasing spreads on renewals and tenant retention. Assess tenant mix, anchor health, credit quality, and co-tenancy exposure. For a net-lease portfolio, examine weighted-average remaining lease term, rent escalations, and tenant diversification. On the financial side, consider FFO, AFFO, dividend coverage and sustainability, leverage, and the balance sheet. New competing retail supply and redevelopment potential can change the outcome as well. The same core metrics appear across [other REIT sectors](best-reit-sectors-residential-industrial-healthcare-and-more.html), but retail requires careful format and location work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a retail REIT?

A retail REIT is a Real Estate Investment Trust that owns and operates income-producing shopping real estate. It collects rent from tenants and passes much of that income to shareholders as dividends. Like all REITs, it must distribute at least 90% of taxable income. But the category contains very different property types: enclosed regional malls, open-air strip and shopping centers, and single-tenant net-lease buildings. Its risk and resilience depend much more on those formats and property quality than on the word “retail.”

### What are the main types of retail REITs?

There are three broad types. Regional mall REITs own enclosed malls with large anchors and inline tenants; they are the most exposed to e-commerce, though trophy “A” malls have been more resilient than commodity “B” and “C” malls. Strip-center REITs own open-air centers, often grocery-anchored, with needs-based demand that has generally been more resilient. Net-lease REITs own single-tenant freestanding buildings on long triple-net leases; their more bond-like income depends on tenant credit and lease terms rather than traffic. Each format has distinct demand drivers, contracts, and online exposure.

### How do malls and strip centers differ?

Malls are enclosed destination properties with department-store or large-format anchors and a corridor of inline specialty tenants. They depend heavily on anchor health and discretionary foot traffic and have carried much of the e-commerce and department-store pressure. High-quality trophy malls in affluent, supply-constrained markets have held up better than commodity malls in weaker locations.

Strip centers are open-air and convenience-oriented. A grocery anchor creates frequent necessity traffic, while pharmacies, salons, fitness users, and quick-service restaurants benefit from it. That needs-based demand has generally made grocery-anchored centers more defensive and less exposed to online substitution. The quality of the grocer, location, competition, and tenant mix still determine the result.

### What is a net-lease retail REIT?

A net-lease retail REIT owns single-tenant freestanding buildings such as pharmacies, convenience stores, dollar stores, auto-parts stores, and quick-service restaurants. The tenant commonly pays property taxes, insurance, and maintenance under a triple-net lease, in addition to rent. The leases often last 10 to 20 years and include escalations, so the income can look more fixed than mall income. The key variables are tenant credit, remaining term, and escalation terms. Diversifying across tenants, industries, and geographies helps address the concentration risk inherent in one tenant per property.

### How has e-commerce affected retail REITs?

Its effect is uneven. E-commerce has pressured discretionary, shippable goods most heavily, hurting commodity malls and weaker apparel centers through closures, anchor failures, and traffic decline. Necessity and convenience uses such as grocery, pharmacy, and services still need regular in-person trips. Experiential retail—dining, entertainment, and fitness—cannot be shipped. Pickup and fulfillment locations can benefit from omnichannel retail. Current trends continue to change, so check the facts for the portfolio and market in question.

### Are grocery-anchored centers more resilient?

Generally, grocery-anchored centers have been more resilient than many retail formats because grocery stores draw frequent, recurring traffic for necessities. That traffic supports nearby pharmacies, services, fitness users, and quick-service food. Smaller, lower-cost formats may also be easier to re-tenant than a mall department-store box. Resilience is a tendency, not a guarantee. Grocer health, location, competitive alternatives, and the individual tenant mix remain material.

### What makes a trophy mall different from a commodity mall?

A trophy or “A” mall is generally a high-quality property in an affluent, growing, supply-constrained market. It tends to have strong sales per square foot, desirable anchors and tenants, and often an experiential or luxury orientation. Those qualities can support traffic and attractive re-leasing. Commodity “B” and “C” malls are more likely to be in weaker markets, reliant on struggling department-store anchors and discretionary apparel, with lower sales productivity. Both might be called malls, but their futures can be quite different. Verify current portfolio quality and sales data.

### How do I evaluate a retail REIT?

Begin with format and quality, then location and demographics. Review occupancy, leasing spreads, renewal trends, tenant retention, tenant mix, anchor health, credit, and co-tenancy exposure. For net-lease property, check weighted-average lease term, escalations, and diversification. Then examine FFO, AFFO, dividend coverage, leverage, and the balance sheet. Consider new competing supply and redevelopment potential. This is an educational framework, not a recommendation to purchase a specific REIT, and it should be applied using verified current conditions.

### What is a triple-net lease?

In a triple-net, or NNN, lease, the tenant pays rent plus property taxes, building insurance, and maintenance. The structure shifts much of the operating and ownership cost to the tenant and can leave the landlord with relatively predictable income. It is the foundation of many net-lease REITs. Because the lease is commonly 10 to 20 years with escalations, income can feel more like a bond coupon than the variable cash flow of a mall. It is still dependent on actual lease language and tenant credit, both of which must be reviewed.

### Are retail REITs a good income investment?

Retail REITs can be income-oriented because REITs distribute at least 90% of taxable income. Whether a particular one is a sound income holding depends on format and quality. Net-lease retail and grocery-anchored centers can offer more defensive income characteristics than commodity malls. Distributions can be cut if occupancy, rents, or tenant credit deteriorate, and property values can fall. Income is never guaranteed and a retail allocation should be sized and diversified to fit the investor's own goals and risk tolerance.

### What is co-tenancy risk in retail?

Co-tenancy risk comes from lease clauses that connect an inline tenant's obligations to the presence of key anchors or a minimum occupancy level. If an anchor closes or occupancy falls below a threshold, an inline tenant may reduce rent, switch to percentage rent, or terminate. The damage from a failed anchor can therefore move through the center, reducing rents and traffic in a downward spiral. This is why anchor quality and occupancy deserve close attention in mall and shopping-center underwriting.

### How does location affect a retail REIT?

Location is a basic driver of resilience and income. Household incomes, population growth, density, and the competitive environment in a trade area influence tenant sales, traffic, rent, occupancy, and retention. A center in an affluent, growing, supply-constrained market can support better rents and tenants than a similar-looking center in a declining or oversupplied market. Location can also create redevelopment value through alternative or mixed uses. Market conditions change, so current trade-area facts need verification.

### Can retail real estate be redeveloped?

Yes. A well-located but challenged mall or center can be repositioned into mixed use, combining retail with residential, office, hotel, medical, entertainment, or fulfillment use. Replacing a failed department-store anchor with fitness, medical offices, experiential tenants, or apartments can improve an asset. That optionality does not make a challenged property a certain success. Redevelopment requires capital, time, zoning approvals, and execution skill, and not every site has an economically viable alternative use.

### What risks do retail REITs face?

Risk varies by format. E-commerce most affects discretionary shippable goods. Anchor failure, tenant bankruptcy, co-tenancy provisions, vacancy, and re-leasing at lower rents can weaken center income. Properties in declining or oversupplied markets face location and demographic risk. Net-lease portfolios carry single-tenant concentration and tenant-credit risk. Interest rates, leverage, distribution reductions, and—particularly for non-traded REITs—illiquidity apply more broadly. Grocery-anchored, net-lease, and trophy properties can be more defensive, but no format removes the possibility of income or value loss.

## Glossary

- **Retail REIT:** A REIT that owns and operates income-producing retail real estate.
- **Regional Mall:** An enclosed retail property built around anchors plus inline tenants.
- **Strip/Shopping Center:** An open-air retail property, often grocery-anchored and needs-based.
- **Net-Lease Retail:** Single-tenant freestanding retail on long triple-net leases.
- **Triple-Net (NNN) Lease:** A lease where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.
- **Anchor Tenant:** A large tenant that drives traffic to a retail center.
- **Inline Tenant:** A smaller specialty tenant alongside the anchor.
- **Grocery-Anchored:** A center anchored by a grocer, drawing recurring necessity traffic.
- **Trophy / 'A' Mall:** A high-quality mall in a strong market, more resilient.
- **Commodity 'B/C' Mall:** A lower-quality mall in a weaker market, more challenged.
- **Co-Tenancy Clause:** A lease term tying a tenant's rent to anchor or occupancy levels.
- **Sales per Square Foot:** A measure of a retail property's productivity.
- **Occupancy:** The share of leasable retail space currently leased.
- **Leasing Spread:** The change in rent on renewed or re-leased space.
- **Experiential Retail:** Dining, entertainment, and services that resist e-commerce.
- **Redevelopment:** Repositioning retail property into new or mixed uses.
