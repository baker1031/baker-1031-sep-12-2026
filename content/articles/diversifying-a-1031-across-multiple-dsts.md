# Diversifying a 1031 Across Multiple DSTs

*Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep seeing the same decision framed as a choice between one “good” replacement property and a scattered list of smaller ones. That is too simple. When the proceeds from a 1031 exchange go into one property, the investor is also accepting one tenant base, one local market, one sponsor or manager, and one set of surprises.

Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs) can make another approach possible. Their relatively low minimums can let an investor divide one exchange among several fractional interests rather than buy one entire building. That can spread exposure across multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, self-storage, other property types, different metros, and different management teams.

First-order thinking says that more DSTs automatically means a safer exchange. Second-order thinking asks whether the trusts are genuinely different, whether every sponsor and property still clears diligence, whether the investor can complete the identification correctly, and whether the added administration is worth the reduction in concentration. Diversification can reduce a single-property failure point. It cannot eliminate market, interest-rate, structural, or liquidity risk, and it does not promise a return.

## Why diversify an exchange

The case for diversifying a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) is the same as it is elsewhere in investing: do not let one asset decide the outcome for all of the capital. A relinquished property can embody years of appreciation and a large deferred gain. Reinvesting all of that value in a single replacement property leaves the entire exchange exposed to that one property’s tenants, market, and management.

Several DST interests divide that exposure. A tenant default, a soft local market, or poor sponsor execution at one trust affects the portion invested there, not necessarily the entire exchange. That matters because a DST is normally a multi-year, illiquid holding and because a failed or poorly planned exchange has real tax consequences.

The distinction is important: diversification reduces concentration risk; it is not a device for eliminating risk. DSTs can still share broad market, property, financing, sponsor, and structural pressures. Distributions and returns are projections, not guarantees. The practical aim is not to assure a better outcome. It is to avoid allowing one asset, one market, one tenant, or one sponsor to determine the whole result.

## Spread exposure across asset classes

Different property types respond to different demand drivers, lease structures, and risks. DST offerings can include multifamily apartments, industrial and logistics warehouses, net-lease retail—often a single-tenant property on a long lease—medical office and healthcare, self-storage, senior housing, and other specialized sectors. [Asset-class diversification through DSTs](building-a-diversified-dst-portfolio-by-asset-class.html) is useful precisely because those sectors do not move in lockstep.

Net-lease retail and self-storage are often used for defensive-income exposure. Multifamily can combine income with the possibility of rent resets over time. Industrial and logistics property has been associated with e-commerce and distribution demand. Healthcare and senior housing can be linked to demographic trends. None of those descriptions is a forecast or a reason to skip underwriting. Each sector has its own lease terms, tenant exposure, supply conditions, and risk-return profile.

Combining sectors can cushion a portfolio when one weakens. Retail might soften while industrial holds up, for example. The suitable mix depends on whether the investor seeks income, growth, or a balance, and on the investor’s risk tolerance. The simplest way to keep one poor year in one sector from defining the exchange is to make sure the exchange is not invested in only that sector.

## Add geographic and sponsor diversification

Asset class is only one layer. Real estate is intensely local. A metro can be shaped by a dominant industry, one major employer, or its own housing cycle. Putting every DST interest in one market ties the exchange to that market’s fortunes. Allocating among different metros and regions—for example, Sun Belt and Midwest markets—can reduce the chance that a single local downturn drives the entire result. DST offerings can make a national spread more accessible, but widespread economic weakness can still affect many regions at once.

Sponsor diversification is equally practical. The sponsor acquires, finances, manages, and ultimately sells a DST property. Its execution, judgment, financial health, track record, asset-class specialty, and approach can all affect the investment. Using several reputable sponsors can limit the damage from one sponsor’s mistake or financial problem and can provide access to different expertise.

It does not remove the need to review each sponsor. A collection of poorly vetted sponsors is not diversification. The investor still needs to evaluate the specific property, market, lease structure, debt, fee load, sponsor record, and projected—not guaranteed—distribution for every offering.

## Use the identification rules before the clock starts

The wish to own several DSTs meets a hard practical constraint: the 1031 identification rules. The clock is not flexible.

The **three-property rule** allows an exchanger to identify up to three properties of any value within the 45-day identification window. It is straightforward for an allocation of three DSTs or fewer.

For four, five, or more DSTs, the **200% rule** is usually the working rule. It allows any number of identified properties as long as their combined value does not exceed 200% of the value of the relinquished property. Since an investor trying to fully reinvest ordinarily expects to acquire DSTs with a total near the relinquished property’s value, the 200% cap can often accommodate a broad allocation.

The **95% rule** technically allows any number of properties, but it requires the investor to acquire 95% of the value of everything identified. That makes it impractical for most diversified DST plans. Every DST must be clearly described in a written identification delivered to the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) by day 45. The identification plan should therefore be made before the exchange becomes a day-44 problem.

## A sample diversified allocation

Consider a generic illustration, not a recommendation. An investor with exchange proceeds who wants income and stability might put one portion into a multifamily DST in a Sun Belt metro, another into a net-lease retail DST with a creditworthy tenant in a different region, and another into an industrial or logistics DST in a third market. A fourth portion might go to a healthcare or self-storage DST, with another sponsor.

That structure could create exposure to roughly four DSTs, four asset classes, four metros, and several sponsors instead of one property. One tenant default, local downturn, sector slump, or sponsor stumble would not by itself control the whole exchange. Low DST minimums are what make this structure possible: proceeds that might otherwise buy one building can be divided among several trusts.

The example does not dictate an allocation. The number of DSTs and size of each position depend on the investor’s objectives, risk tolerance, available offerings, minimum investment amounts, identification rule, suitability review, and the need to reinvest all equity and replace debt to seek full deferral. It is an illustration, not a recommendation or a promise of results.

## Manage the added moving parts

Several DSTs mean several diligence files. Before the 45-day identification deadline, review the sponsor, property, local market, lease structure, debt, fees, and projected distributions for every DST under consideration. A better process is to build a shortlist before the sale closes, or immediately afterward, rather than rush through multiple offerings when time is shortest. The [45-day identification rules](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) leave little room for late preparation.

Coordination matters as much as selection. The qualified intermediary should receive one clear written identification naming every DST by day 45. The QI then has to move the correct portion of exchange equity into each DST subscription within the 180-day period. The combined allocation has to reinvest the equity and replace the debt necessary for full deferral, so the math should be confirmed with the QI and CPA.

The investor also takes on continuing administration: multiple distribution schedules, reporting cycles, hold periods, and eventual full-cycle sales. That is the trade-off. More administration can be worth it when it produces a more resilient allocation, but it is a real obligation rather than a free benefit.

## Key takeaways

- Multiple DSTs can spread deferred gain across several properties and reduce the concentration risk of a single-property exchange.
- Low DST minimums can allow diversification by asset class, geography, and sponsor.
- The three-property rule fits up to three DSTs; the 200% rule can enable a broader group within its value cap.
- Diversification reduces risk but does not erase shared DST market and structural risks, and returns are never guaranteed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I split one 1031 exchange across multiple DSTs?

Yes. A single 1031 exchange can be allocated across multiple DSTs, and low minimums for fractional beneficial interests can make that practical. The allocation can spread exposure across asset classes, geographies, and sponsors rather than concentrate it in one replacement property. The constraints are the 45-day identification rules, full reinvestment of equity, debt replacement for full deferral, and the need to identify each DST correctly. A qualified intermediary and advisor can help coordinate the structure.

### Why should I diversify my 1031 across several DSTs?

It reduces concentration risk. If the full deferred gain is placed in one property, its tenants, market, and manager or sponsor affect the entire exchange. With several DSTs, a tenant default, weak market, or sponsor problem at one holding affects its slice of the allocation. That can be especially relevant for illiquid, multi-year holdings. It does not eliminate shared market and structural risk or guarantee a return.

### How do low DST minimums make diversification possible?

An investor buys a fractional beneficial interest in a DST rather than an entire building. Because many investors participate in a trust, the minimum investment can be much lower than acquiring a whole property. The proceeds that could purchase one replacement building can therefore be divided—for example, into four portions across different property types, markets, and sponsors. Confirm the actual minimum for each offering before proceeding.

### What asset classes can I diversify across with DSTs?

DSTs can provide multifamily, industrial/logistics, net-lease retail, medical office/healthcare, self-storage, senior-housing, and specialized-sector exposure. Multifamily can balance income and growth; industrial can follow logistics demand; net-lease retail and storage are often used for defensive income; healthcare and senior housing can follow demographics. Each has different leases, demand drivers, and risks. The mix should fit income, growth, balance, and risk-tolerance objectives, and each offering requires review.

### What is geographic diversification in a DST exchange?

It means holding DSTs in different metro areas and regions so that one local economy does not determine the exchange outcome. A market can be affected by a dominant industry, employer, or regional housing cycle. Combining markets, such as several Sun Belt metros and a Midwest market, can cushion a local downturn. It adds a layer alongside asset-class and sponsor diversification, but it cannot prevent broad economic conditions from affecting multiple markets.

### Why does sponsor diversification matter?

The sponsor acquires, finances, manages, and sells the DST property. Its execution, judgment, financial health, management quality, and specialty all matter. Several reputable sponsors can limit the effect of one sponsor’s difficulty and can introduce different experience. Every sponsor still needs its own diligence: track record, financial strength, and history are not made irrelevant by holding more than one sponsor.

### How do the identification rules affect diversifying across DSTs?

They determine how many replacement properties can be identified in the 45-day window. The three-property rule permits up to three DSTs of any value. The 200% rule permits any number, as long as the combined value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property’s value, and it is normally the way to plan a broad multi-DST allocation. The 95% rule allows any number but requires acquisition of 95% of what was identified, which is generally impractical. Identify each DST clearly in writing to the QI by day 45.

### How many DSTs should I diversify across?

There is no universal number. The exchange size, DST minimums, identification rule, need to reinvest equity and replace debt, goals, and diligence capacity all matter. Two or three DSTs fit the three-property rule; four, five, or more typically call for the 200% rule. The objective is enough diversification to reduce meaningful concentration without creating a thin, unwieldy allocation with too many fees, diligence files, or reporting obligations.

### Does diversifying across DSTs guarantee better returns?

No. Diversification can reduce the impact of one underperforming investment and produce a less concentrated result, but it cannot eliminate market, interest-rate, property, or structural risk. A diversified allocation can underperform one well-chosen property, just as it can avoid the damage from one poor property. DST distributions and returns are projections, not promises, and the interests are non-promissory. Use diversification as risk management, not as a performance guarantee.

### Can I diversify across DSTs and still fully defer my gain?

Yes, if the combined allocation satisfies the relevant 1031 requirements. To seek full deferral, reinvest all net equity and replace at least as much debt as was on the relinquished property. DSTs can include non-recourse debt that may count toward replacement debt. The combined allocation must still be identified correctly by day 45. Because equity and debt math across several trusts can be detailed, confirm it with the qualified intermediary and CPA. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### What does a sample diversified DST allocation look like?

An educational example might place portions in a multifamily DST in one metro, a net-lease retail DST with a creditworthy tenant in another, an industrial or logistics DST in a third, and a healthcare or self-storage DST with another sponsor. That could spread an exchange over approximately four DSTs, asset classes, and metros. No single tenant, market, sector, or sponsor dominates. Actual allocations are personal, suitability-dependent, and not recommendations.

### Is there a downside to diversifying across many DSTs?

Yes. Each DST requires sponsor, property, market, lease, debt, and fee diligence within the 45-day window. Every one has its own minimum investment and fees. Broad diversification needs careful 200% rule planning, and multiple DSTs create separate distributions, holding periods, reporting, and sale timelines. These are trade-offs to manage, not reasons to ignore diversification. Confirm the fees and terms of each trust and avoid spreading capital so thinly that the structure becomes difficult to manage.

### Do all DSTs in a diversified allocation have the same risks?

No. DSTs share baseline risks including multi-year illiquidity, sponsor dependence, fees, projected rather than guaranteed distributions and returns, and broad market and interest-rate exposure. Their particular risks vary by asset class, geography, tenant concentration, and debt structure. A net-lease retail trust, for example, has different tenant-credit risk from a multifamily or industrial trust. Diversification is more useful when the holdings are genuinely different, but each needs individual risk review.

### Are sample allocations recommendations?

No. They are generic educational illustrations, not advice to buy particular investments in particular proportions and not an assurance of performance. The right allocation depends on personal finances, goals, liquidity needs, risk tolerance, exchange size, available DSTs, and a suitability review. Because DST interests are securities, an actual recommendation follows a broker-dealer suitability review. Treat samples as teaching tools and confirm each DST’s suitability and risks.

## Glossary

- **Diversified DST Portfolio:** An exchange spread across multiple DSTs to reduce concentration risk.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in which investors own fractional interests.
- **Concentration Risk:** The risk of relying on a single asset, market, tenant, or sponsor.
- **Asset Class:** A category of property type, such as multifamily or industrial.
- **Geographic Diversification:** Spreading investments across different markets and regions.
- **Sponsor Diversification:** Spreading investments across different DST management companies.
- **Sponsor:** The company that acquires, manages, and sells a DST’s property.
- **Minimum Investment:** The smallest amount accepted into a DST offering.
- **Three-Property Rule:** Identify up to three properties of any value.
- **200% Rule:** Identify any number up to 200% of relinquished value.
- **95% Rule:** Identify any number but acquire 95% of the identified value.
- **Net-Lease Retail:** Single-tenant property on a long lease, often defensive income.
- **Debt Replacement:** Matching the relinquished property’s debt to fully defer.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds proceeds and receives the identification.
- **Suitability Review:** Confirming each DST offering fits the investor before investing.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.
