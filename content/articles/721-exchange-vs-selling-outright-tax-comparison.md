# 721 Exchange vs. Selling Outright: A Tax Comparison


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing the sale decision framed as a choice between keeping a building and getting cash. For an owner ready to exit direct property, the more consequential choice is often what happens to the embedded gain on the way out.

Selling outright pays the tax now and leaves after-tax proceeds available for any use. A 721 contribution trades the property for [OP units](op-units-explained.html), defers gain, and moves the full pre-tax value into a REIT. Both end direct ownership and can produce a more passive position, but the tax treatment and destination are very different.

The first-order view is that the 721 preserves more capital. The second-order view is whether preserving tax capital justifies committing to REIT ownership, its risks, and its one-way structure instead of having unrestricted cash. Tax math matters, but it does not answer the whole allocation question.

## The two choices

An outright sale is simple: sell, pay tax on gain, and reinvest the after-tax proceeds in stocks, a REIT, other real estate, a business, or spend them. Its principal benefit is flexibility.

A 721 contribution is more complex. The owner contributes property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, with gain deferred rather than paid at the transition. The investor receives diversified, passive REIT ownership with potentially convertible units. The gain may be recognized on conversion or possibly eliminated through a step-up at death.

The trade-off is therefore simplicity and unrestricted proceeds versus tax deferral and a commitment to REIT ownership.

## Selling outright: immediate tax

Selling triggers the four-layer tax stack: [capital-gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html), up to 20%; [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), up to 25% on prior depreciation; the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html); and state income tax. Together, these often total a third or more of gain.

On a $2,000,000 gain with a combined tax near one-third, the tax might be around $660,000, leaving roughly $1,340,000 to reinvest. Exact results depend on gain, depreciation, income, and state. The immediate tax permanently reduces the capital that can compound for the owner.

## 721 exchange: deferred tax

Under Section 721, contributing property to the REIT operating partnership for OP units generally defers the tax that an outright sale would trigger. The full $2,000,000 in the example moves into the REIT rather than only the $1,340,000 after-tax remainder. Deferred gain is embedded in the units’ basis, recognized on conversion, or may be erased if units are held until death and a step-up applies.

That deferral keeps full pre-tax value invested, as a 1031 does in replacement real estate. It is the core tax advantage over selling, but it comes with ownership of the particular REIT rather than the flexibility of cash.

## The capital difference

The capital difference is the heart of the comparison: $2,000,000 can compound inside the REIT under the 721 example, while $1,340,000 compounds after an outright sale. Assuming comparable returns, the larger base produces more wealth over time, and the gap widens as returns compound.

The deferral does not create a guaranteed investment result. It preserves the amount that otherwise would have been paid in tax, leaving it working in the selected REIT. Actual performance depends on the REIT and the alternative investment.

## Long-term wealth and step-up

If OP units are held until death, a step-up in basis can erase deferred gain. The heirs inherit units with a stepped-up basis, so the tax a sale would have paid upfront may never be paid. For long-term wealth and estate planning, that can make the 721 materially more tax-efficient than a sale.

Selling pays tax now and loses it permanently. A 721 defers the tax and can combine the larger working base with a potential tax elimination at death. Current law and individual estate circumstances must be confirmed with tax and estate advisors.

## When selling outright makes sense

Selling may be the better choice when the owner needs cash now, wants to invest outside a REIT, does not want REIT ownership, has a modest gain, needs a complete liquidation, or lacks a suitable 721 opportunity. In each case, flexibility can be more valuable than deferral.

A partial approach can sometimes work: sell some property for after-tax flexibility and contribute other property through a 721. Feasibility depends on the properties, REIT acceptance, and the owner’s plan.

### Key Takeaways

- An outright sale pays the four-layer tax now and leaves after-tax proceeds; a 721 defers gain and moves full pre-tax value into a REIT.
- On a $2,000,000 gain, roughly $660,000 of tax could leave $1,340,000 to reinvest; a 721 keeps the full value working, subject to its terms and risks.
- The larger pre-tax base can compound over time, and a step-up at death can potentially eliminate deferred gain.
- Selling still fits owners who need cash, value broad investment flexibility, have modest gain, or do not want REIT ownership.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between a 721 exchange and selling outright?

Selling is a taxable sale that produces unrestricted after-tax cash. A 721 contributes property for OP units, defers gain, and commits the owner to REIT ownership. Both exit direct property, but one pays tax for flexibility and the other preserves tax capital for a REIT investment.

### How much tax does selling outright trigger?

The four layers are capital gains up to 20%, recapture up to 25%, 3.8% NIIT, and state tax. They can total a third or more of gain. A $2,000,000 gain could produce about $660,000 in tax and about $1,340,000 to reinvest; actual tax depends on individual facts.

### How does the 721 exchange defer the tax?

Section 721 generally defers gain when property is contributed to a REIT operating partnership for OP units. Full pre-tax value goes into the REIT, with the gain recognized later on conversion or potentially erased by a step-up at death.

### Why does the capital difference matter?

More capital is working: $2,000,000 in the 721 example rather than $1,340,000 after tax. Assuming comparable returns, the larger base compounds to more wealth over time. Performance is not guaranteed.

### Does the step-up make the 721 better than selling?

For long-term estate planning, it can. Holding units until death may permit a step-up that erases deferred gain, while selling pays the tax immediately. The rules and estate circumstances need professional confirmation.

### When does selling outright make more sense?

When the owner needs fully liquid cash, wants other investments, dislikes REIT ownership, has a modest gain, or lacks a suitable 721 opportunity. Flexibility can outweigh tax deferral.

### Is a 721 always more tax-efficient than selling?

On pure tax math, it generally defers tax and may eliminate it through a step-up, so it is usually more tax-efficient for long-term wealth. It is not automatically the better overall choice because a sale offers flexibility and avoids commitment to a REIT.

### Can I sell part and 721 part?

Potentially. Selling one portion can create cash and a 721 contribution of another can preserve deferral. The approach depends on property facts, REIT acceptance, and advice from the owner’s professionals.

### Does selling give me more investment options than a 721?

Yes. After-tax cash can be used for stocks, bonds, other real estate, a business, or spending. A 721 directs value into a specific REIT. That is the central flexibility-versus-deferral trade-off.

### How do I decide between a 721 and selling?

Quantify tax with a CPA, then compare deferral, larger working capital, and possible step-up benefits with the need for unrestricted proceeds and the attractiveness of REIT ownership. Goals, not tax alone, should control the decision.

### Is a 721 exchange or selling simpler?

Selling is generally simpler: sell, pay tax, and reinvest. A 721 has partnership, securities, tax, documentation, and ongoing-unit-ownership complexity. The question is whether deferral justifies that complexity.

### How does the income from each compare?

Sale income depends on reinvestment of the reduced after-tax proceeds. A 721 can pay OP-unit distributions on the full pre-tax value in the REIT. At comparable yields, the larger base can produce more income, but actual income depends on the REIT’s yield and alternative investments.

### Does selling let me avoid REIT risk?

Yes. Selling permits reinvestment outside the particular REIT and avoids its management, performance, and share-price risks. A 721 ties the outcome to that REIT, even though its portfolio is diversified. Avoiding that risk costs the immediate tax.

### Can I sell to a REIT and still defer via a 721?

No. Cash from a REIT is a taxable sale. Deferral requires a contribution to the operating partnership for OP units. Cash versus units determines the tax treatment.

## Glossary

- **Selling Outright:** A taxable property sale that pays tax now.
- **721 Exchange:** A property contribution for OP units that defers tax.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax triggered by sale.
- **Immediate Tax:** Tax paid now in an outright sale.
- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing tax through a 721 contribution.
- **After-Tax Proceeds:** What remains to reinvest after sale and tax.
- **Full Pre-Tax Value:** Value transitioned to the REIT through a 721.
- **Capital Difference:** Full 721 capital versus after-tax sale capital.
- **Compounding:** Returns earning returns, favoring the larger 721 base.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Death-time reset that may eliminate deferred gain.
- **Flexibility:** Unrestricted use of sale proceeds.
- **REIT Ownership:** The 721 destination that a sale does not require.
- **Long-Term Wealth:** The possible compounding advantage from deferral.
- **Modest Gain:** A smaller gain for which sale tax is less significant.
- **Partial Approach:** Selling some property and using 721 for other property.
- **Tax Efficiency:** 721’s ability to defer and possibly eliminate tax.

## 721 exchange vs. selling outright at a glance

| Factor | 721 Exchange | Sell Outright |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax at transaction | Deferred via the 721 contribution | Capital-gains tax due immediately |
| Capital preserved | Full value continues working | Only after-tax proceeds |
| What you receive | REIT operating-partnership units | Cash |
| Diversification | Into a broad REIT portfolio | Depends on cash redeployment |
| Liquidity | Semi-liquid; conversion to shares is taxable | Fully liquid immediately |
| Estate planning | OP units may receive a step-up at death | No deferred gain remains |
| Best when | You want capital invested and diversified | You need cash or want to fully exit |

## Which should you choose?

A 721 preserves capital that an outright sale would erode through immediate tax, lets the full amount compound in a diversified REIT, and may permit a step-up at death for heirs.

Selling is cleaner when cash is needed now, the owner wants to redeploy outside real estate, or embedded gain is small enough that the deferral’s value does not justify the complexity of a sale alternative.
