# The 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax on Real Estate

I keep seeing a sale analysis stop at the long-term capital-gains rate. It is an understandable shortcut. But for a higher-income real-estate investor, the answer can be short by 3.8 points.

That additional federal layer is the net investment income tax, or NIIT. It can apply to gain, rents, royalties, and REIT dividends. Its thresholds have been frozen since 2013, so inflation and wage growth can bring more sellers into the tax in 2026.

First-order thinking says, “My gain is taxed at 20%.” Second-order thinking asks what is recognized this year, where MAGI lands, which income falls inside the NIIT base, and whether a deferral changes the result. That broader view does not turn a tax strategy into an investment thesis. The price, financing, income risk, and liquidity of a replacement still have to earn their place.

## Key takeaways

- The NIIT is a flat **3.8%** surtax on the **lesser** of net investment income or the amount modified AGI exceeds the relevant threshold.
- The thresholds are frozen rather than inflation-indexed: **$200,000** for single and head-of-household filers, **$250,000** for married taxpayers filing jointly, and **$125,000** for married taxpayers filing separately.
- Capital gains on rentals, depreciation recapture, passive rent, royalties, dividends, and interest can count. Wages, active business income, municipal-bond interest, IRA distributions, and qualifying §121 home-sale gain do not.
- The real estate professional exception can remove rental income and gain from NIIT treatment, but its tests are demanding and most passive investors will not satisfy them.
- A **1031 exchange defers the gain and the 3.8% surtax** until recognition later. A step-up in basis at death can eliminate the deferred liability.
- A **DST** is a passive 1031 option. Its income and gain generally remain NIIT income for an accredited passive investor above the threshold.

## What the NIIT actually is

Congress enacted the net investment income tax in 2010 under Section 1411 of the tax code as part of the Affordable Care Act; it took effect for tax years beginning in 2013. It is often called the Medicare surtax, although its proceeds do not flow into the Medicare trust funds in the same way as payroll Medicare tax. For a taxpayer, the mechanics are the point: it is a flat 3.8% charge on top of regular income tax and capital-gains tax, computed on **Form 8960** and carried to **Form 1040**.

The most common mistake is to treat NIIT as 3.8% of all investment income. It is not. The tax applies to the lesser of two figures: net investment income for the year, or the amount by which modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds the filing-status threshold.

That distinction matters at the margin. A taxpayer who barely crosses the line pays NIIT on only the small excess, even with a substantial investment-income base. Someone far above it can pay on all of that net investment income. A one-time sale of an appreciated rental frequently does both: it adds a large gain to MAGI and adds a large block of investment income.

Consider a married couple with $230,000 of MAGI from salary and modest interest. A $400,000 long-term rental gain takes MAGI to $630,000, or $380,000 above the $250,000 joint threshold. If net investment income is roughly the $400,000 gain plus interest, the lesser figure is $380,000. At 3.8%, that is about $14,440 of NIIT on top of capital-gains tax. The example is illustrative, not a projection for any transaction.

## Who owes it and the frozen thresholds

NIIT thresholds are written into the statute and are not indexed for inflation. They are the same in 2026 as when the tax began in 2013.

| Filing status | MAGI threshold | Inflation-indexed? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Single | $200,000 | No, frozen since 2013 |
| Head of household | $200,000 | No, frozen since 2013 |
| Married filing jointly | $250,000 | No, frozen since 2013 |
| Qualifying surviving spouse | $250,000 | No, frozen since 2013 |
| Married filing separately | $125,000 | No, frozen since 2013 |

These are MAGI thresholds, not taxable-income thresholds, and they apply per return. For most domestic real-estate investors, MAGI is adjusted gross income plus a limited set of foreign-income add-backs. It is generally the bottom-line AGI on the front of the 1040 before the standard or itemized deduction. A deduction that lowers taxable income does not necessarily lower MAGI.

Because the thresholds do not rise, ordinary income growth creates quiet bracket creep. A two-income household near the $250,000 joint line can cross it when a rental or brokerage account produces gain. For a real-estate investor, a single profitable sale can almost guarantee the surtax for that tax year unless the gain is deferred or restructured. The $125,000 married-filing-separately threshold is especially easy to reach. A CPA should work from the taxpayer’s own return rather than apply a headline rate.

## What counts as net investment income, and what does not

Net investment income is defined, not a catch-all for every type of return.

| Counts as net investment income | Does not count |
| --- | --- |
| Taxable interest | Wages and salary |
| Dividends, including REIT dividends | Self-employment income |
| Capital gains, including investment-real-estate gain | Active trade-or-business income |
| Depreciation recapture as part of rental-sale gain | Tax-exempt municipal-bond interest |
| Net passive rental income | Qualified-plan and IRA distributions |
| Royalty income from mineral and similar interests | Gain excluded under §121 on a primary residence |
| Non-qualified annuity income | Social Security benefits |
| Income from passive businesses without material participation | Income from a non-passive trade or business |

The classification of a particular item depends on the facts and the investor’s participation. Depreciation recapture deserves special attention. It is part of the gain, not an exempt category. The part of rental-sale gain tied to prior depreciation is generally unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, taxed federally at up to 25%, and the 3.8% surtax can apply as well. See [depreciation recapture on real estate](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html).

The §121 primary-residence exclusion is different. Qualifying excluded gain—up to $250,000 for a single filer or $500,000 for a joint filer—never enters gross income, so it never reaches NIIT. Gain above the exclusion can. For the underlying gain mechanics, see [how capital gains tax on real estate works](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html).

Active business income and self-employment income sit outside NIIT. That does not mean they escape surtax altogether; they can face the 0.9% additional Medicare tax on earned income. A dollar is not subject to both regimes. The passive-versus-active distinction is also central to the real estate professional exception.

## The real estate professional exception

Rental income is passive by default, and passive income is normally inside the NIIT base. The route to different treatment is to establish non-passive trade-or-business income, which is where the Section 469 real estate professional rules matter.

The taxpayer must satisfy both tests in the same year:

- More than **750 hours** of personal services in real property trades or businesses in which the taxpayer materially participates.
- More than **half** of all personal-service time in a trade or business during the year must be in those real property trades or businesses.

Those are entry tests, not the whole answer. The taxpayer must also materially participate in the particular rental activities, and the activity must rise to a trade or business rather than a passive holding. A full-time professional in another field will often have trouble with the more-than-half test. A retiree with a brokerage account and a few triple-net leases may have trouble with the trade-or-business and material-participation requirements. The status is more realistic for full-time operators, brokers, developers, and property managers, and it requires contemporaneous hours records because it is frequently audited.

The practical assumption for most passive owners, including passive DST holders alongside a W-2 job, is that rental income and gain are inside NIIT. Treat the exception as a CPA question, not an assumption. See [active versus passive real estate investing](active-vs-passive-real-estate-investing.html).

## How a 1031 exchange interacts with the NIIT

NIIT reaches gain that is recognized. A qualifying [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) defers the capital gain on investment real estate into replacement property. Because that gain is not recognized in the year of the exchange, it does not create net investment income for NIIT. The 3.8% tax is deferred with it.

This has its greatest force in a large one-time sale, the same event that otherwise lifts MAGI past the threshold. The deferred NIIT becomes a future potential liability when replacement property is sold in a taxable transaction. If exchanges continue, deferral continues.

The long view deserves equal weight. A step-up in basis at death can erase the deferred liability: appreciated property generally receives a basis reset to fair market value, leaving no recognized capital gain and no NIIT on that appreciation. “Swap till you drop” describes repeated exchanges until that basis step-up. It is not a guaranteed outcome. Tax law can change, and a failed exchange recognizes gain.

Deferral is not the only tool. Sale timing in a lower-income year, capital-loss harvesting, and deductible retirement contributions can keep MAGI nearer the threshold and reduce the exposed slice. See [tax deferral strategies](tax-deferral-strategies-compared.html) and [reducing capital gains tax on investment property](how-to-avoid-capital-gains-tax-investment-property.html). A CPA should calculate the trade-offs before a sale is committed.

## How a DST interacts with the NIIT

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) is a passive way to complete a 1031 exchange. Rather than purchase and manage property directly, an investor can exchange into a fractional beneficial interest in a trust holding institutional real estate. That exchange defers the original gain, and the NIIT on that relinquished-property gain, just as a direct exchange does.

The ongoing DST treatment is separate. Because a DST is passive by design, its beneficiary does not materially participate. DST rental distributions and eventual gain when the trust property is sold are generally net investment income for an accredited passive investor whose MAGI exceeds the threshold. A DST cannot make an investor a real estate professional and does not exempt the new income stream from NIIT.

The value of a DST in this context is the deferral it preserves, not an ongoing surtax exemption. An eventual DST exit may be rolled into another 1031 exchange or, in some programs, a 721 exchange into a REIT, extending deferral. DSTs remain speculative, illiquid securities sold only to verified accredited investors through a private placement memorandum. They can lose principal. Tax mechanics do not change that risk.

## Putting the calculation together

For a top-bracket seller of appreciated investment real estate, the federal stack can include a 20% long-term capital-gains rate, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain taxed up to 25%, and the 3.8% NIIT to the extent the lesser-of test reaches the gain. State income tax can be added after that. The familiar 20% headline can become 23.8% federally before state tax and before recapture.

Two refinements keep an estimate honest. First, NIIT applies to the lesser of net investment income or excess MAGI, so a seller who only barely crosses the threshold may owe much less than 3.8% of the entire gain. Second, it is computed on the year’s total: interest, dividends, and passive rents can increase the investment-income base. The calculation belongs with a CPA before a binding sale decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the 3.8% NIIT charged on top of capital gains tax?

Yes. It is a separate surtax. A top-bracket long-term gain can face 23.8% federally—20% plus 3.8%—before state tax and depreciation recapture.

### Are the NIIT thresholds adjusted for inflation each year?

No. The $200,000 single and head-of-household, $250,000 joint, and $125,000 married-filing-separately thresholds have been frozen since 2013. Inflation and wage growth therefore bring more taxpayers across them over time.

### Does a 1031 exchange avoid the net investment income tax?

It defers rather than avoids it outright. A properly structured 1031 exchange defers gain recognition, so there is no NIIT on that deferred sale gain in the exchange year. It can apply later upon a taxable sale. A step-up in basis at death can erase the deferred liability.

### Is rental income subject to the NIIT?

Usually, if the rental is passive to the owner. A Section 469 real estate professional who materially participates may have non-passive rental income excluded from net investment income, but most passive investors do not meet those tests.

### Is depreciation recapture subject to the 3.8% surtax?

Generally yes. Recapture is part of rental-sale gain, not a separate exempt category. It is unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, taxed federally at up to 25%, and NIIT can apply to the extent the lesser-of test does.

### Is income from a DST subject to the NIIT?

Generally yes. A DST is a passive 1031 investment, so its distributions and eventual gain are generally net investment income for an accredited passive investor above the MAGI threshold. The original exchanged gain remains deferred; the DST does not exempt its new income stream.

### Does selling my primary home trigger the NIIT?

Only on gain above the §121 exclusion. Up to $250,000 single or $500,000 joint of qualifying excluded gain never enters income. Excess gain can face NIIT if MAGI is above the threshold.

### How do I lower my exposure to the NIIT in a year I sell?

The principal levers are gain deferral and MAGI management. A 1031 exchange or DST defers the gain and surtax with it. Sale timing, capital-loss harvesting, and deductible retirement contributions may reduce excess MAGI. Run the actual facts with a CPA before acting.

## Glossary

- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A flat 3.8% Section 1411 surtax on the lesser of net investment income or excess MAGI, reported on Form 8960.
- **Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI):** Adjusted gross income with limited foreign-income add-backs; for many domestic investors it is AGI and is the figure tested against NIIT thresholds.
- **Net Investment Income:** Interest, dividends, capital gains, passive rental income, royalties, and similar returns less allocable expenses; wages and active business income are excluded.
- **Real Estate Professional:** A taxpayer meeting the more-than-750-hours and more-than-half real-property service tests and materially participating in rental activity.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** The portion of rental-sale gain attributable to prior depreciation, generally unrecaptured Section 1250 gain taxed at up to 25% and generally inside NIIT.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive 1031 replacement vehicle holding institutional real estate. It defers original gain, while its income and eventual gain are generally investment income for passive investors.
- **Section 121 Exclusion:** A qualifying primary-residence exclusion of up to $250,000 single or $500,000 joint; excluded gain never enters NIIT.
- **Form 8960:** The IRS form used to compute and report NIIT before it carries to Form 1040.
