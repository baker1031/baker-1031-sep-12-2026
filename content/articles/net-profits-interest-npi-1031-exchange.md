# Net Profits Interest (NPI): Does It Qualify for 1031?

**Mineral & Royalty · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep coming back to the document before the story around an oil and gas interest. A net profits interest can sound close enough to a royalty to make the answer feel obvious. The terms usually decide whether it is close enough for a 1031 exchange.

An NPI pays its holder a share of a mineral property’s net profits: gross revenue less specified costs. It is cost-free to the holder in the sense that the holder is not personally liable for operating expenses, but its payment depends on the property’s profitability. That places it between a royalty and a working interest.

First-order thinking starts with the label “NPI” and assumes a familiar mineral interest should qualify. Second-order thinking reads the calculation, the term, the carve-out, state-law characterization, and whether the interest is a continuing stake in real property or merely a right to payments. That is the difference between a defensible exchange and one that can be disallowed with full tax and penalties. This is general education, not tax or legal advice.

For context on interests that qualify more cleanly, see [the complete mineral rights and royalty 1031 guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html).

## What is a net profits interest?

A net profits interest is a share of the net profits from an oil and gas property, carved out of a working interest or mineral interest. The NPI holder receives an agreed percentage of revenue after the costs specified in the instrument are deducted. Those commonly include operating costs and may include a share of development costs. The holder does not personally fund those costs; they are netted from revenue before the payment is calculated.

That makes the NPI a hybrid. Like a royalty holder, the NPI holder never receives a bill or a capital call. If there are no net profits, the worst result for that period is no payment, not an out-of-pocket cost. Like a working-interest owner, however, the holder’s payment rises and falls with the cost side of the operation. The NPI holder participates in the property’s profitability, not simply gross production.

NPIs are used to compensate a party, share upside without operating liability, or monetize part of a property’s economics. Their terms can differ substantially. Cost definitions, duration, and the way the interest was carved out all vary. Eligibility depends on those facts, not on the “NPI” label.

## NPI vs. royalty vs. working interest

A royalty interest pays a share of gross production, or its value, free of operating costs. It is the most passive of these interests and is usually the clearest real-property case. A working interest bears operating costs, capital calls, and operational and environmental liability; it receives revenue after royalties and combines real property with taxable equipment.

The NPI lies between them. It is cost-free to its holder like a royalty but cost-sensitive in its calculation like a working interest. A gross-royalty owner receives a percentage off the top regardless of cost. An NPI holder sees payment shrink as costs rise and expand as costs fall. The payment is therefore more volatile and more dependent on operating efficiency.

The NPI holder does not take on the working-interest owner’s capital, operational, or environmental obligations. It captures some of the profit-sharing without those burdens. That economic attraction is also why its classification is a genuine question rather than a settled answer.

## Is an NPI a real-property interest?

The core 1031 question is whether the NPI is an interest in real property. Oil and gas interests carved from a mineral or leasehold estate may be real property under state law. An NPI that functions as [a continuing interest in the production of real property](perpetual-interest-rule-oil-gas-real-property.html) can qualify on that basis.

The favorable case is a perpetual NPI carved from a mineral or leasehold estate that works economically like a royalty with a cost adjustment. It looks more like the continuing real-property interests that the like-kind rules recognize.

The case weakens when the instrument looks more like a contract or financing arrangement. An elaborate net-profits calculation, a stated term or cap, or a right that is substantively a defined stream of payments may resemble a production payment—treated as debt under Section 636—or a mere contract right. Neither is like-kind real estate.

There is no blanket answer because state-law and federal-tax characterization both matter and NPI terms vary widely. Perpetual royalties generally qualify cleanly. Production payments do not. A particular NPI must be read against the authorities, and a professional opinion is essential.

## 1031 treatment and the open questions

Where an NPI is perpetual, comes from a real-property mineral or leasehold estate, and functions as a cost-adjusted royalty, the better view is generally that it can be exchanged for other real property. That may include another royalty, conventional investment real estate, or a DST. Practitioners often reason by analogy to royalties and [overriding royalty interests](overriding-royalty-interest-orri-1031-exchange.html).

The open questions deserve real weight. A term-limited NPI can be different from a perpetual one. A cost computation that resembles a financing arrangement can be different from a simpler operating-cost adjustment. State law may characterize a particular instrument differently. Nor is there direct, on-point IRS guidance for every NPI arrangement.

This uncertainty is itself an investment and tax risk. A tax adviser’s written opinion should address the specific instrument and state the degree of confidence behind the conclusion. A strongly qualifying NPI can support an exchange with more comfort. A borderline NPI creates a meaningful chance the IRS disagrees, leaving the owner with a disallowed exchange, full tax, and penalties. The time to understand that spectrum is before selling.

## Documenting the exchange

With an NPI, the source instrument is central evidence. Preserve the definition of net profits, the term, the way the interest was carved out, and the underlying real-property interest from which it derives. A tax adviser should compare the instrument with the relevant authorities and issue an opinion on whether the NPI is exchangeable real property, and with what confidence. That opinion is the foundation of a defensible exchange.

Then execute ordinary 1031 mechanics without shortcuts. Engage a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) before the sale closes so the seller does not take constructive receipt. Have the QI assigned into the contract, identify like-kind replacement property in writing within 45 days, and close within 180 days. Reinvest fully to avoid [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html). Identify a fast-closing backup, route trailing income carefully, and keep a defensible valuation. An NPI exchange can receive more scrutiny than a routine exchange, so every step belongs in the file.

The replacement property needs the same rigor. One NPI is not automatically like-kind to another merely because both are profit-linked. The replacement interest must itself qualify. Many holders exchange into a cleaner asset—a perpetual royalty, conventional real estate, or a DST—to remove characterization uncertainty going forward. The instrument, tax opinion, QI agreement, identification record, valuation, and closing file together demonstrate clean mechanics.

## Key Takeaways

- An NPI pays a share of net, not gross, profits. It is cost-free to the holder but cost-sensitive in the calculation.
- It is a hybrid of a royalty and a working interest, so its real-property characterization is fact-specific.
- A well-structured perpetual NPI over real property can qualify; a term-limited or financing-like interest may not.
- Base an exchange on a written tax opinion and thorough records. A cleaner replacement asset may be worth considering.

## Alternatives if your NPI doesn't qualify

If a tax adviser concludes that the NPI is not exchangeable real property—because it is term-limited, financing-like, or a contract right under the relevant state law—a direct 1031 exchange is unavailable. One option is a deliberately planned taxable sale, perhaps timed to manage a tax bracket or paired with offsetting losses under a CPA’s guidance.

Another is restructuring before the sale. In some circumstances, counsel may be able to modify an interest or structure a transaction so a qualifying real-property interest is exchanged. That work must be done well in advance and on sound legal advice. A non-qualifying interest cannot be retroactively converted after the sale.

Other tools may help with the taxable portion. An [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) can spread gain across the years in which payments are received. A Qualified Opportunity Fund can defer gain from any source by reinvesting the gain, not the full proceeds. Charitable strategies may reduce tax for owners with philanthropic intent. None is a 1031 exchange, and each carries its own rules and trade-offs. Knowing whether the NPI is qualifying, borderline, or non-qualifying before a sale makes it possible to choose deliberately instead of discovering the problem on the tax return.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a net profits interest (NPI)?

It is a percentage of an oil and gas property’s net profits—revenue minus specified costs—carved out of a working or mineral interest. The holder is not personally liable for those costs, but payment changes with them. It is cost-free to the holder like a royalty and cost-sensitive in its calculation like a working interest.

### Does a net profits interest qualify for a 1031 exchange?

It depends on the structure. A perpetual NPI carved from a real-property interest and functioning like a cost-adjusted royalty can qualify as exchangeable real property. A term-limited NPI or one that resembles financing may not. Eligibility is instrument-specific and needs a tax adviser’s opinion.

### How is an NPI different from a royalty?

A gross royalty pays a percentage of production off the top, regardless of costs. An NPI pays a percentage after specified costs are deducted, so payment falls when costs rise and increases when they fall. Both are cost-free to their holders, but the NPI is more dependent on property profitability and can be more volatile.

### How is an NPI different from a working interest?

A working interest bears costs and capital calls and includes operational and environmental liability. An NPI holder bears none of those obligations; costs are only netted from revenue before the NPI share is calculated. The NPI therefore has some profit-sharing features without the working-interest burdens.

### Why is the NPI's 1031 status uncertain?

It is a hybrid interest. The result depends on duration, the net-profits calculation, state-law treatment, and whether the economics resemble a continuing real-property interest or a contractual/financing right. No authority supplies a blanket answer for every NPI structure.

### When is an NPI most likely to qualify?

The favorable case is a perpetual interest carved from a mineral or leasehold real-property estate that operates like a cost-adjusted royalty rather than a defined payment stream. That combination more closely resembles the real-property interests that like-kind rules accept.

### When might an NPI not qualify?

Concern rises when the interest is term-limited or capped, when the computation resembles a production payment or loan under §636, when it is simply a contract right, or when state law does not treat it as real property. A careful opinion is necessary before an exchange proceeds; in some cases it may not be available.

### Do I need a tax opinion to exchange an NPI?

It is strongly advisable and, in practical terms, essential. The written opinion analyzes the particular instrument, tells the owner whether it qualifies and with what confidence, and identifies the risk being accepted. Its cost is small beside the tax and penalties from a disallowed exchange.

### Can I exchange an NPI for real estate or a DST?

If the relinquished NPI qualifies as real property, yes. It can be like-kind to conventional investment real estate or a DST. A perpetual royalty, real estate, or DST can be a cleaner replacement that removes the NPI’s characterization question. Both the relinquished and replacement property must qualify.

### Can I exchange one NPI for another?

Potentially, but each must be analyzed. Two profit-linked interests are not automatically like-kind. The relinquished NPI and the replacement NPI must both qualify as real property. Given the uncertainty, many owners prefer a cleaner replacement.

### How should I document an NPI exchange?

Retain the creating instrument, the tax adviser’s opinion, the qualified-intermediary agreement, written identification, defensible valuation, and closing records. The records should show that a qualifying interest moved through clean exchange mechanics. NPI exchanges can attract more scrutiny than ordinary exchanges.

### Is an NPI exchange riskier than a royalty exchange?

From an eligibility perspective, usually yes. A perpetual royalty has more settled authority, while an NPI depends on specific terms. The added uncertainty is why a written opinion, careful records, and sometimes a cleaner replacement are important.

### What if my NPI doesn't qualify for a 1031?

Plan the taxable sale deliberately, consider a pre-sale restructuring with counsel, or assess other tools for the taxable portion: an installment sale, Qualified Opportunity Fund, or charitable strategy. The choice depends on the facts, and learning status before the sale is what permits a considered path.

### Can I restructure a non-qualifying NPI to qualify?

Sometimes, with careful planning and legal advice well before a sale. An interest may be modified or a transaction structured so qualifying real property is exchanged. It cannot be done retroactively after closing, and the uncertainty means it should be pursued only on sound counsel.

### Does the 'net profits' computation affect eligibility?

Yes. A complicated calculation that deducts development and capital costs in a way that resembles financing or a fixed payment stream weakens the real-property case. A simpler calculation that nets operating costs while leaving a royalty-like continuing interest can support it.

### Can I exchange an NPI into a royalty-pool DST?

If the NPI qualifies as real property, yes. A royalty-pool DST can be like-kind replacement property, can diversify across many interests, and may remove the uncertainty of holding another NPI. The original NPI still needs to qualify.

### How does state law affect my NPI's 1031 status?

State law determines whether the NPI is characterized as a real-property interest, which is the federal starting point. Oil and gas property law varies by state, so the same structure may receive different treatment depending on the location of the minerals. An adviser’s opinion should account for that law.

### Is there IRS guidance specifically on NPIs and 1031?

There is authority addressing royalties and production payments, but not clean, direct guidance for every NPI structure. That gap requires a case-by-case analysis of existing authorities and a written professional opinion before exchange.

### Should I just sell my NPI and pay the tax?

It depends on the gain and the confidence in the eligibility opinion. A clearly qualifying NPI and meaningful deferral can support an exchange. A modest gain and borderline eligibility can make a deliberate taxable sale simpler and less risky than an aggressive position the IRS could challenge. Model both paths with a CPA.

## Glossary

**Net Profits Interest (NPI).** A share of an oil and gas property’s net profits, revenue minus specified costs, carved from a working or mineral interest.

**Net Profits.** Property revenue minus the costs specified in the NPI instrument, the basis for the holder’s share.

**Royalty Interest.** A cost-free share of gross production, free of operating costs; the clearest mineral real-property interest.

**Working Interest.** The operating interest that bears costs and liability; real property plus taxable equipment.

**Production Payment.** A right to a specified sum or volume of production, treated as a loan under IRC §636 rather than real property.

**Real Property Interest.** An ownership interest in land or minerals; the only property eligible for 1031 since 2017.

**Perpetual Interest.** An open-ended interest with the durational character of a real-property fee estate.

**Term Interest.** An interest limited to a fixed period or quantity that may not be like-kind to fee real property.

**Like-Kind.** The requirement that exchanged property share the character of real property held for investment.

**Tax Opinion.** A professional adviser’s written analysis of whether a specific interest qualifies for like-kind treatment.

**Section 636.** The Code section treating mineral production payments as loans rather than real-property interests.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI).** The independent party that holds exchange proceeds so the seller does not take constructive receipt.

**Constructive Receipt.** Access to or control over proceeds that disqualifies an exchange.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST).** A securitized fractional interest in institutional real estate that qualifies as 1031 replacement property.

**Carve-Out.** Creation of an NPI or override by separating it from a larger working or mineral interest.

**Trailing Income.** Production proceeds for pre-closing periods received after sale that need careful routing.
