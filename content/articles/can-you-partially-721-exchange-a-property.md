# Can You Partially 721 Exchange a Property?

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have noticed that the all-or-nothing framing around a 721 exchange is often the thing that makes an otherwise interested owner hesitate. They may want some passivity and some diversification, but not a complete exit from direct real estate.

A partial 721 exchange can provide a middle path. The owner contributes some real estate to a REIT in return for OP units and retains the rest in direct ownership. That may mean contributing selected properties from a portfolio while keeping others, or, less commonly, contributing a partial interest in one property. The contributed portion can receive the 721’s deferral, passive ownership, diversification, and estate-planning features. The retained portion preserves direct control, its own income and appreciation, and future 1031 flexibility.

The details matter. A partial approach does not erase the risks of OP units, the one-way loss of 1031 eligibility on the contributed assets, or the possibility of loss of principal. It simply allows an owner to decide how much of the portfolio takes on those features. This guide explains what a partial 721 exchange is, how it is commonly structured, how tax treatment is allocated, and how it compares with a full transition.

## What a partial 721 exchange means

A partial [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) is an approach rather than a separately defined tax transaction. The owner contributes part of their real estate to a REIT and keeps the balance in direct ownership.

The cleanest form arises when an owner has several properties. They can contribute selected whole properties to the REIT and retain other whole properties. Each property is handled separately: the contributed properties enter the REIT structure and the retained properties do not.

An owner with a single property may try to contribute only a partial interest. That is possible in principle, but more involved. The REIT must be willing to accept and accommodate a partial interest, which introduces co-ownership arrangements and more structuring work. It is less common than contributing whole properties from a larger portfolio.

The practical meaning is straightforward: partial does not require choosing between “contribute everything” and “contribute nothing.” It means choosing an amount of direct real estate to transition into REIT ownership while deliberately retaining another amount.

## Keep some, contribute some

The contributed portion receives the features associated with a 721 exchange. Its built-in gain is generally deferred into OP units. The owner receives exposure to the REIT portfolio, gives up active management of that portion, and may receive estate-planning benefits including [step-up in basis and divisibility](721-exchange-estate-planning.html) for the units.

The retained portion remains direct real estate. The owner keeps control over it, retains the income and appreciation that come from it, and can use a 1031 exchange on that property later if the applicable requirements are met. None of those tax or ownership attributes changes merely because a different asset was contributed to a REIT.

First-order thinking treats a partial exchange as a smaller full exchange. Second-order thinking sees two different capital structures operating side by side. The contributed part has REIT-related benefits and constraints; the retained part has direct-property control and ongoing 1031 optionality. This can be a deliberate balance rather than an incomplete decision.

## Tax treatment of the contributed and retained portions

[Section 721 generally applies to the property that is contributed](section-721-exchange-tax-code-behind-upreit-conversions.html). The owner does not generally recognize gain on that property at contribution; the gain is deferred into the [OP units](op-units-explained.html), as it would be in a full 721 exchange. The contributed portion therefore receives the Section 721 deferral.

The tax position of the retained property is unchanged. It retains its existing basis and ownership treatment because it has not been disposed of. No gain is recognized on that property merely because another property, or another interest, was contributed to the REIT.

Debt needs its own analysis. If the contributed property has debt, the relevant [deemed-distribution considerations](721-exchange-and-existing-debt-on-your-property.html) apply to that contributed portion, just as they would in a full 721 exchange involving debt. A CPA and attorney should evaluate basis, debt, and the transaction structure for the particular contributed assets.

The central allocation is simple in concept: Section 721 deferral applies to what is contributed; the retained portion stays where it was. The execution can still be technical, especially where the owner seeks to transfer a partial interest in a single asset rather than selected whole properties.

## Why owners use a partial approach

Owners may seek a partial 721 exchange for four recurring reasons.

First, it preserves control and flexibility. Retaining direct property means retaining control over that property and its future 1031 flexibility. It can hedge the one-way commitment created when an owner receives OP units. See [the 721 one-way-door limitation](721-exchange-one-way-door-losing-1031-eligibility.html).

Second, the owner may want to keep a particular property for sentimental, strategic, income, or other reasons. A partial approach allows selected assets to transition while the valued property remains in direct ownership.

Third, it can be a staged transition. An owner can move some properties into a REIT today and consider additional exchanges later rather than changing the entire portfolio at once. The incremental approach can make sense for someone who wants to ease into REIT ownership.

Fourth, retained property can reduce concentration in a single REIT. Keeping some real estate outside the REIT spreads exposure between the REIT and direct property rather than placing all transitioned real estate in a single issuer. That is not a removal of risk; it is a different allocation of risk. An owner should still evaluate [the REIT behind the 721 exchange](evaluating-the-reit-behind-a-721-exchange.html).

## How to structure the partial contribution

For a portfolio owner, contributing whole properties and retaining others is generally the cleanest arrangement. It avoids dividing a single title and lets the parties consider each property individually: which assets fit the REIT and which ones does the owner prefer to keep?

The selection should address REIT fit, ownership objectives, taxes, and debt. Properties that are institutional-quality, well located, and of the right type may be more readily contributable, particularly through a DST bridge. Properties the owner wants for income, control, sentimental reasons, or strategic value may be stronger candidates to retain. Different debt and built-in-gain profiles may also lead to different contribution treatment.

Partial interests in one property require more work. The REIT must accept the arrangement and accommodate co-ownership. That is less common and more complex than contributing selected whole properties. An owner with only one property who wants a partial exchange should have advisors explore whether that structure is available and suitable rather than assume it will be accepted.

## Partial versus full 721 exchange

A [full 721 exchange](721-exchange-explained-convert-property-into-reit-units.html) contributes all subject real estate. It maximizes the 721-related benefits: the greatest transition to passive ownership, the greatest exposure to the REIT portfolio, and deferral on all contributed gains. It also creates the largest commitment to REIT ownership by giving up direct control and 1031 flexibility for the entire contributed set of assets.

A partial exchange produces partial benefits and retains partial flexibility. The owner has passivity, diversification, deferral, and the estate features of OP units for the contributed portion, while retaining direct control and future options for the remainder. It gives the owner less total REIT exposure and less total direct-property exposure.

Neither is inherently better. A full exchange can suit an owner ready for a full transition. A partial exchange can suit an owner who values a balance between the REIT’s benefits and continued direct ownership. The question is how much of the portfolio the owner wants to make subject to the 721 structure.

## Risks that remain in a partial exchange

A partial exchange reduces single-REIT concentration compared with moving all property into one REIT. It does not eliminate the risks of the contributed position.

OP units are illiquid. Unlike direct real estate, they cannot themselves be 1031-exchanged later. Once property has been contributed, that portion loses future 1031 eligibility. Unit value is tied to the performance of the REIT and its operating partnership, including REIT fees and property-level results. A cash redemption is typically taxable and can trigger the deferred gain. As with other real-estate securities, the investor can lose principal.

The retained properties have their own direct ownership risks and requirements. The owner still manages or oversees those assets, retains their concentration and operating exposure, and remains responsible for any decisions associated with them. A mixed allocation is not a risk-free allocation.

## Key Takeaways

- A partial 721 exchange contributes some real estate to a REIT and keeps the rest in direct ownership.
- The contributed portion receives the potential benefits of Section 721 deferral, REIT diversification, passive ownership, and estate planning; the retained portion preserves direct control and 1031 flexibility.
- Section 721 applies to the contributed assets, while the retained assets retain their existing basis and tax position. Debt analysis applies to the contributed assets.
- Owners may retain control and options, keep a valued property, stage the transition, or reduce single-REIT concentration. Contributing selected whole properties is usually cleaner than transferring a partial interest in one property.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you partially 721 exchange a property?

Yes. A partial 721 exchange contributes some real estate to a REIT and keeps the rest in direct ownership. It is most cleanly accomplished by contributing selected whole properties from a portfolio while retaining other properties. A partial interest in one property can also be contributed in principle, but it requires the REIT to accept and structure a co-ownership arrangement. The approach balances the benefits for contributed assets with retained ownership for the rest.

### Why would I do a partial 721 exchange?

It can balance passivity, diversification, and deferral on contributed assets with retained control and 1031 flexibility on kept assets. An owner might keep a valued property, transition more gradually over time, or limit exposure to a single REIT by retaining some direct property. It is a middle path between contributing all real estate and retaining all real estate.

### How is a partial 721 exchange taxed?

The Section 721 deferral generally applies to the contributed property: gain is not generally recognized and carries into OP units. The retained property is unchanged because it was not disposed of, so it retains its basis and tax situation. If the contributed asset has debt, the deemed-distribution and other debt considerations apply to that portion. A CPA handles the transaction-specific treatment.

### What do I get from a partial 721 exchange?

For contributed assets, the investor may receive deferred gain, REIT diversification, passive ownership, and estate features such as potential step-up and unit divisibility. For retained assets, the investor keeps direct control, direct income and appreciation, future 1031 flexibility, and any particular property they value. The result is a mix of 721 features and direct-ownership advantages.

### What is the cleanest way to structure a partial exchange?

Generally, contribute selected whole properties from a portfolio and retain the others. The assets can be treated separately, avoiding the co-ownership complications of dividing a single property. Advisors can assess which properties fit the REIT and which the owner wants to keep, as well as their taxes and debt.

### Can I contribute a partial interest in one property?

Possibly, but it is less common and more complex. The REIT must accept and accommodate a partial interest, including co-ownership arrangements. An owner with one property should not assume that a partial-interest contribution will be available; the structure needs careful work with advisors and the REIT. Contributing whole properties is generally the more practical partial approach for a portfolio owner.

### Does a partial 721 reduce single-REIT risk?

Yes, relative to a full transition to one REIT. Retaining direct property spreads exposure between the REIT and the remaining direct assets. It is a way to avoid placing all transitioned real estate in one REIT. It does not eliminate REIT risk, the risks of direct property, or the possibility of loss of principal.

### Should I do a partial or full 721 exchange?

It depends on the desired balance. A full exchange maximizes REIT diversification, passivity, and deferral but gives up direct control and future 1031 flexibility across all subject assets. A partial exchange retains some control and optionality while providing the 721 features only for the contributed portion. The decision turns on readiness for a complete REIT transition versus a desire to retain some direct real estate.

### Can I do more 721 exchanges later after a partial one?

Potentially. The owner may later contribute more of the retained properties in additional 721 exchanges, creating a staged transition into REIT ownership. The retained assets remain available for future 1031 or 721 exchanges, subject to the applicable rules and transaction structure. This incremental flexibility is one reason an owner may start with a partial exchange.

### Does a partial 721 exchange work with the DST bridge?

Potentially. An owner can 1031 selected properties into a DST structured for a 721 exit, with that portion later moving toward REIT ownership, while other property remains in direct ownership. The partial approach can therefore be combined with a DST bridge. The mechanics depend on the assets and structure, and advisors should design the partial-plus-bridge approach around the owner’s goals.

### How do I decide which properties to contribute?

Consider which assets fit a REIT’s criteria—such as institutional quality, location, and property type—especially where a DST bridge is contemplated. Also consider which properties the owner values for income, control, sentimental, or strategic reasons, and the tax and debt profiles of each. Properties the owner is ready to place in passive ownership and that fit the REIT can be candidates to contribute; properties the owner wants to control or that do not fit can be retained.

### Is a partial 721 exchange more complex than a full one?

Somewhat. The owner must decide what to contribute and keep and manage both contributed and retained portions. But contributing selected whole properties is not dramatically more complicated than applying a 721 exchange to the full portfolio; it applies the 721 mechanics to some assets while others remain held. A partial interest in a single property is the materially more complex case. Advisors can coordinate the two portions.

## Glossary

**Partial 721 Exchange:** Contributing some real estate to a REIT while keeping the rest.

**Contributed Portion:** The real estate moved into the REIT and subject to 721 treatment.

**Retained Portion:** The real estate left in direct ownership.

**Whole Property Contribution:** Contributing selected whole properties while retaining others; generally the cleanest structure.

**Partial Interest:** A contribution of a fraction of one property, which is more complex.

**Section 721 Deferral:** The nonrecognition treatment applying to contributed property.

**Control:** Direct-ownership decision-making preserved for retained assets.

**1031 Flexibility:** The ability to use a future 1031 exchange, retained for direct property.

**Single-REIT Concentration:** Exposure to one REIT that can be reduced by retaining direct property.

**Easing In:** A gradual transition using partial exchanges.

**Valued Property:** A property retained for sentimental, strategic, income, or other reasons.

**Full 721 Exchange:** Contributing all subject real estate to the REIT.

**Balanced Approach:** Combining 721 benefits with retained direct ownership.

**Staged Transition:** Moving more assets over time through successive exchanges.

**Deemed Distribution:** A debt consideration for the contributed portion.

**Co-Ownership:** The arrangement that can be required for a partial-interest contribution.
