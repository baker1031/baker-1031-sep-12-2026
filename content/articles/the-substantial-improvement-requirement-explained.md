# The Substantial Improvement Requirement Explained

**Opportunity Zone Funds · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing the same first question around an Opportunity Zone fund: “What is the property worth today?” It matters, of course. But for an existing building, another question can matter just as much: can the fund actually put enough money into the building, on time, to keep the property qualified?

That is the substantial-improvement requirement. For a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) that buys existing property rather than original-use property, the usual test is to roughly double the basis in the building within 30 months. This is educational information, not tax advice. Opportunity Zone rules are technical, time-sensitive, and evolving, so confirm the current rules with your tax adviser.

The first-order view is that this is a development budget. The second-order view is that it shapes the whole investment: the kind of asset a fund buys, the work it must do, the execution and lease-up risk it takes, and the tax benefits it is trying to preserve.

## What substantial improvement means

Substantial improvement applies to purchased tangible property that is not original-use property: in plain terms, an existing, previously used property the QOF has acquired. To qualify as Opportunity Zone business property, the QOF generally must make significant improvements. Buying an existing stabilized building and simply holding it is not enough. The fund generally needs additions to basis that roughly double the basis in the building.

The policy point is straightforward. The program is intended to support genuine development and economic activity in the zones, rather than a transfer of ownership with no real improvement. The requirement is what pushes many Opportunity Zone funds toward development and redevelopment.

Original-use property works differently. A newly constructed building, or other property first used in the zone by the QOF, can satisfy the standard through that original use. It does not have to go through the separate doubling calculation that applies to purchased existing property.

## The 30-month doubling rule

The operating rule is commonly called the 30-month doubling rule. Within any 30-month period after the QOF acquires the property, the fund must make improvements—additions to basis—at least equal to its basis in the building at the start of that period. Put differently, the improvements must effectively double that building basis.

For example, if the basis in the building, excluding land, is $1,000,000, the fund must put at least $1,000,000 into qualifying improvements within 30 months. Spend a million on the building, then put a million more into improving it. That is why both the amount and the calendar matter. A credible budget without a credible schedule is not a complete compliance plan.

## Land vs. building basis

One distinction does a great deal of work here: the doubling requirement generally applies to building basis, not land basis. When a QOF buys improved real estate, the purchase price must be allocated between the land and the building. The non-land portion is generally the basis the fund needs to double.

That can materially affect the amount of improvement required. Where land makes up a large part of the value, the building basis can be smaller, and so can the required improvement amount. IRS guidance has clarified that land is not subject to the substantial-improvement requirement in the same way; a fund does not have to “improve the land” by the value assigned to it.

The implication is not that the allocation is a casual exercise. An overstated building basis can make the required project spend larger than budgeted. An understated building basis can lead to under-improvement. The allocation has to be supportable before the work plan is treated as dependable.

## How funds satisfy the test

A value-add redevelopment can meet the test through substantial renovation, repositioning, or expansion of the building. A fund may also pursue ground-up development on acquired land with an existing structure, where construction spending can satisfy the relevant requirement. In each case, the practical goal is to reach the required building-basis improvement within the 30-month window.

Funds may use the [working-capital safe harbor](the-opportunity-zone-working-capital-safe-harbor.html) to hold and deploy capital for improvements under a written plan. That written plan, a defined improvement budget, a realistic timeline, and records of improvement spending all matter. They are how a fund connects its intended development program to the timing rule.

For new construction that is original use, the construction itself meets the original-use standard. For an acquired existing building, the fund needs planned and documented substantial improvement. These are related paths, but they are not the same test.

### Key takeaways

- A QOF generally must roughly double building basis in existing purchased property to make it qualify.
- The concrete test is improvements at least equal to the building basis within 30 months.
- Land basis is generally excluded. High land value can therefore reduce the improvement amount, but the land/building allocation must be sound.
- Major renovation or construction, supported by the working-capital safe harbor and documentation, can satisfy the rule. Missing the amount, the time limit, or the records can put the result at risk.

## Common compliance failures

The most obvious failure is insufficient improvement: the fund does not invest enough to reach the building-basis doubling requirement. The next is timing. Development delays can leave a project short of the required work when the 30-month window closes.

Allocation and recordkeeping can be just as consequential. A mistaken allocation between land and building can distort the target spend. A missing written working-capital plan or weak improvement records can make it harder to show how the capital was deployed. These failures can jeopardize the property’s status, the Opportunity Zone benefits, and the [90% asset test](the-opportunity-zone-90-percent-asset-test-deep-dive.html), potentially triggering penalties.

This is where first-order and second-order thinking separate. First-order thinking sees a construction draw schedule. Second-order thinking asks whether the sponsor has enough time, budget discipline, basis support, and documentation to withstand a rule that is central to the investment’s tax treatment.

## What investors should look for

Start with a defined improvement budget and timeline. The plan should show how the fund intends to put at least the building basis into improvements within 30 months. It should not rely on an optimistic completion date with no room for ordinary development delays.

Then assess sponsor execution. A capable sponsor has a record of completing development on schedule. Delays and cost overruns are not merely economic disappointments in this setting; they can threaten compliance. Finally, examine the structure: the land/building basis allocation, the working-capital plan, and the documentation process.

Substantial-improvement compliance is technical and central to the benefits an investor may be seeking. That makes professional help appropriate. The relevant diligence is the plan, the sponsor, and the compliance structure together—not just a projected return.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the substantial improvement requirement?

It requires a QOF to significantly improve purchased existing property so it can qualify as Opportunity Zone business property. For tangible property that is not original use, the fund generally invests in improvements that roughly double the building basis within 30 months. It cannot simply buy and hold an existing building. The rule supports genuine development rather than a transfer of ownership. Original-use property, such as new construction, qualifies differently through its original use.

### What is the 30-month doubling rule?

Within any 30-month period after acquisition, the QOF generally must make additions to basis at least equal to the building basis at the start of the period. That effectively doubles the building basis. If a building basis excluding land is $1,000,000, at least $1,000,000 of improvements are generally required within 30 months. The rule has both a spending target and a time limit, so the work must be planned and completed on schedule.

### Does the doubling apply to land too?

Generally, no. The doubling typically applies to the non-land, building portion of basis. A purchase price for improved real estate is allocated between land and building, and IRS guidance treats land differently for this requirement. A high land value can reduce the building basis and the required improvement amount. The allocation must still be calculated correctly because it drives the compliance target.

### How do funds satisfy the substantial improvement test?

They may make major improvements to an existing building—renovating, repositioning, or expanding it—within the 30-month window. Ground-up construction can also satisfy the relevant development requirement. Funds can use the working-capital safe harbor to hold and deploy capital under a written plan. The budget, plan, timeline, and improvement records all help demonstrate compliance. New construction that is original use satisfies the standard through the original-use path.

### What are common substantial-improvement compliance failures?

Common failures are not spending enough to reach the building-basis target, missing the 30-month period, incorrectly allocating or calculating land and building basis, and lacking working-capital plans or records. A failure can endanger property qualification, Opportunity Zone benefits, and the 90% asset test, and can trigger penalties. A credible plan, capable sponsor, and proper structure are important safeguards.

### Why does the substantial improvement requirement make Opportunity Zone funds development-focused?

An existing stabilized building cannot simply be purchased and held. It generally must be substantially improved, or the fund must pursue original-use construction. That pushes Opportunity Zone funds toward development and redevelopment rather than passive ownership. It also produces a different risk and return profile: construction and lease-up risk on one side, and appreciation from real improvement on the other, including the program’s 10-year benefit.

### What is original-use property?

Original-use property is property whose first use in the Opportunity Zone is by the QOF. Newly constructed buildings are the familiar example, and other property first placed in service in the zone by the fund can also qualify. It meets the standard through original use rather than a separate doubling of an existing building’s basis. That is why ground-up development and an acquisition of an existing building follow different paths.

### What should I look for in a development fund’s improvement plan?

Look for a realistic improvement budget at least equal to the building basis and a timeline that can meet the 30-month requirement. Review the sponsor’s ability to complete development on schedule, because delays and overruns can threaten the rule. Also examine the land/building allocation, working-capital plan, and recordkeeping. Professional diligence can help test whether the plan, sponsor, and structure are credible.

### What happens if a fund fails the substantial improvement requirement?

If existing property is not improved enough within the required period, it may not qualify as Opportunity Zone business property. That can create a 90% asset-test problem, jeopardize Opportunity Zone benefits, potentially trigger penalties, and undermine the deferral and 10-year exclusion investors are seeking. It is a serious compliance issue, which is why the fund’s ability to meet the rule deserves careful attention.

### Does the substantial improvement requirement apply to equipment or just buildings?

The requirement applies to acquired tangible property that is not original use. In most Opportunity Zone real-estate funds, the practical focus is an acquired building and its building basis. Equipment and other tangible property used in an Opportunity Zone business can have their own original-use or substantial-improvement and in-zone-use considerations. The fund and the investor’s CPA should address the particular asset type.

### Can a fund get more than 30 months to substantially improve property?

The basic substantial-improvement period is 30 months. The working-capital safe harbor generally provides up to 31 months, with possible extensions, to coordinate capital deployment under a plan. In limited circumstances, such as federally declared disaster relief, the IRS has extended certain Opportunity Zone deadlines. Funds should not assume a routine extension of the 30-month requirement; they should plan to meet it and confirm current rules and any applicable relief with a CPA.

## Glossary

- **Substantial Improvement:** Significant improvement of existing property so it qualifies.
- **30-Month Doubling Rule:** Improvements equal to the building basis within 30 months.
- **Building Basis:** The non-land basis to which the doubling generally applies.
- **Land Basis:** Basis generally excluded from the doubling requirement.
- **Basis Allocation:** Splitting a purchase price between land and building.
- **Original Use:** Property first used in the zone by the QOF, such as new construction.
- **OZ Business Property:** Qualifying tangible property in an Opportunity Zone.
- **Working-Capital Safe Harbor:** Holding cash to deploy into improvements under a written plan.
- **Value-Add Redevelopment:** Improvement of existing property to meet the rule.
- **Ground-Up Development:** New construction that qualifies as original use.
- **30-Month Window:** The period in which the doubling must be completed.
- **Insufficient Improvement:** Failure to reach the doubling target.
- **Timing Failure:** Failure to meet the 30-month window.
- **Documentation:** Records and plans that support compliance.
- **90% Asset Test:** A test substantial-improvement failures can jeopardize.
- **Sponsor Execution:** A developer’s ability to complete the work on schedule.
