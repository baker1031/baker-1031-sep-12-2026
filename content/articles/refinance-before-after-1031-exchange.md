# Can You Refinance Before or After a 1031 Exchange?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep coming back to the same question when an investor wants both liquidity and deferral: is this loan a real financing decision, or does it merely dress up cash pulled out of an exchange? That difference is where the risk sits.

A loan is not taxable income because it must be repaid. That is why [cash-out refinancing](1031-exchange-vs-cash-out-refinance.html) is attractive. Around a 1031 exchange, though, timing can cause the IRS to view the cash as disguised exchange proceeds—taxable boot—rather than a separate loan. This guide compares refinancing before the sale and after the replacement purchase, the boot risk, step-transaction concerns, and safer paths for accessing cash.

First-order thinking says that a larger loan delivers tax-free cash. Second-order thinking asks whether the timing, purpose, and connection to the exchange make the steps look like one integrated plan. That question is fact-specific. Tax counsel and a CPA should assess the actual facts before an investor acts.

## Refinancing before the sale

Refinancing the relinquished property shortly before selling it is the riskier scenario. The apparent appeal is clear: borrow against the property, take cash that is normally tax-free, then complete the exchange and defer the gain. The risk is that the IRS views the loan as a way of pulling exchange proceeds out tax-free and recasts the cash as taxable [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html).

The issue is substance over form. If cash is extracted immediately before the exchange, the economic result can resemble taking cash from the exchange, only routed through a loan. The IRS and courts can collapse separate steps if they appear to be one plan. The closer the refinance is to the sale, and the more clearly it was planned with the exchange, the greater the risk.

That does not mean every pre-sale refinance fails. A loan obtained well before the exchange was contemplated for a genuine, independent business reason is on firmer ground. But a close-in-time loan whose main purpose is taking cash out before the sale can result in the cash being taxed as boot. The safer general approach is to avoid refinancing the relinquished property near the exchange and consider a later replacement-property refinance instead.

## Refinancing after the purchase

Refinancing the replacement property after the exchange is complete is generally the safer route. Once the investor has acquired the replacement, it is owned like any other property, and a later loan can provide tax-free access to equity. The completed exchange is less likely to be treated as part of that subsequent borrowing.

Separation matters in time and substance. A refinance completed after some time for its own purpose has a better footing than one agreed before the exchange and executed immediately afterward. The more the loan can stand alone as an independent decision, the lower the risk of boot recharacterization.

Even then, there is nuance. A replacement refinance that was plainly pre-arranged as part of the exchange plan can still draw step-transaction scrutiny, even if it is usually lower risk than the pre-sale case. A genuine, independent post-purchase refinance, undertaken with reasonable separation, is the standard lower-risk method for accessing cash from exchanged property.

> A separate refinancing of the replacement after the exchange is generally the safer path: loan proceeds are not taxable income, provided the transaction is genuinely separate rather than an integrated cash-out plan.

## The cash-out boot risk

**Boot** is cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange, taxable up to the gain. A normal refinance is debt, not value received from an exchange, so it is not automatically boot. The concern arises when the loan is structured as a device to extract exchange value tax-free, especially before sale and in close connection with the exchange.

The dividing line is genuine financing versus disguised receipt. An independently motivated, property-secured loan can be normal financing. A loan arranged to obtain both cash and the exchange deferral can be treated differently. Timing, purpose, and connection to the exchange determine the analysis.

Getting the structure wrong can turn a cash-access plan into an unexpected tax bill. The conservative route is a replacement-property loan after the exchange, with reasonable separation and an independent rationale; that better supports treatment as tax-free loan proceeds rather than taxable boot.

## Step-transaction concerns

The step-transaction doctrine allows the IRS to treat formally separate steps as one transaction when they are really parts of a single plan. Around refinancing and a 1031, the question is whether the loan and exchange would each have happened on their own or whether they were steps in a unified effort to obtain tax-free cash while deferring gain. If integrated, the cash can be treated as boot.

The facts include timing, intent, and interdependence. A pre-sale refinance executed immediately before the exchange and planned with it has the hallmarks of an integrated transaction. A later refinance undertaken for a separate reason has fewer. The practical response is not a formula: create real separation, document the independent purpose, and do not pre-arrange the borrowing as a component of the exchange.

## Safer timing strategies

For most investors seeking both deferral and later access to equity, the cleaner approach is to complete the exchange, then refinance the replacement property after reasonable separation as a genuine independent transaction. That avoids the heightened pre-sale boot and step-transaction exposure.

A deliberate **partial exchange** is another option. The investor intentionally takes some cash as boot, pays tax on that portion, and defers the remaining gain. It is transparent: there is no attempt to characterize exchange cash as loan proceeds. The trade-off is obvious—the cash is taxable—but it avoids recharacterization risk.

If money must be accessed before or around an exchange, separation and purpose are central. A refinance well before the exchange was even contemplated, backed by a genuine business reason, is materially different from a last-minute cash extraction. Get a written view from tax counsel on the particular facts.

- A refinance of the relinquished property shortly before sale can be recharacterized as taxable boot.
- A well-separated replacement-property refinance after the exchange is generally safer for tax-free cash access.
- The step-transaction doctrine can collapse a refinance and exchange that are one integrated plan.
- The more conservative options are a later replacement refinance, a transparent partial exchange, or a truly independent, well-separated refinance supported by tax counsel.

## Common refinance scenarios

Suppose an investor needs **$100,000**. The risky version is a new pre-sale loan on the relinquished property that produces an extra **$100,000**, followed immediately by an exchange. The IRS could treat that cash as boot. A safer alternative is to complete the exchange, then refinance the replacement later for **$100,000** in a separate transaction. Another is a partial exchange: take **$100,000** as known taxable boot and defer the balance.

Consider a different fact pattern: a property was refinanced a year ago for ordinary reasons and only later did the owner decide to exchange. That earlier loan, made before the exchange was contemplated and for an independent purpose, is generally not connected to the exchange and does not create the same step-transaction issue.

Debt replacement adds another layer. Increasing debt on the relinquished property before sale can increase the debt that must be replaced on the replacement property to avoid mortgage boot. Debt on the replacement, including a later refinance, can also affect the deferral math. A CPA should model both the boot risk and the debt-replacement requirement.

## What the case law suggests

The refinance question has been litigated, and outcomes depend on facts. Courts have sometimes declined to classify borrowing as boot where the taxpayer had a genuine independent purpose and meaningful separation from the exchange. Cases that go the other way tend to involve borrowing tightly linked to the exchange with little independent reason beyond extracting cash.

The pattern does not produce a bright-line waiting period. It does show why timing, purpose, and interdependence matter. A pre-sale refinance is not categorically forbidden, but it carries real risk. A later replacement refinance or transparent partial exchange is normally the more conservative path, and a tax adviser's view is especially useful when facts sit near the line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I refinance before a 1031 exchange to pull out cash?

You can, but it is risky. A close-in-time refinance of the relinquished property can be recharacterized as a disguised exchange cash-out and taxable boot under the step-transaction doctrine. An independent refinance well before the exchange is safer; obtain tax counsel's view.

### Is refinancing after the exchange safer?

Generally, yes. Refinancing the replacement after the exchange is complete, with reasonable separation and an independent purpose, is less likely to be viewed as part of the exchange. It is the standard lower-risk approach to accessing cash.

### Why is a pre-sale refinance risky?

The IRS may see it as a device to extract exchange proceeds tax-free. If the refinance and exchange appear to be one plan, they can be collapsed and the cash taxed as boot. Closer and more connected steps carry more risk.

### What is the step-transaction doctrine?

It is a doctrine that permits the IRS to collapse formally separate steps into one transaction when they are components of an integrated plan. Here, it asks whether the loan and exchange are truly separate; if not, loan proceeds can be treated as boot.

### Will a refinance always create boot?

No. A genuine, independent refinance secured by property is normal financing, not boot. The risk is a loan that serves as a device to extract exchange value, especially a close-in-time pre-sale loan. Timing, purpose, and connection matter.

### How long should I wait to refinance after an exchange?

There is no bright-line period. Reasonable separation supports the view that the loan is independent. The cleaner the purpose and separation, the lower the recharacterization risk; tax counsel should advise on the particular circumstances.

### What's a safer alternative to a risky refinance?

A deliberate partial exchange. Take some cash as taxable boot and defer the rest. It is transparent and has no recharacterization risk, although the cash is taxed.

### Does a refinance affect the debt I must replace?

Yes. A pre-sale debt increase may need to be replaced on the replacement property to avoid mortgage boot. Replacement debt, including a later loan, also interacts with the deferral calculation. Coordinate with a CPA.

### Is a refinance done a year before the exchange a problem?

Generally not when it was an ordinary, independent loan made before the exchange was contemplated. The risk is the close-in-time, exchange-motivated loan; a prior independent refinance does not ordinarily taint a later exchange.

### Can I get both the deferral and cash from my property?

Yes, with a sound structure. Complete the exchange and later refinance the replacement for tax-free loan proceeds, or use a partial exchange and take taxable boot while deferring the rest. Both are safer than a risky pre-sale refinance.

### Should I get tax advice before refinancing around an exchange?

Yes. A CPA and tax counsel can assess timing, purpose, and connection; model debt replacement; and document the loan's independent rationale. The tax cost of an adverse recharacterization can be significant.

### Why is refinancing real estate tax-free at all?

Loan proceeds are not taxable income because the borrower must repay them. The 1031 issue is whether that normal borrowing is so connected to the exchange that the IRS treats it as taxable cash-out boot instead.

### Has the refinance question been litigated?

Yes. The outcomes are fact-specific. Cases reflect a difference between genuinely independent, well-separated financing and a loan closely connected to extracting exchange cash. The pattern supports careful fact review rather than a mechanical rule.

### Is there a bright-line rule for refinancing around an exchange?

No. Case law turns on timing, purpose, and interdependence. For borderline circumstances, a post-exchange replacement refinance or transparent partial exchange is generally more conservative, and a tax adviser's opinion is valuable.

## Glossary

**Refinance:** Replacing a property's existing loan with a new one, often to pull out cash tax-free.

**Cash-Out Refinance:** A new loan larger than the existing loan that puts cash in the owner's pocket.

**Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange; taxable up to the gain.

**Step-Transaction Doctrine:** A doctrine collapsing separate steps into one transaction if they form an integrated plan.

**Pre-Sale Refinance:** Refinancing the relinquished property before sale; the higher-risk boot scenario.

**Post-Exchange Refinance:** Refinancing replacement property after the exchange; the safer cash-access approach.

**Substance Over Form:** The principle that tax treatment follows economic reality rather than transaction form.

**Disguised Cash-Out:** A refinance structured to extract exchange value tax-free and risk boot recharacterization.

**Partial Exchange:** An exchange that intentionally takes some cash as taxable boot while deferring the rest.

**Mortgage Boot:** Taxable gain from not replacing debt paid off in an exchange.

**Debt Replacement:** Matching paid-off debt with new debt or cash; a pre-sale refinance can affect it.

**Independent Purpose:** A genuine separate reason for a refinance that supports it not being part of the exchange.

**Interdependence:** Whether the refinance and exchange would each happen on their own.

**Relinquished Property:** The property sold in the exchange; its pre-sale refinance carries greater risk.

**Replacement Property:** The property acquired in the exchange; later refinancing is generally safer.

**Tax-Free Loan Proceeds:** Borrowed money that is not income because it must be repaid.
