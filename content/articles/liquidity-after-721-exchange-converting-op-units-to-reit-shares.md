# Liquidity After a 721 Exchange: Converting OP Units to REIT Shares

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › Liquidity After a 721 Exchange: Converting OP Units to REIT Shares

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I start with the distinction that the appeal of a 721 is partly the ability to access wealth without selling an entire illiquid property. But the path is not a button marked “cash.” It runs from [OP units](op-units-explained.html), to REIT shares, to cash—and the conversion step recognizes deferred gain.

First-order thinking sees the liquidity. Second-order thinking asks when conversion is allowed, what kind of REIT shares will result, and how much of the gross value will remain after the tax event. Those questions determine the liquidity an investor actually has.

## The liquidity path

OP units are a partnership interest; they are not directly traded on a market. The usual route to cash is conversion to REIT shares, typically one-for-one after a holding period. For a public REIT, converted shares are market-tradable and can be sold for cash:

**OP units → REIT shares → cash**

That is a real advantage over direct real estate. A building usually takes time to sell and is largely an all-or-nothing position. Units can be converted and shares sold in portions. With a public REIT, the resulting shares can be sold relatively quickly.

The crucial qualification is tax. [The tax consequences of converting OP units to REIT shares](tax-consequences-of-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html) include recognition of the deferred gain on the converted units. Conversion is the taxable gateway to liquidity. The route provides access to wealth, but it is not tax-free.

## The holding period before conversion

OP units usually have a minimum holding period before conversion is available. The period is often around one year, though it varies by the operating partnership agreement and REIT terms. During that time, the investor holds units, receives distributions, and continues deferral, but cannot yet convert to shares.

Liquidity is therefore not immediate after a 721. An investor expecting near-term cash needs to understand that the holding period delays the conversion option. When the period ends, the conversion route opens, but each conversion still triggers tax.

Many investors hold far beyond the minimum to receive income and keep deferred gain in the units. The holding period is the initial constraint; it does not require conversion when it expires.

## Converting gradually for liquidity

A common approach is gradual conversion: convert and sell only portions over time rather than all units at once. A full conversion in one year recognizes all deferred gain in that year and can produce a large tax bill. Converting a portion each year spreads recognition and the tax over multiple years.

Gradual conversion also ties liquidity to actual needs. Convert only what is needed for retirement income, a purchase, or another specific purpose; leave the remainder in OP units, where it remains deferred and income-producing. That avoids converting excess units simply to hold cash or shares.

For many investors, this is the practical liquidity strategy: access wealth over time, spread tax, and keep unconverted units deferred. Units never converted may also remain eligible for a possible basis step-up at death, subject to current law.

## Traded vs. non-traded REIT liquidity

The degree of liquidity depends strongly on whether the REIT is publicly traded or non-traded.

| REIT type | After OP-unit conversion | Practical liquidity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Publicly traded | Shares are listed on a stock exchange | Shares can generally be sold quickly at market prices |
| Non-traded | Shares are not exchange listed | Liquidity usually depends on periodic share-repurchase programs, subject to limits, discretion, and conditions |

A public REIT thus offers stronger market-based liquidity after conversion. A non-traded REIT provides a more limited, program-based route. The REIT type determines how liquid converted shares will actually be, so it should be understood before completing a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html).

## Managing liquidity and taxes together

Post-721 liquidity is also tax planning. The aim is to access what is needed while limiting and spreading tax. That can mean converting only the necessary amount, converting gradually across years, and [timing conversions to cash in a tax-smart way](converting-op-units-to-cash-tax-smart-timing.html), such as in lower-income years or when deductions are available, with CPA guidance.

Every conversion recognizes gain. How much to convert and when to convert are therefore tax decisions as much as liquidity decisions. The CPA can model the taxable result so the investor understands the after-tax proceeds rather than only the gross share value.

The longest-horizon approach is to hold units not needed for liquidity. They continue to defer gain and may generate income. If held until death, a basis step-up may erase deferred gain on unconverted units, subject to the law then in effect. The investor can use liquidity as needed while holding the rest toward that potential estate outcome.

## Key Takeaways

- The liquidity path is OP units to REIT shares to cash. Unit conversion is taxable because it triggers deferred gain.
- There is commonly a holding period, often around a year, so post-721 liquidity is not immediate.
- Convert gradually to spread recognition and tax over years while leaving the balance deferred.
- A public REIT offers market liquidity after conversion; a non-traded REIT offers more limited repurchase-program liquidity.

## Liquidity planning strategies

**Hold and convert as needed.** This is the common middle path. Hold OP units for distributions and deferral, converting only portions when liquidity is required. It keeps most units working while spreading tax.

**Hold for the step-up.** Investors with limited liquidity needs may hold indefinitely, convert little or nothing, receive distributions, and seek the potential basis step-up at death. This emphasizes deferral and estate planning over liquidity.

**Planned gradual conversion.** Investors seeking systematic liquidity can convert a planned amount annually—for example, for retirement income. It supplies predictable access while spreading tax among years.

The right approach depends on the amount and timing of liquidity needed and on tax and estate objectives. An advisor and CPA can help implement the strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get liquidity after a 721 exchange?

Convert OP units to REIT shares, typically one-for-one after the holding period, then sell the shares for cash. Public REIT shares are market-tradable. Conversion recognizes deferred gain, so liquidity is real but not tax-free.

### Are OP units themselves liquid?

Not directly. They are partnership interests rather than market-traded securities. Liquidity comes indirectly from conversion to REIT shares and, for public REITs, selling the shares. Investors needing direct immediate liquidity should not treat OP units as a cash substitute.

### Is there a waiting period before I can convert?

Usually. The operating partnership agreement and REIT terms commonly require a holding period, often around one year. During that time units can earn distributions and defer gain but cannot be converted. Plan near-term liquidity needs around the waiting period.

### Does converting OP units to shares trigger taxes?

Yes, generally. Conversion or cash redemption recognizes deferred gain on the converted units. Investors may convert gradually to spread tax over years, or hold unconverted units toward a possible step-up at death.

### Why should I convert OP units gradually?

Converting all units at once recognizes all deferred gain in one year. Gradual conversion spreads recognition and tax while matching liquidity to need. The unconverted units remain deferred and can continue to generate distributions.

### What's the difference between traded and non-traded REIT liquidity?

Public REIT shares trade on an exchange and can generally be sold quickly at market prices after conversion. Non-traded REIT shares rely on periodic repurchase programs with limits, discretion, and conditions. The latter is more constrained and program based.

### Can I access liquidity without triggering tax?

Generally not for principal. Conversion to shares, the usual route to cash, triggers deferred gain. Unit distributions are income taxed under applicable rules but do not trigger that deferred gain. Holding units avoids conversion tax but does not make principal liquid.

### How much liquidity will I really have?

It depends on REIT type and conversion tax. Public-REIT shares can provide significant market liquidity after conversion, while non-traded REIT liquidity is limited by its program. In either case, after-tax liquidity is less than gross share value because conversion recognizes gain.

### What if I don't need liquidity?

The investor can hold units indefinitely, receive distributions, and continue deferral. Holding until death may provide a step-up that erases deferred gain for heirs, subject to current law. Many investors use a long-term income-and-deferral strategy and convert rarely or not at all.

### What's the best liquidity strategy?

It depends on needs. Convert-as-needed suits occasional liquidity; hold-for-step-up suits estate-focused investors with little liquidity need; planned gradual conversion suits systematic needs such as retirement income. Choose based on liquidity requirements and tax and estate priorities, with advisor and CPA input.

### Should I choose a traded or non-traded REIT for liquidity?

If liquidity is a priority, publicly traded shares generally provide more robust market liquidity after conversion than a non-traded REIT's repurchase program. But portfolio quality, REIT strength, and the investor's goals also matter. Discuss tradability with an advisor as part of the liquidity plan.

### How do I manage liquidity and taxes together?

Convert only the needed amount, convert gradually, time conversions thoughtfully with CPA guidance, and hold the rest toward a potential basis step-up. This coordinates access to cash with tax planning and focuses on after-tax liquidity.

## Glossary

**Liquidity Path**  
OP units → REIT shares → cash, the route to accessing wealth.

**Conversion**  
Exchanging OP units for REIT shares, the taxable liquidity gateway.

**Holding Period**  
The minimum time (often ~1 year) before units can convert to shares.

**Gradual Conversion**  
Converting portions over time to spread the gain and tax.

**Publicly-Traded REIT**  
A REIT whose shares trade on an exchange, offering robust liquidity.

**Non-Traded REIT**  
A REIT without exchange-listed shares, with limited liquidity.

**Share-Repurchase Program**  
A non-traded REIT's limited liquidity mechanism.

**Deferred Gain**  
The gain triggered when you convert units to shares.

**Distributions**  
Income from units, not triggering the deferred gain.

**Hold for the Step-Up**  
Holding units toward the death-time step-up, minimal conversion.

**Planned Gradual Conversion**  
Systematically converting a portion yearly for liquidity.

**Convert as Needed**  
Converting portions when liquidity is needed.

**After-Tax Liquidity**  
The net liquidity after the conversion tax.

**Step-Up in Basis**  
The death-time reset erasing gain on unconverted units.

**Market Liquidity**  
The tradability of traded-REIT shares after conversion.

**Tax Timing**  
Choosing when to convert to manage the gain recognition.
