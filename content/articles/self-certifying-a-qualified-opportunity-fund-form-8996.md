# Self-Certifying a Qualified Opportunity Fund (Form 8996)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Opportunity Zone Funds · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how much confidence investors sometimes place in a fund's label. “Qualified Opportunity Fund” sounds like a designation that someone else has already checked. It is not. A QOF certifies itself through IRS Form 8996; there is no IRS application or pre-approval.

The first-order view is that a completed form establishes a fund. The second-order view is that the filing starts an ongoing burden: qualifying investments, the **90%** asset test, records, annual reporting, and exposure if the fund falls short. For sponsors and sophisticated investors, the mechanics of Form 8996 are therefore part of the underwriting—not a clerical footnote.

This guide covers Form 8996, the self-certification process, annual asset-test reporting, ongoing obligations, and consequences of non-compliance. Opportunity Zone rules are time-sensitive and evolving. Verify current rules and forms with a tax advisor; this is educational information, not tax advice.

## What is Form 8996?

Form 8996, titled **Qualified Opportunity Fund**, is the IRS form a QOF uses to self-certify and report annual compliance. A QOF—a corporation or partnership—files it with its federal income-tax return. The form does two jobs:

1. It makes the initial self-certification, electing QOF status effective from a chosen month.
2. It reports annually whether the fund meets the **90%** asset test.

There is no separate approval process. Filing the form is the mechanism by which the entity declares its QOF status, and annual filing is how the fund documents continuing compliance. For investors, Form 8996 is the fund's central compliance instrument: a properly filing fund is attending to its certification and reporting obligations, although a filing alone does not prove the underlying investment requirements are met.

## The self-certification process

An eligible entity—a corporation or partnership organized to invest in qualified Opportunity Zone property—self-certifies by filing Form 8996 with its timely filed federal return. The entity elects QOF treatment and states the first month it is a QOF.

Its organizing documents must reflect the purpose of investing in qualified OZ property. The elected first QOF month matters because it begins the clock for the asset tests.

There is no IRS application and no approval. Filing Form 8996 and making the election creates the QOF status. But filing is the simple part. The substance is the ongoing work of investing in qualifying property, observing the **90%** test, maintaining records, and reporting accurately.

## Meeting the 90% asset test

The **90%** asset test is the core annual requirement reported on Form 8996. A QOF must hold at least **90%** of its assets in qualified OZ property, measured as the average of two testing dates each year—generally the six-month point and the end of the fund's tax year.

The fund calculates its qualifying-asset percentage at each date, averages those measurements, and reports the result on Form 8996. Capital must actually move into qualifying OZ property. The working-capital safe harbor can allow cash to be held for deployment into development under a plan, but it is not a blank check for idle capital.

Falling below **90%** on average triggers a penalty calculation. That is why the test is more than a compliance checkbox: it is evidence of whether the fund is making the investments its structure promises.

## Ongoing reporting obligations

Self-certification is not a one-time event. Every year, the QOF files Form 8996 with its federal return, reports the two testing-date measurements and average, and affirms continuing QOF status. See our guide to [QOF reporting and compliance requirements](qof-reporting-and-compliance-requirements.html) for related obligations.

The fund must keep records supporting the result, including asset valuations, qualified-property support, and working-capital plans when those plans are used. It also needs to supply investors with the information they require for their own investment and deferral reporting. IRS information reporting around QOFs and OZ investments has increased over time, so the fund must track current requirements.

## Key Takeaways

- Form 8996 is the IRS form a QOF uses to self-certify and report annual **90%** asset-test compliance.
- A corporation or partnership self-certifies by filing the form, electing QOF status and its first QOF month. No IRS approval is required.
- The fund must hold **90%** or more of its assets in qualified OZ property, averaged across two annual testing dates and reported on the form.
- Annual Form 8996 filings, supporting records, and investor information remain necessary after certification. Failure of the test can produce penalties.

## Penalties for non-compliance

If a QOF holds less than **90%** of its assets in qualified OZ property on average across the testing dates, it may owe a monthly penalty based on the shortfall—the amount by which it missed the **90%** threshold—at a specified rate for every month of non-compliance. See [Opportunity Zone penalties for non-compliance](opportunity-zone-penalties-for-non-compliance.html).

There is a reasonable-cause exception, so a justified failure can avoid a penalty. Without that exception, the financial cost rises with both the shortfall and duration. Persistent or serious failure can also jeopardize QOF status and, with it, the Opportunity Zone benefits investors expect. That makes compliance an investment consideration as well as a fund-administration responsibility.

## Why this matters to investors

The fund, not the investor, files Form 8996. Still, the fund's proper self-certification and continued **90%**-test compliance support the Opportunity Zone tax benefits on which investors rely, including the deferral and the **10**-year exclusion. See the [Opportunity Zones guide](opportunity-zones-guide.html).

Due diligence should ask whether the fund properly self-certified, meets and reports the **90%** test, files Form 8996 each year, and maintains meaningful records. A fund with reliable compliance practices is better positioned to preserve the intended benefits. Sponsors and sophisticated investors creating their own QOFs need the same understanding to execute the structure correctly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Form 8996?

Form 8996, Qualified Opportunity Fund, is the IRS form a QOF corporation or partnership files with its federal return to self-certify status and report annual compliance. It elects a chosen first QOF month and reports the year's **90%** asset test. There is no separate IRS approval, which makes the form the central fund compliance instrument.

### How does a fund self-certify as a QOF?

An eligible corporation or partnership organized to invest in qualified OZ property files Form 8996 with its timely federal return, elects QOF status, and specifies its first QOF month. Organizing documents should reflect the OZ investment purpose. There is no application or pre-approval, but the entity must genuinely meet the investment and compliance requirements.

### What is the 90% asset test on Form 8996?

The QOF must hold at least **90%** of its assets in qualified OZ property, measured as the average of two annual testing dates, generally the six-month point and the end of the tax year. The calculation is reported on Form 8996. Working-capital safe-harbor treatment can apply to development cash under a plan, and failure of the test triggers penalties.

### What are a QOF's ongoing reporting obligations?

Each year the QOF files Form 8996, reports the two testing-date percentages and their average, and affirms continued QOF status. It maintains records for asset valuations, qualifying property, and any working-capital plan, gives investors needed information, and keeps up with evolving reporting rules. Compliance is annual, not one-time.

### What are the penalties for non-compliance?

A QOF below **90%** on average may owe a monthly penalty based on its shortfall at a specified rate for each non-compliant month. Reasonable cause can be an exception. Persistent or serious non-compliance can jeopardize QOF status and investor benefits.

### Does the IRS approve a QOF?

No. The fund self-certifies by filing Form 8996. The IRS enforces substantive requirements through reporting, including Form 8996 and Form 8997, penalties for failed tests, and examination. Investors should not mistake self-certification for an IRS stamp of approval; they should evaluate the fund's actual structure and compliance.

### Can I form my own QOF?

Yes. A sophisticated investor or sponsor can organize an eligible corporation or partnership for qualified OZ property and self-certify using Form 8996. Running a compliant QOF involves the **90%** test, substantial improvement, reporting, and penalty risk, so it requires expertise and professional help. Many individual investors instead use sponsor-managed, passive QOFs and avoid operating the fund themselves.

### How does Form 8996 relate to my own taxes as an investor?

Form 8996 is the fund's form, not the investor's. Investors separately report QOF investments and deferral elections; historically, that has involved Form **8949** and Form **8997**, which tracks QOF investments and deferred gains. The fund provides investment information, while the investor's CPA handles investor-level reporting.

### Why should I care about a fund's Form 8996 compliance?

Proper fund self-certification and ongoing **90%**-test compliance support the investor's deferral and **10**-year exclusion. Review the fund's certification, test results, filings, and records as part of due diligence because those practices protect the benefits investors seek.

### When must a QOF first file Form 8996?

A QOF files Form 8996 with the federal return for its first QOF tax year, elects its first QOF month, and then files annually. That elected month matters because it starts the **90%**-test clock. Tax professionals should determine the timing and election details, and investors should confirm that the fund follows sound filing practices.

### Does self-certification mean less IRS scrutiny?

No. No upfront approval does not mean less scrutiny. The IRS relies on Form 8996, Form 8997, other reporting, and examination to enforce the rules. A fund that misses the **90%** test or substantial-improvement requirements can face penalties or jeopardize the benefits. Self-certification is a filing mechanism, not a relaxation of substantive requirements.

## Glossary

- **Form 8996:** The IRS form a QOF uses to self-certify and report compliance.
- **Self-Certification:** Declaring QOF status by filing Form 8996; no IRS approval is required.
- **Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF):** The self-certifying Opportunity Zone investment vehicle.
- **First QOF Month:** The elected month in which QOF status begins.
- **90% Asset Test:** The requirement to hold **90%** or more of assets in OZ property and report the result on Form 8996.
- **Testing Dates:** The two annual dates at which the asset test is measured.
- **Working-Capital Safe Harbor:** A plan-based allowance to hold cash while it is deployed.
- **Annual Reporting:** The yearly Form 8996 compliance filing.
- **Monthly Penalty:** The shortfall-based charge for failing the **90%** test.
- **Reasonable-Cause Exception:** A justified failure that can avoid the penalty.
- **QOF Status:** The fund's qualification, which persistent non-compliance can threaten.
- **Form 8997:** The investor form that tracks QOF investments and deferred gains.
- **Eligible Entity:** A corporation or partnership that can serve as a QOF.
- **Organizing Documents:** Documents that should reflect the Opportunity Zone investment purpose.
- **Compliance Records:** Documentation that supports a fund's qualification.
- **Due Diligence:** Evaluation of a fund's certification and compliance.
