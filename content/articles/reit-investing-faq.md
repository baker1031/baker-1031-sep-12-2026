# REIT Investing FAQ

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep hearing the same practical questions about REITs: what do I actually own, how does it pay me, and when does it belong in a portfolio? The answers are straightforward, but the trade-offs are not. This FAQ covers REIT basics, income, risks, liquidity, taxes, 1031 exchanges, and the difference between a REIT and a DST.

First-order thinking treats a REIT as a high-yield stock. Second-order thinking starts with the properties, leverage, rate sensitivity, tax character, liquidity terms, and whether the investment solves the right problem. That distinction matters before capital is committed.

## Key Takeaways

- A REIT owns income-producing real estate and pays most of its income to shareholders as dividends—similar to a mutual fund for real estate.
- REITs primarily pay dividends under the 90% distribution rule, with possible appreciation in equity REIT shares.
- Whether a REIT is appropriate depends on income, diversification, and liquidity goals weighed against rate sensitivity, market or illiquidity risk, and ordinary-income taxation.
- REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property, so they are not 1031 eligible. A DST is the passive, 1031-eligible real-estate alternative.

If you are new to real estate investment trusts, it is reasonable to ask what one is, how it pays, whether it is a good investment, how it differs from a DST, and [how to get started](reit-investing-for-beginners-how-to-get-started.html). Because REITs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, their yields have historically tended to run higher than the broad stock market. The answers below are educational, not investment, tax, or legal advice.

## What Is a REIT in Simple Terms?

A REIT is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders. Think of it as a mutual fund for real estate: rather than buying, financing, and managing a building, an investor buys a share of a professionally managed portfolio. It can own apartments, industrial buildings, offices, retail, healthcare real estate, hotels, self-storage, or [data centers](data-center-reits-explained.html).

To qualify, a REIT must follow federal rules, including distributing at least 90% of taxable income, holding most assets in real estate, and earning most income from real-estate sources. That structure avoids corporate-level tax on qualifying income and creates the dividend emphasis. See [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html) for broader context.

## How Do REITs Pay Investors?

Equity REITs earn rent and, after expenses, distribute most income as regular dividends, often monthly or quarterly. Mortgage REITs finance real estate and earn interest on mortgages or mortgage-backed securities. The 90% distribution rule drives this high-payout model; many REITs distribute close to 100% of taxable income. Because most earnings pass through rather than being retained, REIT yields have historically tended to run higher than the broad stock market. Equity shares can also appreciate or decline with portfolio value and market pricing.

Dividends are targets, not guarantees. A REIT can reduce or suspend a payout if property income, occupancy, financing costs, or coverage weaken.

## Are REITs a Good Investment?

It depends. Potential benefits include income, professionally managed and diversified real-estate exposure—one REIT can own many properties, and real estate has historically behaved somewhat differently from stocks and bonds—plus public-share liquidity and access to property types that may be difficult to own directly. Professional management also removes the direct owner's tenant, repair, and financing work. Risks include interest-rate sensitivity, public-market volatility, leverage, property and sector weakness, dividend cuts, and, for private or non-traded REITs, illiquidity, redemption limits, and historically higher fees.

Most REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income, though qualified REIT dividends may receive a 20% Section 199A deduction that the 2025 OBBBA legislation made permanent. A good fit is determined by goals, horizon, risk tolerance, taxes, and liquidity needs—not by yield alone.

## REIT vs. DST: What's the Difference?

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) is fractional, passive real estate that can qualify as replacement property in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). A REIT share is a security and is [not eligible for a 1031 exchange](reits-and-1031-exchanges-why-reit-shares-dont-qualify.html). That difference often controls the choice after a property sale where tax deferral is the objective.

Public REIT shares trade daily. A DST is illiquid and generally held until the sponsor sells the property, often several years. REITs typically own diversified portfolios, while a DST may own one or a limited set of properties. A DST can later participate in a 721 roll-up to an [UPREIT](721-exchange-guide.html), but that is not guaranteed.

## How Do I Start Investing in REITs?

Public REITs and REIT funds or ETFs can be purchased through a brokerage account, often in small dollar amounts. This route has low minimums, daily liquidity, and transparent pricing, and is available to virtually any investor without special qualification. Non-traded and private REITs are not exchange-traded; they are offered through a broker-dealer, often with minimum investments and a suitability review. Private REITs generally require verified accredited investors, while non-traded REITs may require accredited or otherwise suitable investors depending on the offering.

Read the prospectus or private placement memorandum, understand strategy, fees, leverage, distributions, redemption terms, and tax treatment, and evaluate whether the capital can be illiquid. Start small, diversify, and size the allocation to fit your overall plan. A higher yield is not a substitute for due diligence.

## REIT Fees and Taxes to Understand First

Public REIT shares generally have ordinary brokerage costs, often zero commission, and REIT funds or ETFs have expense ratios. Non-traded REITs historically have higher loads, dealer-manager, asset-management, and other fees. Private REIT costs and liquidity terms also deserve close review.

Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed at ordinary rates because the REIT did not pay corporate tax. Qualified REIT dividends can receive a 20% Section 199A deduction. Distributions can also include capital-gain components or return of capital, which reduces basis and defers tax. Review Form 1099-DIV and confirm specific treatment with a CPA.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a REIT in simple terms?

A REIT is a company owning, operating, or financing income-producing real estate that pays most taxable income to shareholders as dividends. It provides a mutual-fund-like way to own diversified real estate without managing properties.

### How do REITs pay investors?

Mostly through dividends: equity REITs distribute rental income after expenses, often monthly or quarterly; mortgage REITs distribute interest income. The 90% distribution rule requires at least 90% of taxable income to be paid out, which is why REIT yields have historically tended to run higher than the broad stock market. Equity shares may also appreciate or decline.

### Are REITs a good investment?

They can provide income, diversification, professional management, and public-market liquidity. One REIT can own many properties, and real estate has historically behaved somewhat differently from stocks and bonds; professional management means no direct tenant, repair, or financing work. They also carry rate, property, leverage, market, dividend-cut, tax, and—when non-traded or private—illiquidity risk. Fit depends on investor goals and circumstances.

### What is the difference between a REIT and a DST?

A REIT share is a security and cannot serve as 1031 replacement property. A DST is fractional, passive real estate that can qualify for 1031 treatment. Public REITs are liquid; DSTs are generally illiquid, held to term, and may be less diversified.

### How do I start investing in REITs?

Buy public shares or REIT funds through a brokerage account, or access non-traded and private REITs through a broker-dealer after suitability review. Public shares and funds generally offer low minimums, daily liquidity, and transparent pricing and are broadly available. Read offering documents, fees, liquidity terms, strategy, and risks before investing; start small, diversify, and size the allocation to fit your overall plan.

### Why are REIT yields higher than stock yields?

REITs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, so more cash flow is paid out instead of retained and yields tend to run higher than the broad stock market. The trade-off is that REITs cannot easily fund growth from retained earnings, so they often raise new capital by issuing shares or debt. A high yield can also signal risk or an unsustainable dividend; it is not a guarantee.

### Are REIT dividends taxed?

Mostly as ordinary income. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the 20% Section 199A deduction. Some payments can be capital-gain distributions or return of capital. Form 1099-DIV reports the character; consult a CPA.

### Are REITs liquid?

Publicly traded REITs are liquid because shares trade on exchanges. Non-traded and private REITs are illiquid and rely on limited redemption programs, commonly capped around 2% of NAV per month and 5% per quarter, which may be delayed or suspended.

### Can I invest in REITs with a small amount of money?

Usually in publicly traded REITs, funds, and ETFs, where an investor can buy one share or use fractional shares. A fund or ETF can spread a modest amount across many REITs. That contrasts with direct real estate ownership, which typically requires a substantial down payment, financing, and ongoing capital. Non-traded and private REITs generally have larger minimum investments.

### What are the main risks of investing in REITs?

Interest-rate sensitivity, share-price volatility, property and tenant risk, leverage, refinancing, dividend cuts, ordinary-income tax treatment, and—in non-traded or private REITs—illiquidity, fees, and limited redemptions.

### What's the difference between equity, mortgage, and hybrid REITs?

Equity REITs own property and earn rent. Mortgage REITs finance property and earn mortgage interest, often with greater rate and leverage sensitivity. Hybrid REITs own property and hold mortgage debt.

### Can I use a REIT in a 1031 exchange?

Not directly. REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property. A DST can be used as 1031 replacement property, and a later 721 or UPREIT transaction may be possible under its own terms.

### Should I hold REITs in a retirement account?

It depends. In a traditional IRA, ordinary REIT income is not taxed currently; in a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals can be tax-free. A taxable account can use the 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends, but that deduction applies only in taxable accounts. Account placement is individual; discuss it with a CPA.

### How are REITs different from REIT mutual funds or ETFs?

A direct REIT investment holds one company, its strategy, sector, and management. A fund or ETF holds many REITs, reducing the risk that any single REIT's problems hurt you disproportionately and providing diversified exposure across multiple sectors and management teams. Individual shares offer more control to target specific sectors or companies but concentrate risk; a fund or ETF charges an expense ratio and can still decline with the REIT market.

## Glossary

### REIT

A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.

### Equity REIT

A REIT that owns property and earns income from rents.

### Mortgage REIT (mREIT)

A REIT that finances real estate and earns mortgage interest.

### Hybrid REIT

A REIT that both owns property and holds mortgage debt.

### Dividend

The distribution a REIT pays shareholders from its income.

### 90% Distribution Rule

The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

### Yield

Annual dividend income expressed as a percentage of share price.

### Publicly Traded REIT

An exchange-listed, liquid, market-priced REIT.

### Non-Traded REIT

An SEC-registered but unlisted, illiquid REIT.

### REIT ETF / Fund

A fund holding many REITs for diversified exposure.

### Section 199A Deduction

The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

### Form 1099-DIV

The form reporting REIT dividends and their tax character.

### Return of Capital

A distribution that reduces basis rather than being taxed currently.

### Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)

1031-eligible fractional real estate (unlike a REIT share).

### 721 / UPREIT Exchange

Contributing property to a REIT for OP units, preserving deferral.

### Suitability Review

Assessing whether a REIT offering fits the investor.
