# Are Private REITs Safe? Understanding the Risks

I keep seeing a familiar comfort in private-REIT discussions: a statement whose price barely moves and a distribution that arrives on schedule. It can look calmer than the stock market. Calm on a statement, however, is not the same thing as safety in an investment.

The useful question is not whether a private REIT is automatically safe or unsafe. It is what risks the portfolio carries, when those risks become visible, and whether the investor can live with them. A private REIT can be a sound income-producing holding. It can also be a leveraged, illiquid position whose trouble is simply not displayed every day.

First-order thinking stops at the smooth NAV and the headline distribution. Second-order thinking asks what sits underneath: the buildings, tenants, debt, appraisal assumptions, sponsor, concentration, and terms governing an exit. This is educational information, not investment, legal, or tax advice, and no return, distribution, or tax result is assured.

For the underlying structure, see our [2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## Key takeaways

- A stable appraisal-based price is not proof of low risk. It is a price that is not marked to a public market daily, so the risk often appears at redemption or sale rather than on a screen.
- The main risks are illiquidity and redemption gates, appraisal lag and judgment, leverage, concentration, interest-rate exposure, and sponsor quality.
- Distributions are not guaranteed. They can include return of capital or even new investor money, which can make a yield look better while eroding the REIT.
- A redemption program is conditional. Lock-ups, caps, and a sponsor’s right to suspend redemptions mean exit can be least dependable when markets are under stress.
- REIT asset and income tests, professional management, diversification, and suitability review are meaningful protections. They are still lighter than the reporting and market discipline of a public REIT.
- A private REIT may fit an accredited investor who wants real-estate income and accepts the trade-offs. The judgment should be made offering by offering, based on the portfolio and sponsor—not the smooth line alone.

## What “safe” really means here

The question usually arrives after an investor has seen steady distributions and a price that does not lurch with the stock market. That presentation can make a private REIT feel safe. But it blends two very different ideas: stability of a quoted price and actual investment risk.

A publicly traded REIT is marked to a live market throughout the day, so the price responds immediately to changes in rates, sentiment, property values, and risk appetite. A private or non-traded REIT normally uses periodically appraised **net asset value**, or NAV. The buildings, debt, and tenants still bear the same kinds of real-estate risk. The difference is that the risk is not visibly repriced each day; it may show up when an investor seeks redemption or when assets are sold.

That is why “stable price” and “low risk” are not synonyms. The job is to examine the portfolio and the people running it, one risk at a time.

## Liquidity risk and redemption gates

Illiquidity is the most concrete issue. A private REIT has no public market where an investor can simply sell shares to another buyer. Instead, exit typically depends on a **redemption program**. Those programs may include an initial lock-up, periodic redemption windows, caps on total redemptions in a period, and a sponsor’s discretion to limit or suspend redemptions entirely.

The constraints can stack. A lock-up can prevent any redemption for a year or more. A quarterly cap—often a small percentage of net assets—can mean that a request is prorated and paid over several quarters. A suspension right can stop the program altogether.

That discretion serves a legitimate purpose: it can prevent forced property sales into a weak market and protect the remaining portfolio. But it also means the ability to get capital back is real only on specified terms, and can be least reliable when many investors want out at once. Treat this capital as money you can leave invested for years. Size the position so a multi-year hold is an inconvenience rather than a crisis, and read redemption terms as carefully as return projections. A steady price offers little comfort if the gate is down when cash is needed.

## Valuation risk

Without a live market price, a private REIT is generally priced through appraisal-based NAV, updated periodically. That creates two related concerns: judgment and lag. Cap rates, rent growth, and discount rates are inputs on which reasonable appraisers can disagree. NAV is therefore an estimate, not a market verdict.

It can also be stale when conditions are changing fastest. New subscriptions and redemptions occur at that NAV, so a lagging value can advantage a redeeming investor or a subscribing investor, depending on which way the market has moved. That does not make NAV dishonest or inherently wrong. It means the process matters.

Ask who conducts the appraisals, whether they are independent, how frequently NAV is recalculated, what assumptions are used, and how the NAV behaved during the last period of market stress. A sponsor that explains those answers gives an investor a way to test the number. One that is vague asks for faith. A NAV that holds suspiciously steady while comparable public real estate falls may be an appraisal that has not caught up.

## Leverage and concentration risk

Debt magnifies both gains and losses. A private REIT with meaningful debt maturing in a weak market faces **refinancing risk**: if it cannot roll loans on acceptable terms, it may cut distributions, sell assets at an unfavorable time, or dilute investors to raise equity. The amount of debt, the fixed-versus-floating-rate mix, and the maturity schedule are among the most important facts in an offering document.

A modestly leveraged portfolio with staggered fixed-rate maturities is in a different position from a heavily leveraged portfolio with a near-term wall of floating-rate debt. The details are knowable. They should not be skipped.

Concentration is the other structural axis. A REIT concentrated in one sector, such as office, or one region will rise and fall with that area’s fortunes. A broad portfolio can cushion the effect when a market or property type struggles. Focus is not automatically bad; a specialist can outperform. But it raises the stakes on the sponsor’s judgment and the selected sector’s outlook.

Read the portfolio plainly: how much debt is there, what kind of debt is it, when does it mature, how many properties are owned, and how diverse are the property types and markets? Those facts usually say more about risk than the smoothness of a reported NAV.

## Interest-rate and property-market risk

Private REITs are not insulated from the forces that move commercial real estate. Higher interest rates raise borrowing costs on new and refinanced debt, which can pressure cash flow and distributions. They also tend to push property cap rates higher, which lowers property values. A public REIT often reflects that quickly in its share price; a private REIT tends to reflect it more slowly through NAV. The economics of the buildings are still the same.

The same holds for rising vacancy, falling market rents, a weak sales environment, and tenant-credit problems. Those conditions affect a private REIT just as they affect a directly owned building. Appraisal-based pricing changes the timing and visibility of the effect, not the exposure itself.

## Sponsor risk and distribution sustainability

As with a [DST](dst-guide.html), the sponsor is central. The sponsor’s ability to acquire, finance, and manage the portfolio largely determines the outcome. Private vehicles often operate with lighter regulation and disclosure than public ones, which makes sponsor integrity and skill more consequential.

Look for a long, verifiable record through full market cycles, including downturns. Review unsuccessful offerings as well as the ones that went well. Ask whether investor capital remained intact through a recession. A long record does not eliminate risk, but it is more informative than a polished presentation.

Distribution sustainability deserves its own question. A payout funded from property cash flow is different from one funded partly by **return of capital** or, worse, new investor money. Return of capital reduces an investor’s basis and is not automatically a problem in a depreciation-heavy structure. But a distribution that repeatedly exceeds operating cash flow can make yield look attractive while quietly eroding the REIT.

Ask directly what funds the distribution. A sponsor that can show the operating-cash-flow source gives a meaningful answer. A sponsor that cannot, or will not, gives a reason to slow down.

## What protections do exist

Private REITs are not unregulated free-for-alls. The REIT form includes asset and income tests intended to keep the company genuinely invested in real estate, and it requires distribution of most taxable income. Investors receive professional management and usually more diversification than a single property or syndication can provide. Access is commonly through broker-dealers and advisers that conduct a suitability review, and third-party due-diligence reports may be commissioned on the sponsor and offering.

Those are real protections. They distinguish a registered or properly placed private REIT from an unvetted private deal. They are, however, lighter than the continuous disclosure, audited public reporting, and market discipline a publicly traded REIT faces. They reduce risk without eliminating it, and they are not a substitute for an investor’s own diligence.

## Putting the risks in perspective

Naming risks is not the same as condemning the structure. Every real-estate investment has them. In some respects, a diversified, professionally managed private REIT can be less concentrated than a single rental property or a one-deal syndication. One failing asset may be a fraction of a portfolio rather than the entire investment. That is a genuine benefit.

The important distinction is visibility, not whether risk exists. A public REIT shows its risk every day in a moving price. That can be uncomfortable, but it is immediate. A private REIT can carry related real-estate risk with a calmer statement, and the danger is mistaking that calm for safety and skipping the work.

The practical posture is neither fear nor false comfort. Underwrite the sponsor, leverage, diversification, and redemption terms. Size the position to survive a closed gate. Then ask a useful test question: would you be comfortable owning this investment if its price were quoted daily and moved like a public REIT? If the honest answer is no, the steady statement may be doing the persuading rather than the underlying real estate.

## How to gauge whether one is right for you

A private REIT may be appropriate for an accredited investor who wants diversified real-estate income, has done the diligence, and can accept illiquidity for years. It is less appropriate for someone who may need the capital soon or is drawn in mainly by a smooth price and headline yield. The structure is neither a safe haven nor a trap; it is a long-term, illiquid real-estate investment whose risks are less visible than a public REIT’s.

Look beyond the line on the statement. Review the sponsor’s record, leverage and maturity schedule, diversification, interest-rate exposure, distribution coverage, fees, and redemption terms. Our [how-to-invest memo](how-to-invest-in-a-private-reit.html) lays out the same discipline, and [private vs. public REITs](private-reit-vs-public-reit.html) compares the structures directly. Weigh any specific offering with your own advisers before investing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are private REITs safer than public REITs because the price is stable?

No. A private REIT’s price is stable because it is appraisal-based and not marked to a public market every day, not because underlying risk is lower. Real-estate, leverage, interest-rate, and liquidity risk remain. They simply tend to appear at redemption or sale rather than in a daily quote.

### What is a redemption gate?

A redemption gate is a limit or suspension on redemptions that a private or non-traded REIT can impose, often when markets are stressed and many investors seek an exit. It can protect the remaining portfolio from forced sales, but it makes the investor’s ability to exit conditional exactly when cash may be most wanted.

### What are the main risks of a private REIT?

The core risks are illiquidity and redemption gates, appraisal-based valuations that may lag, portfolio leverage and concentration, interest-rate and broader property-market exposure, sponsor quality under lighter disclosure, and distributions that may include return of capital. Offering documents should allow each to be assessed.

### How can I tell if a private REIT’s distribution is sustainable?

Ask whether it is funded by property cash flow or partly by return of capital or new investor money. A distribution that consistently exceeds operating cash flow can make the yield look better while eroding the REIT. A transparent sponsor should identify the source; evasion is a warning sign.

### Does an interest-rate rise affect a private REIT even if the price is steady?

Yes. Higher rates raise borrowing and refinancing costs and tend to lower property values, which can pressure distributions and NAV. A public REIT generally reflects the change right away; a private REIT records it later through periodic NAV. The economic effect on the buildings is the same, while timing and visibility differ.

### What protections do private REIT investors have?

REIT asset and income tests keep the company invested in real estate, and the form requires distribution of most income. Investors also receive professional management and usually diversification, while advisers conduct suitability review and often obtain third-party diligence. These are real protections, but they are lighter than a public REIT’s disclosure and market discipline.

### Who should consider a private REIT?

An accredited investor who wants diversified real-estate income, has completed due diligence, and can accept years of illiquidity may consider one. It is not a fit for an investor who needs liquidity soon or who is relying only on a steady price and yield. Size the position so a closed gate would be an inconvenience, not a crisis.

## Glossary

- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The appraisal-based per-share value used to price private and non-traded REITs instead of a live market price.
- **Redemption Gate:** A limit or suspension on redemptions a REIT can impose, often during market stress, to avoid forced selling.
- **Lock-Up:** An initial period after investing when redemptions are not permitted at all.
- **Distribution Coverage:** Whether distributions are funded by operating cash flow rather than return of capital or new investor money.
- **Leverage:** Borrowed money used to finance a portfolio; it magnifies returns and losses and creates refinancing risk.
- **Refinancing Risk:** The risk that maturing debt cannot be rolled on acceptable terms, requiring distribution cuts, asset sales, or dilution.
- **Concentration Risk:** Exposure created by a portfolio focused on one sector or region instead of broad diversification.
- **Return of Capital:** A distribution that gives back part of an investor’s own investment; it reduces basis and can mask a payout not covered by income.
