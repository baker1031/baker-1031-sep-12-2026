# Working Interest vs. Royalty Interest

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Oil & Gas Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 13 min read

I have been thinking about how often a tax deduction is allowed to stand in for a risk decision. In oil and gas, the difference between a working interest and a royalty interest is not a minor technical choice. It decides who gets the deductions, who receives the costs, and who must live with the liability.

First-order thinking focuses on the first-year write-off or the cost-free revenue check. Second-order thinking starts with the operating obligation beneath each one: a working interest shares in the well's economics and liabilities; a royalty interest collects a share of production revenue without carrying those operating costs. The return profile follows from that distinction.

The two main ways to own oil & gas income are opposites in almost every way that matters — costs, liability, taxes, and risk. Here's how working and royalty interests differ.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">Investors entering oil and gas quickly meet two terms that define very different experiences: the working interest and the royalty interest. One bears the costs and risks of producing oil and gas — and earns the big tax deductions; the other collects a share of revenue free of costs and liability, but without those deductions. They sit at opposite ends of the risk-and-reward spectrum, and choosing between them is the first real decision an oil and gas investor makes. This memo lays out the differences that matter.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>A working interest bears the costs and liability of operations and earns the deductions; a royalty interest collects cost-free revenue with no liability.</li>
<li>Working-interest losses are treated as active and can offset W-2 and business income; royalty income is passive.</li>
<li>Working interests carry the IDC and depreciation deductions; royalty interests get depletion but not drilling deductions.</li>
<li>Working interests offer higher potential return and higher risk; royalties offer simpler, lower-risk passive income.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 id="core">The core distinction</h2>
<p>The cleanest way to hold the difference is by who bears costs. A <strong>working interest</strong> is the operating interest: it owns a share of the well's operations, pays its share of the costs of drilling and production, and in return receives a share of the revenue and the tax deductions. A <strong>royalty interest</strong> is a share of production revenue carved out <em>free of</em> operating costs — the royalty owner simply receives a percentage of what the well produces, off the top, without paying to drill or operate it. That single difference — bearing costs versus not — drives everything else: liability, taxation, and risk. These interests also connect to our pillar on <a href="mineral-rights-1031-guide.html">mineral rights and royalties</a>, which can have their own 1031 implications.</p>
<h2 id="working">Working interest: costs, deductions, liability</h2>
<p>Owning a working interest means you're an active participant in the venture's economics. You pay your proportional share of drilling and operating costs, including the <a href="intangible-drilling-costs.html">intangible drilling costs</a> that generate the large first-year deduction, and you depreciate the tangible equipment. You also receive your share of revenue when the well produces. Critically, a working interest carries <strong>liability</strong> — you're exposed to the costs and obligations of operations, including potential cost overruns or environmental and legal liabilities, depending on how the interest is held. The working interest is the high-engagement, high-deduction, higher-risk side of oil and gas, and it's the side that delivers the sector's signature tax benefits.</p>
<h2 id="royalty">Royalty interest: cost-free, passive income</h2>
<p>A royalty interest is the opposite posture: passive, cost-free, and lower-risk. As a royalty owner you receive a share of gross production revenue without paying operating costs and without bearing the operational liabilities the working interest carries. The trade-off is that you don't get the drilling deductions — no IDCs, no equipment depreciation — though royalty income does benefit from the <a href="oil-gas-depletion-allowance.html">depletion allowance</a>, which shelters a portion of it. Royalty interests appeal to investors who want exposure to oil and gas income without the costs, complexity, and liability of operations — closer to collecting rent than running a business.</p>
<h2 id="tax">The tax differences: active vs. passive</h2>
<p>The tax treatment follows from who bears the costs. <strong>Working-interest income and losses are generally treated as active</strong>, not passive — a deliberate exception in the tax code that, as our <a href="oil-gas-losses-w2-income.html">W-2 offset memo</a> explains, lets working-interest losses (driven by IDCs) offset active income like wages and business profits. <strong>Royalty income is passive</strong>, taxed as it's received and reduced by depletion, but without the active-loss benefit. So the working interest offers both the bigger deductions and the more favorable loss treatment, while the royalty offers simpler, steadier income. As always, AMT and at-risk rules can affect the working-interest side, so model it with a CPA.</p>
<h2 id="risk">Risk and return profiles</h2>
<p>The two also differ sharply in risk. A <strong>working interest</strong> can deliver outsized returns if the well is productive — you're sharing directly in the upside — but you also bear the cost of dry holes, cost overruns, and operational problems, plus liability exposure. A <strong>royalty interest</strong> is lower-risk: your downside is generally limited to the value of the interest, you never pay operating costs, and you have no operational liability, but your upside is capped at your share of revenue and you bear commodity-price and production risk. Higher engagement and deductions on one side; simplicity and lower risk on the other.</p>

<h2 id="which">Which fits your situation</h2>
<p>Choose by what you want from the investment. A <strong>working interest</strong> fits a high-income, risk-tolerant investor who specifically wants the large first-year deductions and the ability to offset active income, and who can accept the costs, liability, and higher risk that come with operations. A <strong>royalty interest</strong> fits an investor who wants simpler, lower-risk, cost-free passive income from oil and gas without operational exposure or the need for deductions. Many investors' real question is whether they're after the <em>tax shelter</em> (working interest) or the <em>income</em> (royalty), and answering that usually settles the choice. Either way, the underlying commodity and production risk remains, so weigh the specific offering and your situation with your advisors.</p>

<summary>What's the difference between a working interest and a royalty interest?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A working interest pays operating costs and carries liability but earns the deductions and a share of revenue; a royalty interest receives cost-free revenue with no liability and no drilling deductions (though depletion applies).</p></div>

<summary>Which one gets the tax deductions?<span class="ic"></span></summary>

<summary>Is royalty income active or passive?<span class="ic"></span></summary>

<details>
<summary>Which is riskier?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>The working interest. It can deliver higher returns but bears the cost of dry holes, overruns, and operational liability. A royalty interest is lower-risk and cost-free, with upside limited to its share of revenue.</p></div>
</details>

<summary>Which should I choose?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>It depends on your goal: a working interest if you want the large deductions and active-loss offset and can accept the cost, liability, and risk; a royalty interest if you want simpler, lower-risk passive income without deductions.</p></div>

<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>Working Interest</dt><dd>The operating interest in a well that pays costs, carries liability, and earns the related deductions and revenue.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Royalty Interest</dt><dd>A cost-free share of production revenue with no operating liability and no drilling deductions.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Depletion Allowance</dt><dd>A deduction that shelters part of oil and gas income; available to both interest types.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Active vs. Passive Income</dt><dd>Working-interest income is generally active; royalty income is passive, affecting how losses can be used.</dd></div>
</dl>

<h2 id="which-should-you-choose">Which should you choose?</h2>
<p>A working interest fits investors willing to bear operating costs and liability in exchange for the related deductions and a higher-potential, active return. A royalty interest fits those who want a cost-free, passive, lower-risk share of production revenue with no operating liability.</p>
<p>The active-versus-passive distinction also shapes how losses can be used, so weigh your appetite for risk and liability alongside your tax situation — and consult your CPA before deciding.</p>
