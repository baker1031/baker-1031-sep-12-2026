# Self-Storage DSTs: Recession-Resilient Income

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the same small decision play out: a household needs room for a move, an estate, a divorce, or a smaller home, and a storage unit becomes the practical answer. It is easy to call that a niche service. It is more useful to see it as a business tied to change, which happens in every part of the cycle.

A self-storage DST holds one or more storage facilities. Investors own fractional beneficial interests that can qualify as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, letting them defer capital-gains tax while gaining professionally managed, income-producing exposure. The attraction is a mix of life-event demand, low operating expenses, and short leases that give operators frequent opportunities to reprice rents. The risks are just as real: local new supply, active operating demands, financing, and a multi-year illiquid holding period.

For broader context, see [our complete 2026 guide to Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html).

The first-order view is that self-storage is recession-resilient, so it must be a safe income investment. Second-order thinking asks a harder set of questions: Is the trade area already supplied? What is coming out of the ground nearby? Can the operator protect occupancy and rates? And are the projected distributions consistent with those local conditions? The sector story matters. The property, sponsor, and market decide whether that story is worth underwriting.

## Why Self-Storage Holds Up

People rent storage because life is changing, not simply because the economy is strong. In good times, households accumulate possessions and need a place to put them. In weaker periods, households downsize, relocate, combine living arrangements, or settle estates. The sector can therefore find demand on both sides of a downturn, unlike property types that rely more directly on discretionary spending or business expansion.

Industry observers often describe this demand through the four Ds: death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing. An estate must be settled. A household splits. A family moves for work or between homes. Someone trades a larger home for a smaller one. These are continuing, non-discretionary events. They help explain why self-storage occupancy has historically been more stable than occupancy in more cycle-sensitive property types.

That historical stability is not a promise. It is a reason to begin the work, not a reason to stop it. A facility still competes within a local trade area, and a wave of new facilities can overwhelm even durable demand.

## Demand Drivers & Low Op-Ex

The four Ds are only part of the picture. Households tend to accumulate more possessions over time. Smaller urban living spaces leave less room for them. Nearby, climate-controlled storage has become a routine service for many people. Population growth, household formation, and residential mobility all contribute to demand in a particular trade area.

That is why a serious operator studies local demographics and competing facilities rather than relying on a national story. A growing population helps, but it does not erase a nearby construction pipeline. Residential mobility helps, but it does not guarantee a given facility can hold its rates.

The cost structure is another important feature. A storage facility generally needs only a small onsite or remote management team. Its buildings need modest maintenance compared with apartments or offices: there are no kitchens, plumbing-heavy units, or extensive tenant build-outs to maintain. A vacant unit generally needs cleaning and re-renting rather than a major turnover program.

Low payroll, maintenance, and tenant-improvement costs can allow a well-run facility to convert a high share of rental revenue into net operating income (NOI). That lean profile is a source of strong margins. It is not a substitute for management. Marketing, rate setting, customer service, collections, security, and basic maintenance still determine whether the facility earns those margins.

## Income Characteristics

Self-storage leases are typically short, often month-to-month. That is very different from an office or net-lease property with a multi-year contract. A storage operator can raise existing-customer rates on a rolling basis and reprice vacant units to the market. In a healthy market with limited new supply, that can give the operator meaningful pricing power and allow rents to respond to inflation rather than remaining fixed for years.

For a DST investor, this creates the possibility of current income with room to grow. Tenants often do not move belongings over a modest monthly increase because moving is costly and inconvenient. That can help an operator increase rents while retaining occupancy, supporting NOI alongside the sector's low operating costs.

The same feature creates downside. If a local market softens, rents can decline quickly. Short leases are not contractually locked income in the way a long net lease can be. In other words, the mechanism that can raise income in a tight market can lower it in an oversupplied one.

## Risks: Supply & Management

Local oversupply is the principal risk to understand. Storage facilities are comparatively cheap and quick to build. Construction is relatively simple and does not require complex tenant build-outs. When a market looks attractive, multiple developers can arrive at the same conclusion. If supply outruns local demand, operators may face lower occupancy, lower rents, concessions, or all three.

This is a local business. A national report can look healthy while a facility's own trade area is dealing with several new competitors. The relevant questions are how many facilities already exist, what is under construction or planned nearby, and whether household and population growth can absorb it.

Self-storage is also more management-intensive than its passive image implies. The DST investor is passive; the underlying business is not. Revenue depends on marketing to fill units, active rate management, customer service, collections, and security. A sponsor with scale, brand recognition, and capable revenue-management systems may sustain occupancy and rents better than a weaker operator.

Investors also face sponsor, market, tenant, financing, fee, and leverage risk. DST interests are illiquid for the duration of the hold. These considerations are not footnotes to the resilience thesis. They are the conditions that determine whether it holds for a particular investment.

### Key Takeaways

- Self-storage has a recession-resilient reputation because demand comes from the four Ds and consumer accumulation in good times and bad.
- Lean staffing, modest maintenance, and limited build-out needs can let well-run facilities convert a high share of revenue into income.
- Short, month-to-month leases can support pricing power and inflation responsiveness, but rents can fall quickly in a soft local market.
- Low barriers to construction and active operating demands make local supply, sponsor quality, and management execution decisive.

## Sample Self-Storage Offerings

It helps to picture the typical structure without treating it as a promise. A self-storage DST may hold one facility or a small portfolio of facilities. It may use an established storage brand or a sponsor with a national management platform. The sponsor may select markets for favorable supply-and-demand characteristics. Offering materials typically describe location, rentable square footage, unit count, occupancy and rate history, and whether units are climate-controlled.

The minimum investment is commonly in the range of roughly $25,000 to $100,000. The trust may be all-cash, meaning debt-free, or it may use a pre-arranged non-recourse loan at the trust level. Materials also disclose projected distributions, which are estimates rather than guarantees, the fee and load structure, the expected hold period, often around five to seven years, and the business plan. That plan may involve improving occupancy, raising rents, or preparing for an eventual sale.

Those are characteristics to evaluate, not return promises. They provide a way to [compare DST and NNN deals across the marketplace](1031-exchange-marketplace-finding-dst-nnn-deals.html) on substance instead of headline yield.

## Evaluating a Self-Storage DST

Start with the market because the sector's risk is local and operational. Review the supply-and-demand balance in the trade area: existing competitors, planned or under-construction facilities, and whether population and household growth can absorb incremental space. A growing, supply-constrained market may have more room to preserve occupancy and rents. A market with high existing supply and a wave of new construction faces headwinds.

Then look at the property and operator. Current and historical occupancy and rate trends show whether the facility has been performing and whether it has had pricing power. Sponsor track record, scale, brand, and revenue-management capability matter because the business needs active operation.

Finally, read the offering economics: projected distributions, fees and load, leverage, expected hold period, and business plan. Each projected distribution is an estimate, not a guarantee. None of these factors works alone. A capable operator can struggle in a badly oversupplied market, and a good market can underperform under weak management.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a self-storage DST?

A self-storage DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust that owns one or more income-producing storage facilities. Investors own fractional beneficial interests. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured interest can be treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, allowing an investor to sell appreciated real estate and reinvest in the DST while deferring capital-gains tax.

The trust is professionally managed. A sponsor and operator run the facilities while investors receive their pro-rata share of net rental income. The appeal combines passive 1031-eligible exposure with life-event demand and a low operating-expense profile. DST interests remain securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review, and distributions are projected, never guaranteed.

### Why is self-storage considered recession-resilient?

Demand is tied to life events rather than only economic growth. The four Ds—death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing—can require storage in any economic environment. Consumer accumulation can add demand when conditions are strong; moving, combining households, and downsizing can add demand when conditions are weak. That has historically made occupancy more stable than in property types tied to discretionary spending or business expansion.

Resilient does not mean risk-free. Local oversupply can pressure occupancy and rents even when demand is steady. The sector remains subject to local market conditions and the usual DST risks, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### What are the four Ds of self-storage demand?

They are death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing. Death can require an estate to be settled and belongings stored or sorted. Divorce can split a household and create temporary storage needs. Dislocation includes job relocations, moves, and transitions between homes. Downsizing leaves households with more belongings than a smaller home can accommodate.

These events occur continuously in good economies and bad. They provide a baseline of demand less dependent on discretionary spending or business conditions. Local supply still matters greatly to the performance of an individual facility.

### Why does self-storage have low operating expenses?

Storage facilities usually require few staff, often a small onsite or remote management team. Buildings have no kitchens, plumbing-heavy units, or extensive tenant build-outs. When a unit is vacated, cleaning and re-renting it is usually much simpler than apartment turnover work.

Low payroll, maintenance, and tenant-improvement costs can allow a well-run facility to convert a high share of rental revenue into NOI, producing strong margins. The lean cost structure supports income, but revenue still depends on capable management filling units and managing rates.

### How does self-storage income work in a DST?

The facilities collect rent from unit renters. After operating expenses, management fees, and any debt service, net rental income is distributed to investors in proportion to their beneficial interests. Short, often month-to-month leases let operators raise existing-customer rents on a rolling basis and reprice vacancies to market.

That may support current income and inflation responsiveness in a healthy, supply-constrained market. The trade-off is that rents can also fall quickly when the local market weakens. Income is not contractually locked like a long net lease, and all DST distributions are projected estimates, never guarantees.

### What are the main risks of a self-storage DST?

The central sector risks are local oversupply and management intensity. Several cheap, quick-to-build facilities can arrive in the same trade area and pressure occupancy and rents. Revenue also depends on marketing, rate management, customer service, collections, and security, making operator quality important.

On top of that, investors face sponsor, market, tenant, financing, fees and load, and hold-period illiquidity risk. A review should include local supply, occupancy and rate trends, the operator, leverage, and the offering's projected—not guaranteed—distributions.

### Why is oversupply such a concern for self-storage?

Storage has a low barrier to entry. Facilities have simple designs, relatively inexpensive and fast construction, and no complex tenant build-outs. When conditions look favorable, several developers may build at once. The resulting supply can exceed what local demand can absorb, producing concessions, lower occupancy, and lower rents.

Because leases are short, rents may move down quickly. National vacancy figures say little about a particular facility. Review existing competitors plus facilities planned or under construction within the local trade area.

### Is self-storage really a passive investment?

For a DST investor, it is passive: the sponsor and operator run the facility and the investor receives a share of the income. The underlying business is active. It requires marketing, rate decisions, customer service, collections, security, and maintenance.

That is why sponsor and operator quality matter. A platform with scale, brand recognition, and sound revenue-management systems may sustain occupancy and rates better than a weaker one. The investor's role is passive, but the investment's result depends on active operation.

### Can I use a self-storage DST for a 1031 exchange?

Yes, a properly structured self-storage DST can be replacement property. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a DST beneficial interest as a direct interest in real property for 1031 purposes. An investor can work through a qualified intermediary to reinvest proceeds from appreciated investment real estate in a DST and defer capital-gains tax.

For full deferral, an investor generally must reinvest equal or greater equity and replace debt, identify the DST within the 45-day identification period, and close within 180 days. DSTs can move an investor from active ownership to passive management while preserving deferral. Confirm mechanics and individual eligibility with a qualified intermediary and tax advisor; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How long is the hold period for a self-storage DST?

Most self-storage DSTs use a multi-year hold, commonly around five to seven years, although the period varies by offering. During that time the sponsor operates the facilities and pursues the business plan, which may include raising occupancy or rents before an eventual sale. At sale, investors receive their pro-rata share of proceeds and may complete another 1031 exchange into new replacement property.

There is little or no secondary market. A DST interest is illiquid for the hold, so an investor should expect to remain invested through it rather than plan on an early sale. Sale timing depends on the sponsor and market conditions and is not guaranteed.

### What should I look for when evaluating a self-storage DST?

Begin with local supply and demand: existing facilities, the planned and under-construction pipeline, and the capacity of population and household growth to absorb it. Then examine current and historical occupancy and rate trends. Sponsor track record, scale, brand, and revenue-management capability deserve close attention.

Review projected distributions, the fee and load structure, property leverage, expected hold period, and business plan. A good operator in an oversupplied market can struggle, and a good market can be undermined by weak operation. Judge the factors together rather than choosing an offering solely because it has the highest projected yield.

### Are self-storage DST distributions guaranteed?

No. Figures in an offering are projections based on assumptions about occupancy, rental rates, expenses, and market conditions. Actual results can be higher or lower. If a trade area receives too much new supply, occupancy and rents can fall; short leases can make the impact arrive quickly.

Costs can rise, a property can underperform, and debt service can consume more cash flow than expected. A self-storage DST makes no promise to return capital or pay a set yield. Treat projected distributions as estimates, size an allocation for the uncertainty, and review the sponsor's assumptions and local market carefully. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How does self-storage compare to other DST property types?

Compared with a net-lease (NNN) DST, self-storage has short month-to-month leases instead of long-term, bond-like income from one creditworthy tenant. That offers more pricing flexibility and less contractual certainty. Compared with multifamily or [student housing DSTs](student-housing-dsts-pros-cons-and-risks.html), storage typically has lower operating expenses and turnover costs, though it still requires active management.

Its life-event demand can be more durable than demand in some cycle-sensitive sectors. The trade-off is local oversupply risk because storage is easy to build. Many investors consider it one component of a diversified DST allocation rather than a sole holding.

## Glossary

- **Self-Storage DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding income-producing storage facilities.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in which investors own fractional interests.
- **Beneficial Interest:** An investor's fractional, 1031-eligible ownership in a DST.
- **The Four Ds:** Death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing—storage demand drivers.
- **Recession-Resilient:** Demand that persists through economic downturns.
- **Operating Expenses (Op-Ex):** The costs of running a property; low for storage.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** Rental revenue minus operating expenses.
- **Month-to-Month Lease:** A short lease that lets operators reprice frequently.
- **Pricing Power:** The ability to raise rents without losing customers.
- **Oversupply:** Too much new storage built for local demand to absorb.
- **Trade Area:** The local market a storage facility draws customers from.
- **Occupancy:** The share of rentable units that are leased.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that structures and operates the DST.
- **1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange deferring capital-gains tax.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.
- **Non-Recourse Loan:** Trust-level debt for which investors are not personally liable.
