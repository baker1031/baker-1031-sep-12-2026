# DST Investing in California and the Clawback

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read

I keep seeing the California clawback described as though an out-of-state 1031 exchange erases a tax. It does not. It preserves California’s claim on the original California-source gain until that gain is recognized or otherwise resolved.

California investors face a combined state and federal tax burden on an appreciated property sale. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income at a top marginal rate of about 13.3%. A 1031 exchange can defer both federal and California gain, and a Delaware Statutory Trust can be a passive replacement-property option. Out-of-state DSTs add two California-specific requirements: the clawback on California-source gain and annual FTB Form 3840 reporting. This is educational information, not tax or legal advice. Current rules and individual outcomes belong with a CPA and attorney.

Before considering the state rules, review [our comprehensive Delaware Statutory Trust primer](dst-guide.html) for the structure of a DST investment.

## California's high tax burden

California’s treatment of capital gains as ordinary income makes a property sale costly at the margin. The top state rate is around 13.3%, in addition to federal capital-gains tax, the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), and [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html). A long-held property can therefore create a substantial combined tax bill.

A 1031 exchange defers both federal and California gain and can keep more capital in the replacement property. The state-tax deferral is proportionally more valuable in California than in a no-income-tax state such as Texas or Florida. A DST can be one replacement option for an owner moving from active rental management into passive real estate. The state’s large tax stake is also why it tracks California-source gains after they leave the state.

> At a top California rate near 13.3%, deferral can be worth more than it is in most states. That does not change the fact that a deferral still has terms and reporting obligations.

## The clawback provision explained

California’s clawback applies when an investor exchanges California property into replacement property outside the state. Consider an investor who exchanges a California rental into a DST holding property in Texas or Florida. The 1031 exchange defers federal and California gain. Without California’s rule, a later taxable sale of the out-of-state property could leave the original California-source gain outside the state’s tax reach.

The clawback preserves that claim. Deferred California-source gain remains subject to California tax. If the investor later sells the out-of-state replacement property in a taxable transaction, without another 1031 exchange, California taxes the original California-source gain even though the sold property is outside California. Moving the property does not eliminate California’s tax on California appreciation. It defers it. Successive 1031 exchanges continue the deferral; a future taxable sale triggers the claim.

First-order thinking asks whether an out-of-state DST escapes California tax. Second-order thinking recognizes that the out-of-state location can change income, property, and market exposure while the original California-source gain continues to be tracked by California. The clawback is a planning factor, not proof that an exchange is unavailable.

> Exchanging out of California does not make the California tax disappear. It follows the original California-source gain until recognition or other resolution.

## Exchanging into out-of-state DSTs

Many California investors do exchange into out-of-state DSTs. California property can be expensive and low-yielding relative to price. DSTs in [growing Sunbelt markets](sunbelt-vs-coastal-dst-markets.html), including [Texas](dst-investing-in-texas.html), [Florida](dst-investing-in-florida.html), Arizona, and the Carolinas, may offer different current-income, growth, and diversification opportunities.

The like-kind standard is broad: U.S. investment real estate can generally be exchanged for other U.S. investment real estate regardless of state. A California rental can be exchanged into a DST holding out-of-state property while deferring federal and California gain. The exchange itself is not taxed because of the clawback. The trade-off is that California’s original-gain claim remains in place, and annual reporting follows.

Some investors plan to keep deferring through additional 1031 exchanges. Others plan around a potential [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death, which may eliminate deferred gain under current law. These are individual tax and estate matters. A CPA should confirm the present rules and how they apply before the exchange.

## Ongoing reporting requirements

California tracks an out-of-state replacement through FTB Form 3840, the California Like-Kind Exchanges form. When a California taxpayer, or a taxpayer with a California filing obligation, exchanges California property into out-of-state replacement property, the form reports the exchange and deferred California-source gain.

It is not a one-time form. File Form 3840 for the exchange year and every subsequent year the out-of-state replacement property is held, until the gain is recognized through a taxable sale or otherwise resolved. The filing is an information return and does not itself trigger tax. Failing to file can lead California to estimate and assess the deferred gain as though it had become taxable. A CPA usually handles the annual filing with the California return.

For an out-of-state DST investor, the practical consequence is annual compliance for the duration of the hold and across further out-of-state exchanges. Requirements can change, so confirm current instructions with a CPA.

## Key Takeaways

- California taxes capital gains as ordinary income at up to about 13.3%, on top of federal capital-gains tax, the 3.8% net investment income tax, and depreciation recapture.
- A 1031 exchange can defer California and federal gain. The clawback preserves California’s claim on original California-source gain after an out-of-state exchange; it does not make the exchange taxable at inception.
- FTB Form 3840 reports deferred out-of-state California gain annually from the exchange year until recognition or another resolution.
- Plan the exchange, reporting, long-term gain strategy, and suitability with a qualified intermediary, CPA, attorney, and broker-dealer DST advisor.

## Managing the deferred gain over time

One approach is continued deferral. When a DST reaches the end of its hold and sells, the investor may complete another 1031 exchange into a new replacement property, including another DST. As long as the exchange chain continues, the original California-source gain is not recognized and the clawback is not triggered. Form 3840 reporting continues.

Another approach is a “swap till you drop” estate plan. An investor who holds continually exchanged interests until death may leave heirs a step-up in basis to fair market value, which can eliminate deferred federal gain and, in many cases, deferred California-source gain. Some investors also model the eventual clawback and plan for a taxable sale. State and estate rules are technical and can change, so these decisions belong with a CPA and estate attorney.

The choice is continued exchange, a planned taxable sale, or holding for a potential step-up. None should be treated as a generic tax conclusion.

## Planning a California exchange

Build the team early. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) holds sale proceeds to preserve exchange deferral. A CPA handles federal and California consequences, including clawback implications and Form 3840. A DST advisor through a broker-dealer sources and screens suitable offerings and helps coordinate the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines.

The investor should also establish objectives: current income, diversification, passivity, debt replacement, or estate planning. The clawback and annual reporting are not necessarily deal-breakers, but they need to be understood before closing—not discovered later. Some investors value a large current combined-tax deferral and out-of-state passive property enough to accept the preserved California claim and annual filing; others prefer California property. The proper answer depends on circumstances, accreditation, suitability, and professional advice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is a 1031 exchange especially valuable in California?

California’s top marginal rate is around 13.3% and it treats capital gain as ordinary income. That state tax sits on top of federal capital-gains tax, the 3.8% net investment income tax, and depreciation recapture. A 1031 exchange defers the federal and California gain, leaving more proceeds working in replacement property. Because California’s state tax is high, the deferral is relatively valuable compared with states such as Texas or Florida that have no income tax. Plan the details with a CPA.

### What is the California clawback?

It is California’s rule preserving tax on California-source gain after an out-of-state exchange. An investor can exchange California real estate into a DST in another state and defer federal and California gain. If the investor later sells the replacement in a taxable transaction without another 1031 exchange, California taxes the original deferred California-source gain even though the sold property is out of state. Further exchanges keep deferral going; a taxable sale triggers the clawback.

### Does the clawback mean I shouldn't exchange out of California?

Not necessarily. Out-of-state DSTs can offer higher current income, growth potential, and diversification relative to expensive California property. The broad like-kind rule generally permits a California rental to be exchanged into a DST anywhere in the United States. The clawback does not tax the exchange itself; it preserves California’s claim for a later taxable sale. Continued exchanges or holding until a potential step-up may be part of a long-term plan. Decide based on individual facts with a CPA.

### What is FTB Form 3840?

It is California’s Like-Kind Exchanges form. It reports a 1031 exchange from California real estate into out-of-state replacement property and the deferred California-source gain. It is filed for the exchange year and every subsequent year the out-of-state property is held until the gain is recognized or otherwise resolved. It is an information return, but omission can lead California to estimate and assess the deferred gain. A CPA typically files it with the California return.

### How long do I have to file Form 3840?

File it for the year of the exchange and every following year the out-of-state replacement property is held. The obligation continues until the deferred California-source gain is recognized, often through a taxable sale without a further 1031, or otherwise resolved. If another out-of-state 1031 exchange is completed, reporting generally continues. Rules can change and missed filings can prompt an assessment, so a CPA should handle the annual compliance.

### Can I exchange California property into a DST in another state?

Yes. U.S. investment real estate is broadly like-kind to other U.S. investment real estate, regardless of state. A California rental can be exchanged into a DST holding property in Texas, Florida, Arizona, the Carolinas, or elsewhere. The exchange can defer federal and California gain. The investor then must account for the clawback on original California-source gain and file Form 3840 annually. This is educational information, not tax advice.

### Does the clawback apply if I keep doing 1031 exchanges?

Not while the investor keeps deferring. When a DST sells at the end of its hold, a new 1031 exchange can continue deferral of federal and California gain. The clawback becomes relevant when deferred California-source gain is recognized in a taxable sale. Form 3840 reporting continues every year during the exchange chain. Some investors combine successive exchanges with a potential step-up-at-death plan, but current rules should be confirmed with a CPA.

### How does a step-up in basis interact with the clawback?

Through a “swap till you drop” strategy, an investor keeps deferring gain through successive 1031 exchanges and holds the interests until death. Heirs generally receive a basis step-up to fair market value. Under current law, that can eliminate deferred capital-gains tax and in many cases the California-source gain subject to clawback. The interaction of federal basis rules, California rules, and estate law is technical and changeable, requiring a CPA and estate attorney.

### Are DSTs a good fit for California investors?

They can be, but suitability is individual. California owners with heavily appreciated active rentals may use DSTs to defer federal and California gain, reduce property-management work, access passive out-of-state property, diversify, and use relatively low minimums. The trade-offs are the clawback and Form 3840 for out-of-state property plus illiquidity, fees, sponsor execution risk, and non-guaranteed distributions. DSTs are offered to accredited investors through a broker-dealer after suitability review.

### Will I owe California tax when the out-of-state DST sells?

It depends on the next step. A new 1031 exchange into replacement property continues deferral of federal and California gain, and the clawback is not triggered. Taking cash in a taxable sale recognizes the deferred gain and California taxes the original California-source gain through the clawback, even if the DST property was out of state. A DST sale is therefore a decision point. Plan timing and tax with a CPA before the end of the hold.

### Do I need a qualified intermediary for a California DST exchange?

Yes. The QI is an independent third party that holds proceeds from the California relinquished property and acquires the replacement DST interest so the investor does not receive the funds. Engage the QI before the relinquished-property sale closes. The QI also helps meet the 45-day identification and 180-day completion deadlines. A California DST advisor and CPA coordinate the exchange, Form 3840, and clawback analysis.

### Does the clawback apply to other states besides California?

California is the best-known example, but some other states also track or eventually tax in-state deferred gain after an out-of-state exchange, sometimes using their own reporting forms. Rates, mechanics, reporting, and enforcement vary. California’s is notable for the approximately 13.3% rate and annual Form 3840. Multi-state ownership can complicate the interaction, so confirm current state-specific treatment with a CPA.

### What should I plan before a California 1031 exchange?

Assemble a QI, CPA, attorney, and broker-dealer DST advisor. Understand the clawback and Form 3840 before exchange. Set objectives for income, diversification, passivity, debt replacement, and estate planning. Determine the long-term gain strategy: continued deferral, a taxable sale, or a potential step-up at death. Confirm accredited-investor status and identify replacement property within 45 days before closing within 180 days. Verify current rules with a CPA.

### Are DSTs illiquid, and what does that mean for a California investor?

Yes. A DST interest generally cannot be sold on demand and has limited or no secondary market. The investor remains in the trust until the sponsor sells, often after a five-to-seven-year hold. For a California owner with an out-of-state DST, that also means filing Form 3840 annually through the hold and planning further exchanges or a potential taxable exit. Funds need to be available for the multi-year commitment; distributions are projections, not guarantees.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate in which investors own fractional beneficial interests.

**1031 Exchange:** A swap of like-kind investment real estate that defers capital-gains tax.

**California Clawback:** California's rule preserving its tax claim on a California-source gain exchanged out of state.

**FTB Form 3840:** California's annual Like-Kind Exchanges form reporting deferred out-of-state gains.

**Franchise Tax Board (FTB):** California's state tax agency that administers the clawback and Form 3840.

**California-Source Gain:** Gain originating from California property, which California continues to claim.

**Top Marginal Rate:** California's highest income-tax rate, around 13.3%, applied to capital gains as ordinary income.

**Like-Kind Property:** U.S. investment real estate, broadly interchangeable across states.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of basis to fair market value at death, which can erase deferred gain.

**Swap Till You Drop:** Deferring via successive 1031s and holding until death for a step-up.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds exchange proceeds to preserve deferral.

**45-Day / 180-Day Rules:** The 1031 deadlines to identify and complete an exchange.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DST offerings.

**Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on previously claimed depreciation, also deferred by a 1031.

**Taxable Sale:** A sale without a further 1031 that triggers recognition and the clawback.

**Broker-Dealer:** The firm through which DST securities are offered after suitability review.
