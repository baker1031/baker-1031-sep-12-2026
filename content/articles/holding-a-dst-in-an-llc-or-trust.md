# Holding a DST in an LLC or Trust

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing a sound estate-planning or liability question arrive late in an exchange: should the DST be held in my name, an LLC, or a trust? Often an investor can hold the interest through an entity. The answer depends on preserving the taxpayer that sold the relinquished property.

First-order thinking sees a legal wrapper and assumes it changes nothing. Second-order thinking asks whether the wrapper is disregarded for federal income-tax purposes, whether the titling is consistent on both sides of the exchange, and whether a change is being attempted when it can no longer be fixed.

Can you hold a DST interest in an LLC or trust? Often yes, for liability protection or estate planning. A single-member LLC and a revocable living trust commonly preserve 1031 same-taxpayer status; an irrevocable trust is more complex. This guide explains the choices, their limits, and why you should coordinate with your attorney. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. ## Why Use an LLC or Trust

Investors typically use an entity for two reasons: liability protection and estate planning. A DST interest is fractional, passive real estate. The structure is designed to be bankruptcy-remote and non-recourse, yet some investors prefer a further layer of separation by placing the investment in a limited liability company apart from other assets and personal exposure. For investors who already hold other real estate or business interests through LLCs, using the same arrangement can keep the portfolio organized and consistent.

Estate planning is the other driver. A revocable living trust can let the DST interest [pass to heirs through the trust](dsts-for-trust-and-estate-beneficiaries.html) rather than probate, which may simplify administration, preserve privacy, and make the transfer more efficient. A DST is often held until its property is sold; many investors also intend to hold until death to seek a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html). [Estate planning](estate-planning-benefits-of-dsts-step-up-in-basis.html) starts with this question of titling because it affects how the interest reaches the next generation. An entity can make that transfer cleaner.

In short, an LLC can add liability separation, while a revocable trust can support an estate plan. Those are useful benefits layered onto passive DST income, not reasons to skip the tax and legal work.

## Single-Member LLCs and 1031

A single-member LLC is the most common entity used to hold a DST interest while preserving a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). Provided it has not elected corporate tax treatment, it is a “disregarded entity” for federal tax purposes: income, deductions, and ownership flow directly to its one member. The LLC is a separate legal entity, but its owner and the LLC are the same taxpayer for income-tax purposes.

That status is why the structure can work. The same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer who sold the relinquished property to acquire the replacement property. Because the tax code looks through a disregarded single-member LLC, the investor may hold the relinquished property or the replacement DST interest through that LLC without breaking the chain. It combines legal separation with the taxpayer continuity the exchange requires.

## Revocable & Irrevocable Trusts

Trusts split sharply here. A revocable living trust—the common estate-planning form—is generally a disregarded grantor trust. Because the grantor retains control and can revoke or amend it, the trust assets are treated as still owned by the grantor. Holding a DST interest in that trust generally preserves the exchange while allowing the interest to pass outside probate.

An irrevocable trust needs more care. Once assets enter it, the grantor usually gives up control and the trust may be a separate taxpayer with its own taxpayer identification number. That can break the same-taxpayer treatment. Some irrevocable trusts are deliberately structured as grantor trusts and remain disregarded, but many are not; the result depends on the trust’s terms. Getting this wrong can disqualify the exchange, so obtain professional guidance before titling a DST interest in an irrevocable trust.

## Maintaining Same-Taxpayer Status

The governing rule is simple to state: the taxpayer who sells the relinquished property must acquire the replacement DST interest. Disregarded entities—single-member LLCs and revocable grantor trusts—usually preserve that status because the tax code looks through them to the owner. Separate taxpayers generally do not.

Several ordinary decisions can cause trouble. Adding or changing LLC members at the wrong time can convert a disregarded LLC into a partnership, a separate taxpayer. Selling in one ownership form and acquiring through a non-disregarded entity can also break the chain. Changes to entity structure are generally better made well before or well after the exchange, rather than during it. These points are technical and fact-specific; review them with a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) and tax advisor before closing.

### Key Takeaways

- Investors often hold DST interests through an LLC for liability protection or a trust for estate planning.
- A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity, treated as the same taxpayer, so it can preserve 1031 status while adding liability protection.
- A revocable living trust is generally a disregarded grantor trust; an irrevocable trust may be a separate taxpayer and is more complex.
- Maintaining same-taxpayer status requires consistent use of disregarded entities and coordination with your attorney before the exchange.

## Coordinating With Your Attorney

Titling a DST interest sits at the intersection of tax law, estate law, and exchange mechanics. Make the decision with your attorney and CPA, not at the last minute. An estate-planning attorney can confirm whether an existing revocable trust is a disregarded grantor trust, whether an LLC suits a liability concern, and how the interest fits the larger estate plan. Correct titling at the outset avoids costly corrections later.

Timing and communication with the qualified intermediary matter just as much. The entity that will hold the replacement interest should generally be in place and properly titled before closing, with the same-taxpayer chain confirmed in advance. Your attorney, CPA, and qualified intermediary see different parts of the arrangement. Coordinating them helps the entity, exchange, and estate plan line up. A disqualified exchange or unintended tax bill is a high enough cost to justify professional guidance.

## Common Scenarios and How They Play Out

An investor who already holds rental property in a single-member LLC and wants to exchange into a DST can typically have the LLC complete the exchange and acquire the DST interest: the member remains the taxpayer on both sides. A married couple that sells jointly owned property may acquire the DST jointly or through a jointly owned disregarded entity, preserving the same taxpayers. Community-property and joint-ownership rules vary by state, so confirm the details with an advisor.

Other cases are harder. Adding a child as a DST co-owner during an exchange changes the taxpayer and can jeopardize the exchange; a transfer or gift may instead be structured separately afterward. In a multi-member LLC or partnership, the partnership—not its partners—is the taxpayer. Partners seeking separate DST outcomes must address that before the sale, often with a “drop and swap” or similar planning that has its own timing and risk. Advance planning matters in every one of these cases.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I hold a DST interest in an LLC?

Yes. Investors commonly use a single-member LLC for liability protection. When it has not elected corporate treatment, the IRS treats its income and ownership as belonging directly to its single member. That can preserve the same-taxpayer rule while providing legal separation. Formation, titling, and ongoing maintenance matter; adding members at the wrong time can make it a partnership. Confirm the structure with your attorney and CPA before closing.

### Does holding a DST in an LLC affect my 1031 exchange?

It depends on the LLC. A single-member LLC is generally disregarded for federal tax purposes, so holding relinquished property or replacement DST interests through it generally does not affect the exchange. A multi-member LLC is generally a partnership and a separate taxpayer, which can complicate or break continuity if structured incorrectly. Keep membership and tax classification consistent through the exchange and confirm the arrangement with your tax advisor and qualified intermediary.

### What is a disregarded entity?

It is a business entity the IRS ignores for federal income-tax purposes, treating income, deductions, and assets as belonging directly to its owner. A single-member LLC without a corporate election is the common example; a revocable living grantor trust is similar. This legal separation can preserve the same taxpayer on both sides of an exchange, whereas partnerships and many irrevocable trusts generally cannot. Confirm tax classification with your CPA.

### Can I hold a DST in a revocable living trust?

Yes, generally. A revocable living trust is usually a disregarded grantor trust because the grantor retains control and can revoke or amend it. It can preserve the same-taxpayer rule and pass the DST interest to heirs outside probate with privacy and efficiency. Because some investors plan to hold until death for a step-up in basis, that titling can simplify the transfer. Confirm the trust’s status with an estate-planning attorney.

### Can I hold a DST in an irrevocable trust?

Possibly, but it requires careful structuring. Assets in an irrevocable trust often become held by a separate taxpayer, which can break same-taxpayer treatment. Some such trusts remain grantor trusts and may preserve it; the answer rests on their terms. A mistake can disqualify the exchange and trigger the capital-gains tax an investor sought to defer. Work with an estate-planning attorney and CPA before the exchange. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

### What is the same-taxpayer rule in a 1031 exchange?

It requires the taxpayer who sells the relinquished property to acquire the replacement property. The IRS considers the tax-reporting entity, not merely legal title. Single-member LLCs and revocable grantor trusts are useful because they are generally treated as the owner; partnerships, multi-member LLCs, and many irrevocable trusts can violate the rule if used incorrectly. Pitfalls include adding members mid-exchange, mismatched titling, and restructuring during the exchange. Confirm the structure with your qualified intermediary and tax advisor.

### Does an LLC give me liability protection for a DST?

A single-member LLC can provide a measure of liability separation and keep an existing portfolio organized. The DST itself is designed to be bankruptcy-remote and its debt is non-recourse to investors, so personal exposure from the underlying property is already limited. An LLC adds another layer but is not a substitute for insurance or comprehensive asset-protection planning. The degree of protection depends on its formation, maintenance, and the investor’s broader plan; discuss it with an attorney.

### Can a married couple hold a DST together in a 1031?

Yes, provided the structure preserves the taxpayers who sold. Joint owners can generally acquire jointly or through a jointly owned disregarded entity. State community-property, joint-tenancy, and tenancy-by-the-entirety rules can affect both the exchange and step-up in basis at death. Do not add or remove a spouse or change ownership form during the exchange without advice; confirm the facts with an attorney and qualified intermediary before closing.

### What happens to a DST in an LLC when I die?

How it passes depends on titling. If held until death, heirs generally receive a step-up in basis to fair market value, which can eliminate deferred capital-gains tax—the “swap till you drop” benefit. An LLC interest passes under the estate plan and the step-up generally still applies to the underlying asset. A revocable trust can pass the interest outside probate while preserving that benefit. State law and the relationship among entity structure, estate tax, and basis are technical; consult an estate-planning attorney and CPA.

### Can I change how a DST is titled after the exchange?

Changes may be possible after an exchange has fully closed, but timing, structure, and tax consequences matter. Changes during the exchange can break the same-taxpayer chain. A post-close transfer to a trust may be possible; a gift of part of the interest can be taxable or change basis. It is better to get titling right before closing, because some corrections are difficult or impossible. Coordinate a post-exchange change with an attorney and CPA.

### Why not just hold the DST in my own name?

Holding it directly is valid and often simplest. An entity serves particular goals: a single-member LLC can add legal separation, and a revocable trust can pass the interest outside probate with privacy and efficiency. If neither liability protection nor estate planning is a priority, direct ownership may avoid entity cost and administration. Ask an attorney whether the added complexity advances your objectives.

### Does using an entity change the DST's income or distributions?

No, a disregarded entity does not change the DST’s underlying income, distributions, or how they are ultimately taxed to the owner. Distributions flow through a single-member LLC or revocable grantor trust to the owner, who reports them as if holding directly. The entity is a legal wrapper, not a separate tax layer. It can affect legal ownership, liability separation, and passage to heirs, but not property economics. Distributions still depend on performance and are not guaranteed; confirm reporting with your CPA.

### Can a partnership do a 1031 into a DST?

Yes. The partnership can exchange because it is the taxpayer that owns the relinquished property and can acquire the replacement. Difficulty arises when partners want separate outcomes. They cannot simply take their shares and make separate exchanges because the partnership, not each partner, is the taxpayer. A “drop and swap” may distribute property interests before the sale, but it has its own risks and must be planned well in advance. Work with tax and legal advisors before a sale.

### Do I need an attorney to hold a DST in an entity?

It is strongly advisable. An attorney can assess trust classification, LLC suitability, estate-plan fit, and proper titling before closing. Coordinating the attorney, CPA, and qualified intermediary helps avoid costly corrections and a disqualified exchange. ## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)** — A trust holding income-producing real estate as 1031-eligible fractional interests.  
**Single-Member LLC** — A one-owner LLC that is disregarded for federal income tax.  
**Disregarded Entity** — An entity the IRS ignores for tax, treating it as its owner.  
**Revocable Living Trust** — A grantor trust, disregarded for tax, used for estate planning.  
**Irrevocable Trust** — A trust the grantor can't revoke, often a separate taxpayer.  
**Grantor Trust** — A trust whose assets are still treated as the grantor's for tax.  
**Same-Taxpayer Rule** — The 1031 requirement that the same taxpayer sells and buys.  
**Liability Protection** — Legal separation of an investment from other personal assets.  
**Estate Planning** — Arranging how assets pass to heirs, often via a trust.  
**Step-Up in Basis** — Heirs' basis reset to fair market value at the owner's death.  
**Probate** — The court process for transferring assets at death.  
**Qualified Intermediary** — The party that facilitates a 1031 exchange and holds proceeds.  
**Relinquished Property** — The property sold at the start of a 1031 exchange.  
**Replacement Property** — The like-kind property acquired in a 1031 exchange.  
**Drop and Swap** — Distributing partnership property to partners before an exchange.  
**Beneficial Interest** — An investor's fractional ownership stake in a DST.
