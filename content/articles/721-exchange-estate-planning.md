# Estate Planning With a 721 Exchange: The Step-Up Advantage


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about the gap between the asset a family owns and the asset a family can actually divide. A building can be valuable and still be a difficult inheritance. It can require a manager, create disagreement, or force a sale at an inconvenient time.

A 721 exchange is commonly described as a way to trade property for a diversified REIT position while deferring tax. Its estate-planning appeal is different: OP units held until death may receive a stepped-up basis for heirs, potentially eliminating deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture, and the units can be divided among beneficiaries much more readily than a single property.

The first-order view is tax deferral and current distributions. The second-order view is the form of the estate: divisibility, liquidity, control, estate-tax exposure, partnership-agreement terms, and the need to hold units rather than convert them during life. This can be useful planning, but it is not a universal outcome. Tax and estate rules are intricate and subject to change, and qualified counsel should design the plan.

## Key Takeaways
- Holding OP units until death can allow heirs to receive a stepped-up basis, potentially eliminating deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture.
- OP units can divide among multiple heirs more easily than an indivisible building.
- Inherited units may be converted to liquid REIT shares with little income-tax cost, which can provide flexibility in settling an estate.
- The income-tax step-up is separate from estate tax, and qualified estate and tax counsel should evaluate the details.

## The Core Idea: Deferral That Can Become Elimination

A 721 exchange defers tax in a way that resembles a 1031 exchange. The estate-planning insight concerns the deferral at death. Under current law, assets generally receive a basis step-up to fair market value at the owner's death. An owner who has deferred gain into OP units and holds them until death may pass units whose embedded capital gain and depreciation recapture are eliminated for income-tax purposes.

That can turn lifetime deferral into a different outcome for heirs. It follows the familiar “swap till you drop” logic of repeated [1031 exchanges](1031-exchange-guide.html), but the final holding is a diversified REIT operating-partnership interest rather than a directly owned building. An investor whose objective is an efficient wealth transfer rather than personal liquidity may see the appeal. The owner must actually hold the units, and the legal rules must support the result.

## How the Step-Up in Basis Works

During life, OP units carry the low, deferred basis from the property contributed in the 721 exchange. Converting or selling the units can recognize the deferred gain. At death, basis is generally reset to the units' fair market value. If heirs then convert units to REIT shares or sell them, they can have little or no income-tax gain because their basis is current value, not the owner's old basis.

The step-up can therefore erase the income-tax liability embedded in the units at the generational handoff. It does not make the unit value disappear from the estate, and it does not substitute for estate-tax planning.

## “Swap Till You Drop,” Applied to the UPREIT

“Swap till you drop” describes successive 1031 exchanges followed by holding until death, when a basis step-up can eliminate deferred gain. A 721 contribution can be the final leg: an owner can exchange from property to property, or through DSTs, and later contribute to a REIT operating partnership for OP units. The owner can then hold units for life while receiving distributions, gaining diversification and easier transfer.

The appeal of ending with OP units rather than a building is what comes after the contribution: units are divisible, can provide estate liquidity, and can deliver passive income in the meantime. The comparison belongs in a broader [estate-planning guide](estate-planning-real-estate-guide.html), alongside the family's full tax, liquidity, and governance needs.

## Divisibility: OP Units Versus a Single Building

Leaving one apartment building to three children presents an operational problem. Someone has to manage it, heirs can disagree about whether to hold or sell, and there is no clean way to split one building without a sale. The result can be family friction or a forced sale.

OP units are fungible and divisible like shares. An owner can leave a precise number of units to each heir. Each beneficiary can independently hold for income, convert to shares, or redeem for cash without being tied to siblings or a building they do not want to manage. For a family with multiple beneficiaries, this can be a deciding advantage over direct property ownership.

## Liquidity for the Estate

An estate may need money for taxes, debts, equalizing bequests, a charitable gift, or ordinary settlement costs. Illiquid real estate can force a hurried sale. If inherited OP units have a stepped-up basis, heirs may convert them to REIT shares or redeem them for cash with little or no income-tax cost. That flexibility can make the asset more usable for the next generation.

It remains important to check the partnership agreement, conversion terms, redemption terms, and whether the particular REIT structure provides the expected path. Not every OP unit or REIT arrangement is the same.

## Income for the Owner's Lifetime

The estate objective need not require giving up income. While an owner holds OP units, they can receive distributions tied to the REIT's dividend from a diversified portfolio without direct property management. The strategy can therefore combine passive cash flow during life with a potentially more efficient transfer at death.

Distributions are not guaranteed, and neither the income objective nor the estate objective removes the risks of REIT ownership, illiquidity, or loss of principal. A retiree who wants income now and a later transfer needs to weigh these risks against the benefit of a more divisible asset.

## Cautions and What to Confirm With Counsel

The income-tax step-up is separate from estate tax. Units remain in the owner's taxable estate, so a large estate may still owe estate tax even if the income-tax gain is eliminated. These are two different taxes and require separate planning.

The units must be held until death for the relevant portion to receive the step-up. Conversion or sale during life triggers tax on the converted portion and gives up the step-up for it. Basis step-up and estate-tax rules are intricate and have changed before, so a plan based on current law needs regular review.

Finally, partnership-agreement protections and conversion terms matter. This is a strategy for a qualified estate attorney and tax adviser, not an assumption drawn from a general article.

## A Worked Example

Consider a hypothetical investor late in life who completes a 721 exchange and holds OP units with steady distributions. She has a large deferred gain from decades of 1031 exchanges. Instead of converting and triggering gain, she lives on the distributions and holds the units.

At death, the units pass to her two children with a basis stepped up to current value. They split the units evenly. One keeps them for income; the other converts to REIT shares and sells to fund a home purchase. The example assumes each owes little income tax because of the step-up. The strategy gives the mother income and the children a divisible asset, while potentially eliminating the gain deferred for thirty years. It is illustrative and hypothetical only; actual results belong with qualified counsel.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does a 721 exchange help with estate planning?

If OP units are held until death, heirs may receive a stepped-up basis that eliminates deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture for income-tax purposes. The units also divide easily among multiple heirs, unlike a single physical property.

### Does the step-up eliminate estate tax too?

No. The step-up addresses income tax on deferred gain. The units remain in the owner's taxable estate, so a large estate can still owe estate tax. Estate tax is a separate planning question.

### Why are OP units good for leaving to multiple heirs?

They are divisible like stock. A precise number can go to each heir, who can independently hold, convert, or redeem. That can avoid forced sales, management burdens, and disputes often associated with an indivisible building.

### Do I have to hold OP units until death for the benefit?

For the basis step-up that can eliminate deferred gain, yes. Converting or redeeming during life triggers tax on the converted portion and forfeits the step-up for that portion.

### Can my heirs get cash from inherited OP units?

Yes, if the arrangement permits. Heirs with stepped-up basis may convert to REIT shares or redeem units for cash with little or no income-tax cost, potentially providing liquidity to settle the estate. They should check the partnership agreement and conversion or redemption terms.

## Glossary

- **Step-Up in Basis:** A reset of basis to fair market value at death that can eliminate deferred income-tax gain for heirs.
- **OP Units:** Operating-partnership units received in a 721 exchange and economically equivalent to REIT shares.
- **Estate Tax:** Tax on transfer of a taxable estate at death, separate from income tax on deferred gain.
- **Swap Till You Drop:** Deferring gain through exchanges and holding until death so a step-up can eliminate it.
