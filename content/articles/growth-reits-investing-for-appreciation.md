# Growth REITs: Investing for Appreciation

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how often a high yield gets mistaken for a complete investment thesis. A dividend is real cash today. But it is only one part of return, and sometimes the decision to retain more capital inside a real-estate portfolio is the more important fact.

Most investors picture REITs as income vehicles. Many are. Growth REITs take a different route: rather than maximizing the current dividend, they reinvest a larger share of cash flow into development, acquisitions, and improvements. The aim is appreciation and total return over a longer horizon.

That approach can fit an investor who does not need maximum current income and wants a real-estate allocation to grow in value as well as pay income. Growth REITs often focus on property sectors with structural demand potential, including data centers, industrial and logistics facilities, cell towers, and selected residential markets. Their central measure is total return: income plus appreciation, rather than yield alone.

This is educational information, not investment or tax advice. REIT investments carry risk. Yields and returns are not guaranteed, past performance does not guarantee future results, and investors should verify current rules and suitability with their own professionals.

First-order thinking says, “A low-yield REIT is paying less, so it is less attractive.” Second-order thinking asks what the REIT is doing with the cash it retains, what return is already priced into the shares, whether development and acquisitions can earn their cost of capital, and whether the investor can wait through volatility. Yield is a component. It is not the entire answer.

## What Is a Growth REIT?

A growth REIT is a [real estate investment trust](reits-guide.html) oriented toward appreciation and total return rather than maximizing current dividend income. Every REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income to maintain its tax-advantaged status. A growth REIT can nonetheless retain and reinvest more cash flow because its taxable income may be lower relative to its cash flow, in part because of depreciation and its spending on development and expansion.

The usual result is a more modest dividend yield and more potential for share-price appreciation as the portfolio grows. The REIT may use cash flow to develop new properties, acquire assets, and improve existing ones. Those choices can expand the portfolio and net asset value (NAV), and a dividend may grow over time as income rises.

The contrast is an income REIT, which emphasizes high, stable current yield from stabilized, income-producing property. A growth REIT favors building longer-term value over paying the maximum today. It can be appropriate for investors seeking appreciation and total return over a longer horizon who do not need maximum current income from this allocation.

## Appreciation vs. Current Income

The core trade-off is appreciation versus current income. An income REIT commonly distributes most of its cash flow as a high dividend. That provides current income and can lead to a more stable, slower-growing share price, but leaves less cash to reinvest.

A growth REIT does more of the opposite. It reinvests more into expansion, pays a lower current dividend, and seeks to build portfolio value. More of the potential return comes later through appreciation.

Total return is the right lens for both approaches. It is the sum of income from dividends and appreciation in the share price. Two REITs could deliver a similar total return while giving their investors different experiences: an income REIT may provide more today as yield, while a growth REIT may provide more later as appreciation. Which is better depends on the investor’s need for current cash flow versus long-term growth.

An income REIT pays out more of what it earns today. A growth REIT reinvests more, on the view that a larger and more valuable portfolio tomorrow can be worth more than the current dividend foregone. That is a choice about timing and risk, not a promise that the later outcome will arrive.

## Sectors With Growth Potential

Growth REITs commonly concentrate in property sectors with long-term demand potential. Data centers are a leading example, with demand associated with cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and expanding digital infrastructure. Industrial and logistics properties—warehouses, distribution centers, and fulfillment facilities—have grown alongside e-commerce and supply-chain reconfiguration.

Cell towers and communications infrastructure can benefit from rising mobile-data demand and network build-outs. Some residential REITs in high-growth markets, including Sun Belt apartments and single-family rentals in expanding regions, pursue growth as population and rents rise.

These sectors share a possible source of appreciation: secular demand can support rent growth, occupancy, and development opportunity. Yet a sector label should not become a shortcut. Demand can slow or reverse, supply can catch up, and technological change can alter the economics of a property type. Sector exposure creates sector-specific risk as well as a possible tailwind.

## Reinvestment & Total Return

Reinvestment is the engine of a growth REIT. Rather than paying all cash flow out, the REIT can fund new development, acquire additional assets, and make capital improvements intended to raise rents and values. Over time, successful reinvestment can compound the portfolio and NAV.

Total return is the measure of whether that effort worked. A modest-yield REIT may generate strong total returns through appreciation and can outperform a higher-yield REIT on that measure if its growth materializes. The condition is important: development, acquisitions, and portfolio expansion must actually add value.

That is why yield alone can be misleading. A long-horizon investor should assess the dividend and the prospects for appreciation together. Reinvested cash flow can compound over years, but it can also be spent poorly.

Development can disappoint. Projects can run over budget. Acquisitions can be mispriced. Capital markets can tighten when more funding is needed. Sponsor and management quality, balance-sheet discipline, and a credible development or acquisition pipeline therefore deserve as much attention as the current yield.

## Key Takeaways

- A growth REIT reinvests cash flow for appreciation and total return rather than maximizing the current dividend yield.
- It trades high current yield for greater appreciation potential and can fit a longer-horizon investor who does not need maximum current income.
- Growth REITs often concentrate in data centers, industrial/logistics, cell towers, and selected high-growth residential markets.
- Higher growth can mean higher volatility and risk. Judge a growth REIT by total return and its fit with the investor’s goals.

## Growth REIT Risk Profile

Higher growth often comes with higher volatility. Growth REITs and the sectors they target can be more sensitive to economic cycles, interest rates, and investor sentiment than stable income REITs. Their share prices can swing more sharply.

The appreciation being sought is not guaranteed. If growth does not materialize, a modest dividend can provide less income cushion than a high-yield income REIT offers.

Concentration creates another risk. A growth REIT focused on data centers, industrial property, or another single sector is exposed to that sector’s demand, supply, and technology risks. A demand slowdown, oversupply, or technological shift can impair the plan.

Execution risk sits alongside concentration. Development projects can run over budget or disappoint. Acquisitions can be overpriced. The pursuit of appreciation therefore carries volatility, sector exposure, and execution risk. It is more naturally suited to an investor with a long horizon and the ability to tolerate price swings than to an investor who needs stable current income.

## Who Fits a Growth REIT?

The clearest fit is a long-horizon investor who does not need current income from this allocation: an accumulator still building wealth who wants real-estate exposure to grow over years or decades. For someone new to the asset class, [how to start investing in REITs](reit-investing-for-beginners-how-to-get-started.html) explains the fundamentals.

Growth REITs can also fit investors who can remain invested through volatility and who want real-estate exposure to secular-growth themes such as digital infrastructure, logistics, and communications. The lower current yield is acceptable only if the investor does not need it for spending or near-term obligations.

Retirees and income-focused investors who need steady cash flow are generally a poorer fit for a growth-first allocation. Income REITs may be more appropriate for that role. Many portfolios blend income and growth REITs, using one for current cash flow and the other for possible appreciation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a growth REIT?

A growth REIT is a real estate investment trust that seeks appreciation and total return rather than maximizing current dividend income. Every REIT must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, but a growth REIT reinvests heavily in development, acquisitions, and improvements. That can leave it with a modest dividend yield and more potential for share-price appreciation as the portfolio grows.

The investor’s return comes less from a high current dividend and more from share appreciation and, potentially, a growing dividend over time. This differs from an income REIT, which emphasizes a high, stable current yield from stabilized property. Growth REITs can fit long-horizon investors who do not need maximum current income.

### How is a growth REIT different from an income REIT?

The difference is the balance between appreciation and current income. An income REIT pays out more cash flow as a high dividend, delivers more current income, reinvests less, and may have a more stable share price. A growth REIT reinvests more cash flow into expansion, pays a lower current dividend, and seeks more appreciation.

Total return—income plus appreciation—is the relevant measure for both. For the same total return, an income REIT may deliver more now as yield and a growth REIT more later as appreciation. Many portfolios use both, depending on the investor’s income need and growth objective.

### What sectors do growth REITs invest in?

Growth REITs tend to focus on sectors with structural, long-term demand potential. Data centers can benefit from cloud computing, artificial intelligence, and digital infrastructure. Industrial and logistics properties can benefit from e-commerce and supply-chain reconfiguration. Cell towers and communications infrastructure can benefit from mobile-data demand.

Some residential REITs in high-growth markets, including Sun Belt apartments and expanding-region single-family rentals, also pursue growth as population and rents rise. These themes can support rent growth, occupancy, and development, but sector concentration means demand can slow or reverse.

### What is total return, and why does it matter for growth REITs?

Total return is income from dividends plus share-price appreciation. It gives the complete picture of investment return. A growth REIT with a modest yield could deliver strong total returns through appreciation and could outperform a higher-yield REIT on a total-return basis if the growth materializes.

That is why yield alone is not enough for comparing income-oriented and growth-oriented REITs. A high-yield income REIT and a low-yield growth REIT can have different return mixes and potentially similar total returns. The appreciation component is uncertain, so it must be evaluated rather than assumed.

### Why do growth REITs have lower dividend yields?

Growth REITs direct more cash flow to development, acquisitions, and improvements instead of paying it out. Although every REIT must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, depreciation and reinvestment can leave a growth REIT with lower taxable income relative to cash flow and a more modest current dividend.

The intended trade-off is that the reinvestment builds the portfolio and NAV, supports share-price appreciation, and may allow the dividend to grow later. A low yield can signal a growth orientation, not poor performance, but it should still be judged on total return and the quality of the investments being made.

### Are growth REITs riskier than income REITs?

Generally, higher growth comes with higher volatility and risk. Growth REITs can be more sensitive to economic cycles, interest rates, and sentiment. Their share prices can move more than stable income REITs, and their modest yields provide less cushion if appreciation disappoints.

They also face sector-concentration and execution risk. A single-sector REIT is exposed to that sector’s demand, supply, and technology conditions. Development can run over budget or disappoint, and acquisitions can be overpriced. These risks make growth REITs more appropriate for a long-horizon investor who can tolerate swings than for someone who requires stable current income.

### Who should consider a growth REIT?

Long-horizon, growth-oriented, volatility-tolerant investors who do not need current income from the allocation are the clearest candidates. That includes an accumulator building wealth and seeking real-estate exposure that may grow in value over years or decades.

Growth REITs can also suit investors seeking exposure to digital infrastructure, logistics, or communications themes. Retirees and income-focused investors needing steady cash flow generally fit income REITs better. A portfolio can combine both orientations for current income and possible appreciation.

### Can a growth REIT also pay dividends?

Yes. Every REIT must distribute at least 90% of its taxable income, so a growth REIT pays dividends. The dividend is usually lower than that of an income REIT because the growth REIT reinvests more cash flow and depreciation reduces taxable income.

The dividend can grow as the portfolio expands and income rises. A growth REIT therefore does not pay nothing; it offers some current income with the prospect of a potentially growing dividend and appreciation over a longer horizon.

### Are data center and industrial REITs growth REITs?

Often they are. Data centers can benefit from cloud, AI, and digital-infrastructure demand. Industrial and logistics REITs can benefit from e-commerce and supply-chain demand. REITs in these sectors often develop and acquire property to pursue appreciation.

The label is not rigid. Not every data-center or industrial REIT is purely growth-oriented; some are more income-focused. Review the individual REIT’s yield, reinvestment approach, development pipeline, and total-return orientation instead of assuming the sector determines the strategy.

### Should I choose a growth REIT or an income REIT?

It depends on goals, time horizon, and need for current income. A growth REIT may fit an investor with a long horizon, no immediate income need, an appreciation objective, and tolerance for volatility. That investor accepts lower current yield for possible future appreciation.

An income REIT may fit an investor seeking steady, higher current income for retirement or cash flow and preferring more stability. There is no universal answer. Many investors hold both, using income REITs for current cash flow and growth REITs for appreciation. A financial advisor can help assess the appropriate mix for an investor’s circumstances.

### Are growth REITs traded or non-traded?

Growth REITs can be either traded or non-traded. Many growth-oriented REITs are publicly traded, listed on exchanges with daily liquidity, transparent pricing, and lower fees. Many data-center, industrial, and cell-tower REITs are traded.

Non-traded growth REITs also exist. They are not listed, may use periodic NAV pricing, have limited liquidity, and have historically carried higher fees. Growth versus income is a strategy decision; traded versus non-traded is a structure decision. Both affect suitability. Non-traded access often involves accredited or otherwise suitable investors and requires careful consideration of illiquidity and fees.

### How long should I hold a growth REIT?

Growth REITs are generally intended for a long horizon: years or decades, rather than short-term income. Reinvestment needs time to compound the portfolio and support appreciation. A longer hold also gives an investor more ability to ride out growth-sector volatility.

There is no fixed minimum, but a multi-year horizon—often 5-10+ years—aligns with the strategy. Short-term holders are more exposed to price swings and may not capture appreciation. If capital is needed soon or volatility cannot be tolerated, a growth REIT is generally a poorer fit.

### Do growth REITs qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No. REIT shares, whether growth or income and whether traded or non-traded, are securities rather than like-kind real property. They do not qualify for a direct 1031 exchange. Since 2017, 1031 treatment is limited to real property, and REIT shares are excluded.

There is an indirect route sometimes discussed: an investor may complete a 1031 exchange into a DST and later use a 721/UPREIT exchange to convert DST property into REIT operating-partnership units on a tax-deferred basis. That is different from exchanging directly into REIT shares. Buying growth REIT shares is a taxable purchase, not a direct 1031 exchange.

### Are growth REIT returns guaranteed?

No. Growth REIT returns and appreciation are not guaranteed, and past performance does not guarantee future results. The strategy depends on successful reinvestment in development, acquisitions, and growth sectors. Development can disappoint, acquisitions can be mispriced, sector demand can slow, and economic or interest-rate conditions can pressure share prices.

Because a growth REIT typically pays a modest current dividend, there can be less income cushion when appreciation falls short. Loss and underperformance are possible. The appreciation goal is a potential outcome, not a promise, which is why suitability, a long horizon, and tolerance for volatility matter.

## Glossary

**Growth REIT:** A REIT oriented toward appreciation and total return over current yield.

**Income REIT:** A REIT emphasizing high, stable current dividend income.

**Total Return:** Income from dividends plus share-price appreciation.

**Appreciation:** Growth in a REIT’s share price or value over time.

**Reinvestment:** Plowing cash flow into development, acquisitions, and improvements.

**Net Asset Value (NAV):** The estimated value of a REIT’s assets less liabilities.

**Dividend Yield:** The annual dividend relative to the share price.

**90% Distribution Rule:** REITs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income.

**Data Center REIT:** A growth sector associated with cloud, AI, and digital infrastructure.

**Industrial/Logistics REIT:** A growth sector associated with e-commerce and supply chains.

**Cell Tower REIT:** A growth sector associated with mobile-data demand.

**Secular Demand:** Long-term structural growth in sector demand.

**Sector Concentration:** Exposure to one property sector’s fortunes, which is a risk.

**Execution Risk:** Risk that development or acquisitions disappoint.

**Volatility:** Price fluctuation, often higher for growth REITs.

**Long Horizon:** The multi-year timeframe that suits a growth REIT.
