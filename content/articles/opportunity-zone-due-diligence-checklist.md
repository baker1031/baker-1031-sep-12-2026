# Opportunity Zone Due Diligence Checklist

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Opportunity Zone Funds · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how often a tax benefit turns an ordinary underwriting question into an urgent decision. It should do the opposite. First-order thinking asks whether a Qualified Opportunity Fund can defer a gain. Second-order thinking asks whether the sponsor, project, compliance record, fees, and exit can justify tying up capital for a decade.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">Opportunity Zone investing commits your capital for a long time — ideally ten years or more — into an illiquid, often development-stage investment, so the quality of your due diligence matters enormously. The tax benefits are only valuable if the underlying investment performs, which means evaluating a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) the way you would any serious private real-estate investment: scrutinizing the sponsor, the project and market, the OZ compliance, the fees and promote, and the exit strategy. A disciplined, checklist-driven process helps you separate sound funds from weak ones and avoid letting the tax incentive drive you into a poor investment. This guide is a practical due diligence checklist for evaluating an OZ fund, organized into five areas: vet the sponsor, analyze the project and market, confirm OZ compliance, review fees and promote, and assess the exit strategy. Use it as a framework for your own diligence and your conversations with the fund and your advisors. This is educational information, not investment advice — and OZ rules are time-sensitive and evolving, so verify the current rules with your tax advisor.</p>
<h2 id="vet-the-sponsor">Vet the sponsor</h2>
<p>Vetting the sponsor is the foundation of OZ due diligence, because the sponsor's ability to execute largely determines the investment's success — especially for development funds. Examine the sponsor's experience (years in the business, depth of the team), their track record (prior projects, realized returns, and how they navigated downturns), and their reputation (references, litigation history, and standing in the industry). A capable, experienced, reputable sponsor reduces execution and sponsor risk.</p>
<p>Dig into the specifics: Has the sponsor completed similar projects (same property type, similar scale, similar markets)? What were the outcomes — did they deliver on time and on budget, and did investors earn the projected returns? How are they capitalized and staffed to execute this project? Have they managed OZ funds specifically, with the compliance discipline the program requires? A sponsor with a proven, relevant track record and clean reputation is far preferable to an inexperienced or troubled one.</p>
<p>So vetting the sponsor — their experience, track record, and reputation — is the first and most important diligence step. Vet the sponsor — examining their experience (team depth, years), track record (prior projects, realized returns, performance through downturns), and reputation (references, litigation, standing), with particular attention to relevant, similar prior projects and OZ-specific experience — is the foundation of OZ due diligence, because the sponsor's execution drives the outcome. A proven sponsor reduces risk. Understanding sponsor diligence shows where to start. Vet the sponsor's experience, track record, and reputation first — their ability to execute (especially on development) largely determines the investment's success, so a proven, relevant, reputable sponsor is essential.</p>
<h2 id="project-market">Analyze the project &amp; market</h2>
<p>Analyzing the project and market tests whether the underlying investment is sound on its own merits. For the project, examine the business plan (what's being built or improved, the budget, the timeline, the assumptions), the property type and strategy (ground-up development, renovation, or operating asset), and the financial projections (the underwriting, the return assumptions, and how realistic they are). A sound project has a credible plan, reasonable assumptions, and a margin for error.</p>
<p>For the market, examine the location fundamentals — the demand drivers (population and job growth, demographics), the supply picture (competing projects, absorption), and the broader economic trends in the area. An OZ designation alone doesn't make a location good; you want genuine demand and growth prospects independent of the tax incentive. Be wary of projects that rely on the OZ designation rather than real market strength. So scrutinize whether the project and its market would be attractive even without the tax benefits.</p>
<p>So analyzing the project and market — the business plan, the projections, and the location fundamentals — tests the investment's standalone merit. Analyze the project and market — examining the business plan, property type, and financial projections (are the assumptions realistic, is there margin for error?), and the location fundamentals (demand drivers, supply, economic trends) — to confirm the investment is sound on its own merits, not just because of the OZ designation. Real market strength matters. Understanding project/market diligence shows how to test the investment. Analyze the project (business plan, projections, realism) and the market (location fundamentals, demand, supply) to confirm the investment would be attractive on its merits — not just because of the OZ tax incentive.</p>

<h2 id="confirm-compliance">Confirm OZ compliance</h2>
<p>Confirming OZ compliance ensures the fund actually qualifies for (and will maintain) the program's benefits — without compliance, the tax advantages can be lost. Key items to confirm include: the fund holds qualifying <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">opportunity zone</a> property (qualifying business property in a designated zone), it meets the substantial improvement requirement where applicable (for existing buildings, generally doubling the basis of improvements within the required period), and it satisfies the 90% asset test (a QOF must hold at least 90% of its assets in qualified OZ property, measured periodically).</p>
<p>Also confirm the fund avoids prohibited 'sin businesses' (certain excluded businesses like golf courses, country clubs, massage parlors, gambling facilities, and liquor stores, which don't qualify), and that it has a sound working-capital plan (the working-capital safe harbor lets a fund hold cash for a development plan if structured properly, with a written plan and schedule). These compliance elements are technical, so confirm the fund and its counsel have addressed them — a fund that mishandles compliance can jeopardize the benefits.</p>
<p>So confirming OZ compliance — qualifying property, substantial improvement, the 90% test, no sin businesses, and a working-capital plan — protects the tax benefits. Confirm OZ compliance — verifying the fund holds qualifying OZ property, meets the substantial-improvement requirement, satisfies the 90% asset test, avoids prohibited 'sin businesses,' and has a sound working-capital plan (the safe harbor) — to ensure the benefits are available and maintained. Compliance failures can cost the benefits. Understanding compliance diligence shows what to verify. Confirm the fund's OZ compliance — qualifying property, substantial improvement, the 90% test, no sin businesses, and a working-capital plan — since these technical requirements protect the tax benefits; verify the current rules with your tax advisor.</p>
<p>Because these compliance points are technical and the rules are evolving (and were changed by the 2025 OZ 2.0 legislation), have your CPA or attorney review the fund's compliance approach — this is educational, not tax advice, and the current rules should be verified.</p>
<h2 id="fees-promote">Review fees &amp; promote</h2>
<p>Reviewing fees and the promote reveals how much of the investment's return goes to the sponsor versus you. Examine <a href="opportunity-zone-fund-fees-explained.html">the fee structure — acquisition fees, asset-management fees, development fees, disposition fees, and any other charges</a> — and understand how they're calculated and when they're paid. High or layered fees drag on your net return, so you want a reasonable, transparent fee structure aligned with the value the sponsor provides.</p>
<p>Pay particular attention to the promote (carried interest) — the share of profits the sponsor earns above a return threshold (the hurdle or preferred return). Understand the preferred return (the return investors receive before the sponsor shares in profits), the promote percentage, and any catch-up or waterfall tiers. A well-structured promote aligns the sponsor's incentives with yours (they earn more when you do well), while an aggressive promote or a low/no hurdle can shift too much upside to the sponsor. So evaluate whether the fees and promote are reasonable and aligned.</p>
<p>So reviewing fees and the promote — the fee structure and the carried-interest/waterfall terms — shows the cost and alignment of the investment. Review fees and promote — examining the fee structure (acquisition, management, development, disposition fees) and the promote/carried interest (the preferred return, promote percentage, and waterfall tiers) — reveals the cost to you and the sponsor's incentive alignment. Reasonable, transparent, aligned terms are preferable. Understanding fee/promote diligence shows how to assess cost and alignment. Review the fund's fees (acquisition, management, development, disposition) and promote (preferred return, carried interest, waterfall) to assess the cost to you and whether the sponsor's incentives are aligned with strong investor returns.</p>
<h2 id="assess-exit">Assess exit strategy</h2>
<p>Assessing the exit strategy clarifies how and when you'll get your capital back — critical for an illiquid, long-hold OZ investment. Because the tax-free exclusion requires a 10-year hold, the exit is typically structured around the 10-year mark, so examine the fund's planned exit: will it sell the assets, refinance to return capital, or offer another liquidity mechanism, and on what timeline? Understand whether the fund's structure and term align with the 10-year hold you need for the exclusion.</p>
<p>Consider the realism and flexibility of the exit plan: Does the sponsor have a credible path to sell or recapitalize the assets at the 10-year mark? What happens if market conditions are poor at that time — is there flexibility to extend or wait? Is there any interim liquidity (generally limited or none in OZ funds), and what are the consequences of an early exit (forfeiting the exclusion, a possible inclusion event)? A clear, realistic, 10-year-aligned exit plan is important; a vague or misaligned one is a red flag.</p>
<p>So assessing the exit strategy — the planned exit, its timeline, and its alignment with the 10-year hold — clarifies how you'll realize the investment. Assess the exit strategy — examining the planned exit (asset sale, refinance, or other mechanism), its timeline and alignment with the 10-year hold required for the exclusion, the realism and flexibility of the plan, and the (limited) interim liquidity and early-exit consequences — to clarify how and when you'll get your capital back. A clear, 10-year-aligned exit matters. Understanding exit diligence completes the checklist. Assess the exit strategy — the planned exit, its timeline, and its alignment with the 10-year hold — to clarify how and when you'll realize your capital; a clear, realistic, 10-year-aligned exit is essential for an illiquid OZ investment.</p>
<p>Because OZ funds are illiquid and designed for the long hold, the exit is where the investment is ultimately made or lost — so this final checklist item deserves careful attention before you commit.</p>

<h2 id="putting-it-together">Putting the checklist to work</h2>
<p>Putting the checklist to work means applying all five areas — sponsor, project and market, compliance, fees and promote, and exit — together, since a fund must hold up across the board, not just on one dimension. A great sponsor with a poor market, or a sound project with abusive fees, or a compliant fund with no realistic exit, each falls short. So weigh the areas collectively to form an overall judgment about the fund.</p>
<p>Use the checklist to structure your questions to the fund and sponsor, to organize the documents you review (the PPM, financials, projections, and compliance materials), and to coordinate with your advisors — your CPA on the tax/compliance points, your attorney on the legal terms, and your financial advisor on suitability and fit. A disciplined, documented process helps you make an informed decision and avoid being swayed by the tax incentive alone.</p>
<p>So putting the checklist to work — applying all five areas together and coordinating with your advisors — turns diligence into an informed decision.
