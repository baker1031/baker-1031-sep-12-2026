# 1031 Exchange: Primary Residence vs. Investment Property

I keep seeing a home sale described as an automatic 1031 opportunity. It is not. The relevant question is not how much the property appreciated or how long someone has owned it. It is whether the property was held as a home, an investment, a business asset, or some documented mix of those uses.

Section 1031 is for investment or business property. Section 121 is the tax code’s separate tool for a primary residence. The two can meet when use changes over time or when a property has both personal and investment portions, but that is where shortcuts become expensive.

First-order thinking is “the sale has a gain, so I should use a 1031.” Second-order thinking is “which rule fits the property’s actual use, what gain can be excluded versus deferred, and do the recordkeeping and timing support the result?” The tax treatment follows the facts, not the wish.

## Why a Primary Residence Doesn’t Qualify

Section 1031 requires property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business. A primary residence is personal-use property because you live in it. It therefore does not meet the held-for-investment requirement and cannot be the subject of a like-kind exchange.

This is not a technical footnote. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) continues an investment in different real estate. A home you live in is not investment property in that sense, so it falls outside the provision.

The good news is that homeowners have a different rule that is often more favorable: the Section 121 exclusion. For a residence, the useful question is usually not “can I 1031 it?” but “how does Section 121 apply?”

## Section 121: The Home-Sale Exclusion

Section 121 may exclude up to **$250,000** of gain on a qualifying primary-residence sale, or **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly. The owner must have owned and used the home as a main residence for at least **two of the five years** before sale.

Unlike a 1031, Section 121 excludes qualifying gain. It is gone, not deferred, up to the limit. For many homeowners, the **$250,000/$500,000** exclusion covers the entire gain and no tax is due on that part.

The exclusion can generally be used once every **two years** on a qualifying primary residence. Gain above the exclusion limit is taxed as capital gain.

## Section 121 vs. Section 1031

The provisions serve different property and produce different results.

| Rule | Property it covers | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Section 121 | Primary residence | Excludes up to **$250k/$500k** of gain |
| Section 1031 | Investment or business property | Defers gain with no dollar limit through reinvestment in like-kind property |

One excludes and one defers. One is for a home; the other is for investments. One has a dollar cap; the other does not. Whether the property was used personally or held for investment is the starting point.

The more interesting cases sit between those columns: a property that has been both a residence and an investment over time, or a property with mixed use. In those circumstances, the provisions can apply to different periods or portions of the gain.

## Converting a Home to a Rental

A former primary residence can become 1031-eligible if it is converted to a genuine rental and held as investment property. The conversion must be real: the property is actually rented and treated as an investment, not rented briefly as a formality.

There is no bright-line period in the statute. Holding and renting the former home for a meaningful period—often suggested as a year or more, across tax years—can help establish investment use. Once the property is genuinely a rental, it can be exchanged like other investment property.

This can be a planning option where a home’s gain exceeds the Section 121 limit. The owner may convert the house to a genuine rental, hold it as investment property, and later exchange it to defer gain that exceeds the Section 121 exclusion. Timing and genuine use are central, so plan with your CPA.

## Converting a Rental to a Residence

The reverse conversion—moving into a former rental and making it a primary residence—can create access to part of the Section 121 exclusion, but with important limits. After **2008**, periods of non-qualified use, such as rental use instead of residence use, reduce the exclusion.

If the rental was acquired in a 1031 exchange, it generally must be owned for at least **five years** before the owner can use any Section 121 exclusion. That rule prevents a quick conversion of deferred-gain property into excluded-gain property.

Depreciation recapture from the rental period is not excludable under Section 121; it remains taxable. See the discussion of [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html). The non-qualified-use rule, five-year rule, and recapture limitation mean a rental-to-residence conversion can offer some Section 121 benefit without offering all of it. This is work for a CPA.

## Key Takeaways

- A primary residence does not qualify for a 1031. Section 121, which may exclude **$250k/$500k**, is the primary-residence tool.
- Section 121 excludes gain; Section 1031 defers it. One applies to homes, the other to investment property.
- Conversion and mixed-use cases can combine the two provisions, but only with real use, careful allocation, and timed planning.

## Mixed-Use Property Treatment

Mixed-use property can include a duplex where you live in one half and rent the other, a home with a separate rental unit, or a home with a qualifying home office or rented portion. The investment portion may be eligible for a 1031 exchange while the residence portion may use Section 121.

That requires a reasonable, documented allocation of value and gain between the personal and investment portions. In a duplex where one unit is occupied and the other rented, roughly half may be Section 121 property and half may be 1031-eligible, depending on the facts.

This is one of the clearest examples of the provisions working together in one sale: exclude the gain assigned to the residence portion and defer the gain assigned to the investment portion. The allocation needs to be defensible, so develop it with your CPA.

## Combining Section 121 and Section 1031

In some situations, an owner may apply Section 121 to part of the gain and defer the rest through a 1031 exchange. The classic case is a former residence later used as a rental. If the ownership and use tests fit within the relevant lookback period, the owner may exclude gain up to the Section 121 limit and defer remaining gain through an exchange of the now-investment property.

That combination can exclude a meaningful portion of gain outright while deferring the rest. It is also technical and timing-sensitive. The result depends on the Section 121 use tests, non-qualified-use rules, and the 1031 requirements.

Because the rules interact, a CPA should map the plan well before the sale. Do not treat a conversion as a switch that can be flipped at closing.

## Vacation Homes and Second Homes

A second home used mainly for personal enjoyment is personal-use property. It is not 1031-eligible and, because it is not a primary residence, it does not use the Section 121 exclusion. A vacation property genuinely held for investment may qualify for a 1031 exchange.

The IRS safe harbor for vacation-home exchanges generally looks for fair-value rental of at least **14 days** per year and personal use of no more than **14 days** or **10%** of rental days, in each of the **two years** before and after the exchange.

The question is whether the property is actually an investment—rented with limited personal use—or primarily a personal retreat. Document the rental activity and personal-use limits if you intend to exchange a vacation property.

## Common Mistakes With Residence Exchanges

The first common mistake is assuming a home can be exchanged under Section 1031. It cannot, although Section 121 often covers much or all of the gain. The second is a sham conversion: briefly renting a home to claim 1031 eligibility without genuine investment use. The IRS can challenge that approach.

Other errors include overlooking the five-year rule for a 1031-acquired property converted to a residence, forgetting that depreciation recapture is not excluded under Section 121, and failing to document a mixed-use allocation.

These are avoidable errors when the applicable provision is identified early and conversions are genuine. When a property has been both a home and an investment, a CPA’s involvement is essential.

## Which Strategy Fits Your Situation?

For a pure primary residence, Section 121 is the relevant rule, and it often excludes the entire qualifying gain. For pure investment property, a 1031 exchange may defer gain. The planning opportunities are in between.

If home gain exceeds the Section 121 limit, a genuine rental conversion followed by a 1031 may defer the excess. If the property is mixed-use, Section 121 and 1031 may apply to the personal and investment portions. If it is a vacation property, the safe-harbor investment-use facts are central.

These strategies are technical and timing-sensitive. Plan with your CPA before selling or converting. Understanding the full history of personal and investment use is what lets you address the tax result across a property’s entire life.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I do a 1031 exchange on my primary residence?

No. A primary residence is personal-use property and does not qualify for a 1031 exchange. Section 121 is the relevant home-sale rule and may exclude up to **$250,000** of gain, or **$500,000** for married couples, if the ownership and use tests are met.

### What is the difference between Section 121 and Section 1031?

Section 121 applies to a primary residence and excludes up to **$250k/$500k** of gain. Section 1031 applies to investment or business property and defers gain with no dollar limit through reinvestment in like-kind property. One excludes home gain; the other defers investment-property gain.

### Can I convert my home to a rental and then do a 1031?

Potentially. A former residence that is converted into a genuine rental and held as investment property for a meaningful time can become 1031-eligible. The conversion cannot be a brief formality. This may help defer gain above the Section 121 limit.

### Can I combine the 121 exclusion and a 1031 exchange?

In some facts, including a former residence later used as a rental, you can exclude part of gain under Section 121 and defer the rest through a 1031. The combination is technical and timing-sensitive, so use a CPA to apply the 121 tests, non-qualified-use rules, and 1031 requirements.

### How much gain can Section 121 exclude?

Up to **$250,000** for a single filer or **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly on a primary residence owned and used as the main home for at least **two of the five years** before sale. Gain above the limit is capital gain.

### Can I use a 1031 on a mixed-use property?

Yes, in part. With a duplex or other property containing both personal and investment use, the investment portion may be eligible for a 1031 and the residence portion may use Section 121. The allocation must be reasonable and documented.

### Does converting a rental to a residence let me use Section 121?

Partly, with limits. Non-qualified rental use reduces the exclusion, depreciation recapture is not excludable, and a rental acquired in a 1031 generally requires **five years** of ownership before Section 121 applies.

### Can I 1031 a vacation home?

Only if the vacation property is genuinely held for investment. The safe harbor generally calls for fair-value rent for at least **14 days** per year and personal use no greater than **14 days** or **10%** of rental days. A vacation home used mainly for personal enjoyment does not qualify.

### Is there a holding period to convert a home to a rental for 1031?

There is no bright-line rule. A meaningful rental period—often suggested as a year or more, across tax years—helps establish investment use. The conversion must be genuine, not brief formality.

### Does the 121 exclusion cover depreciation recapture?

No. If the property was rented and depreciation was taken, that depreciation recapture remains taxable even when other gain can be excluded under Section 121.

### What is the five-year rule for converting 1031 property to a residence?

If you acquired a property in a 1031 exchange and later convert it to a primary residence, you generally must own it for at least **five years** before using any Section 121 exclusion. The rule prevents quick conversion of deferred-gain property into excluded-gain property.

### Can married couples exclude more under Section 121?

Yes. Married couples filing jointly may exclude up to **$500,000**, rather than **$250,000** for a single filer, provided the ownership and use tests are met. Both spouses generally must use the home as a main residence for **two of the five years**.

### What if my home gain exceeds the 121 limit?

Gain above the **$250k/$500k** exclusion is capital gain. One possible plan is to convert the home into a genuine rental and later exchange it under Section 1031 to defer the excess, but the investment use must be real and the timing should be reviewed with a CPA.

### How do I allocate value in a mixed-use property?

Use a reasonable, documented method, often square footage, separate units, or appraised values for the personal and investment portions. The allocation determines the gain handled under Section 121 and the gain addressed in a 1031. Work out a defensible approach with your CPA.

### What’s the most common mistake with residence and investment property?

Assuming you can 1031 your home is the first mistake. The second is a sham conversion, such as briefly renting a home to assert 1031 eligibility without real investment use. Know which provision applies and plan conversions genuinely.

### Can I use both Section 121 and a 1031 on the same property?

In certain cases, yes. A former residence later used as a rental may permit Section 121 treatment up to the limit if the use tests fall within the lookback period, with remaining gain deferred through a 1031 of the now-investment property. The result remains subject to the 121 tests, non-qualified use, and 1031 requirements.

### How long do I have to live in a home to use Section 121?

You must own and use the home as your main residence for at least **two of the five years** before sale. The two years do not have to be continuous. The exclusion can generally be used once every **two years** on a qualifying primary residence.

### If I move into my rental, can I exclude all the gain?

Not all of it. Non-qualified use during rental periods after **2008** reduces the exclusion, rental depreciation recapture is not excludable, and a 1031-acquired property generally needs **five years** of ownership first. You may obtain some Section 121 benefit, subject to those limits.

### Does a home office or rented room affect my exchange options?

It can make the property mixed-use. A qualifying business or rental portion, such as a home office or rented room, may be 1031-eligible while the residence portion uses Section 121. The treatment depends on the actual extent of the use and the supporting documentation.

### What is the five-year rule and when does it apply?

For a property acquired in a 1031 exchange and later converted to a primary residence, ownership for at least **five years** is generally required before Section 121. The rule keeps deferred 1031 gain from being quickly wiped out through a Section 121 exclusion.

### Can I exchange a second home or vacation property?

Only if it is genuinely held for investment: rented at fair value for at least **14 days** each year, with personal use within the **14 days** or **10%** safe-harbor limit. A second home mainly used personally qualifies for neither a 1031 nor, because it is not a primary residence, Section 121.

### What is the Section 121 ownership and use test?

You must own the home and use it as the main residence for at least **two of the five years** before sale. The two years need not be continuous. Meeting the test supports an exclusion of up to **$250,000**, or **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly.

### Can I avoid all tax on my home sale?

Often, if your gain fits within the **$250k/$500k** Section 121 exclusion, the entire qualifying gain may be excluded. Gain above that limit is capital gain. A former home later converted to a genuine rental may have a 1031 path for deferring the excess.

### Does Section 1031 have a dollar limit like Section 121?

No. Section 1031 has no dollar cap on gain it can defer when investment property is reinvested into like-kind property of equal or greater value. Section 121, by contrast, caps the home-sale exclusion at **$250k/$500k**.

### If I rent out my old home then exchange it, when is it 1031-eligible?

When it is genuinely held and used as investment property. That usually calls for a meaningful rental period, often suggested as a year or more across tax years, rather than a brief formality. Confirm the timing with your CPA.

### Does depreciation on my former home affect the exchange?

If you converted the home to a rental and claimed depreciation, that depreciation creates recapture. A 1031 can defer recapture along with gain, while Section 121 cannot exclude the recapture. In a converted-home situation, 121 may exclude some appreciation while 1031 defers the remaining recapture and gain.

### Can a married couple combine 121 and 1031?

Yes, in appropriate facts. On a former residence converted to a rental, a married couple may exclude up to **$500,000** under Section 121 if the use tests fit, and defer the remaining gain through a 1031 exchange of the investment property. Plan the interaction with your CPA.

### Is a duplex I live in half of eligible for a 1031?

Partly. The rented half can be investment property eligible for a 1031, and the half you live in can use Section 121. A reasonable allocation, often by square footage or units, separates the two portions.

### Should I consult a professional for a 121/1031 situation?

Yes. Conversions, mixed-use allocation, non-qualified use, the five-year rule, and depreciation recapture are technical and timing-sensitive. A CPA experienced with real estate should plan the situation well in advance.

## Glossary

- **Section 121 Exclusion:** The exclusion of up to **$250k/$500k** of gain on the sale of a qualifying primary residence.
- **Primary Residence:** A personal-use home. It does not qualify for a 1031 exchange but may use Section 121.
- **Conversion:** Changing a property’s use from home to rental, or rental to home, to access different tax treatment.
- **Mixed-Use Property:** Property with both personal and investment use, allocated between Section 121 and Section 1031.
- **Non-Qualified Use:** Non-residence use, such as rental use, that reduces the Section 121 exclusion on a converted property.
- **Ownership and Use Test:** Ownership and main-residence use for **two of the five years** before sale.
- **Five-Year Rule:** The **5 years** of ownership generally required before using Section 121 on property acquired in a 1031 exchange.
- **Vacation-Home Safe Harbor:** IRS rental-day and limited-personal-use guidelines under which vacation property can be 1031-eligible.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation. It is not excludable under Section 121, even on a converted residence.
- **Held for Investment:** The 1031 requirement that property be held for investment or business rather than personal use.
- **Capital Gain:** Gain above the Section 121 exclusion limit, taxed at long-term capital-gains rates.
- **Allocation:** Division of a mixed-use property’s value and gain between personal and investment portions.
