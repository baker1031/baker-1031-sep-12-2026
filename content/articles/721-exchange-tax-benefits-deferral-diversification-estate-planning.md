# 721 Exchange Tax Benefits: Deferral, Diversification & Estate Planning


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep returning to the same decision point with long-held property: the sale price is visible, while the amount that actually remains to reinvest is not. Taxes, concentration, management burden, and an estate plan tend to show up only after the headline number has done its work.

A 721 exchange can combine three core benefits in one tax-deferred exit from direct real estate: tax deferral under Section 721, diversification from a single property into a REIT portfolio, and estate-planning advantages from a possible step-up at death and the divisibility of units among heirs. It can also offer passive income and a potential, limited path to liquidity. Those benefits need to be weighed against their trade-offs.

The first-order view is that deferral preserves more capital and diversification reduces the single-property problem. The second-order view asks what is given up: direct control, the ability to do another 1031, certainty around distributions and liquidity, and independence from the REIT’s management and performance. The benefits are meaningful only when that exchange of control for passive REIT ownership fits the owner’s goals. This is educational information, not tax, legal, or investment advice.

## The three core benefits

The appeal of [the 721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) rests on three linked benefits. First, tax deferral: contributing property to a REIT operating partnership for units under Section 721 generally defers capital-gain recognition, as a 1031 can. Second, diversification: a single property becomes a stake in the REIT’s portfolio. Third, estate planning: [OP units](op-units-explained.html) may receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death under current law and can be divided more readily among heirs.

These benefits reinforce one another. The deferral keeps pre-tax capital invested rather than losing it at the transition. That capital becomes part of a broader portfolio rather than remaining concentrated in one property. At death, the units may be passed in smaller, cleanly divided interests, with a possible basis adjustment. The combination is what distinguishes a 721 from other exit choices.

A taxable sale can provide liquidity and allow diversification, but it has no deferral. A 1031 can defer gain, but it keeps the owner in direct, usually concentrated and illiquid property. A 721 brings deferral, diversification, and estate features together, along with the possible income and liquidity features discussed below. Whether that combination is attractive depends on the owner’s readiness to become a passive REIT investor.

## Tax deferral

Tax deferral is the foundation. [Under Section 721](section-721-exchange-tax-code-behind-upreit-conversions.html), contributing property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units generally does not recognize the capital gain at contribution. A taxable sale of appreciated real estate can trigger a four-layer tax: federal capital-gain tax of up to 20%, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) of up to 25%, the 3.8% [NIIT](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. Under 2026 federal rates, that total can be a third or more of gain depending on income, state of residence, and individual facts.

The 721 defers that tax, allowing the full pre-tax value to move into the REIT portfolio rather than investing only the after-tax remainder. Like a 1031, the logic is that a larger amount remains invested and may compound for longer. Actual results still depend on REIT performance and are not guaranteed.

The deferral continues while OP units are held. It may become a permanent elimination of gain through a step-up at death, subject to the relevant conditions. It is not a free tax result; conversion of units to shares triggers deferred gain on the units converted. But preserving the capital that would otherwise be paid in a sale is the starting point that allows the other potential benefits to operate on the full amount.

## Diversification

[Diversification is a benefit the 721 exchange offers](diversification-benefits-of-an-upreit-conversion.html) that is harder to obtain in one step through a 1031 into another single property. A contribution for OP units changes a concentrated holding into an interest in a REIT portfolio that can include many properties, property types—apartments, retail, industrial, office, and others—and geographic markets.

One direct property can be exposed to a single location, tenant base, local economy, damage event, or tenant departure. In a diversified REIT, one property’s difficulty generally affects only a portion of the portfolio. That may reduce concentration risk. It does not guarantee a profit or protect against loss in a declining market.

The move can be immediate and professionally managed. Rather than adding properties gradually in serial 1031 exchanges, the investor receives an interest in a portfolio that is already broad and managed by the REIT team. The benefit is a reduction in single-property dependence, not an assurance that the portfolio will perform well.

## Estate planning

[Estate planning is often the most compelling 721 benefit for older owners](721-exchange-estate-planning.html). It combines a possible step-up in basis with the ease of dividing units. Under current law, if OP units are held until death, heirs may receive a basis adjusted to fair market value at death. That may reduce or eliminate the deferred gain.

The result is conditional. It depends on the operating partnership’s tax elections, generally including a Section 754 election, the estate’s circumstances, applicable estate tax, and future law. Subject to those conditions, the gain deferred through a 721 contribution, along with further appreciation, may pass to heirs without the income tax a sale would have caused. Investors should consult their own tax and estate advisors.

Divisibility is the other part of the estate benefit. It is difficult to split a single building among four heirs without forcing a sale or creating co-ownership problems. OP units can be allocated in specific numbers to each heir, which can divide real-estate wealth cleanly. The step-up may address deferred gain and divisible units can address the logistics of transfer.

## Passive income and liquidity

The three core benefits are supplemented by passive income and limited liquidity. OP units may pay distributions comparable to REIT dividends. For an owner exiting active management, that can replace rental income with hands-off income from the REIT portfolio. Distributions are not guaranteed; they may be reduced or suspended at the REIT’s discretion and may include a return of capital.

[OP units can be converted into REIT shares](liquidity-after-721-exchange-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html). In a publicly traded REIT, those shares can be sold in portions, which can offer more flexibility than direct real estate’s all-or-nothing sale. Conversion usually follows a lock-up and triggers deferred gain on the units converted.

Most 721/UPREIT programs reached through DSTs involve non-traded REITs. Their liquidity can be limited to share-repurchase programs that a board may modify or suspend. Investors should be prepared to hold indefinitely. Where liquidity is available, it can support gradual access to capital, but it is limited and not assured.

### Key Takeaways

- A 721 exchange can combine tax deferral, diversification, and estate planning in one transition.
- Section 721 deferral preserves pre-tax capital by avoiding the four-layer tax that a taxable sale can trigger.
- Diversification changes a concentrated single-property position into an immediate REIT-portfolio stake.
- A possible step-up at death and divisible units can aid estate planning; passive income and limited liquidity are additional benefits.

## Weighing the benefits against trade-offs

The benefits have a cost. The main trade-off is a loss of direct control and flexibility. The REIT manages the portfolio, and OP units are not real property that can be exchanged again under Section 1031. A 721 is generally a one-way move into passive REIT ownership.

The owner also becomes dependent on the REIT’s management and performance. Conversion to shares makes deferred gain taxable unless units are held until death. For public REITs, share-price volatility can affect value after conversion, while the units themselves track the REIT’s value. These are real considerations, not footnotes.

An owner ready for diversification, passivity, potential liquidity, and estate planning may find the benefits outweigh the trade-offs. An owner who values control, direct decision-making, and continued real-estate trading may not. The decision is a comparison of deferral, diversification, estate, income, and liquidity features with lost control, REIT dependence, and conversion tax.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main tax benefits of a 721 exchange?

The core benefits are tax deferral under Section 721, diversification from a single property into a REIT portfolio, and estate planning through a possible step-up at death and divisible units. Passive distributions and a possible liquidity path through share conversion are additional benefits. Together, they can make a 721 a comprehensive exit from direct real estate for the right owner.

### How does the 721 exchange defer taxes?

Contributing property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units under Section 721 generally defers capital-gain recognition at contribution. It defers the capital-gain, depreciation-recapture, NIIT, and state-tax layers that a sale can trigger—often a third or more of gain. Full capital moves into the REIT portfolio, the deferral lasts while units are held, and it may be eliminated through a step-up at death.

### How does a 721 exchange provide diversification?

It converts a concentrated single property into an interest in a REIT’s portfolio, usually with many properties, types, and markets. A problem at one property then affects only part of portfolio value. Compared with gradual diversification through serial 1031 exchanges, a 721 can deliver immediate, professionally managed diversification. It reduces concentration risk but does not eliminate market risk.

### What are the estate-planning benefits?

The potential step-up in basis at death may reduce or eliminate deferred gain when heirs receive OP units, subject to partnership tax elections, estate circumstances, estate tax, and future law. Units are also easier to divide among multiple heirs than one property. Together, those features can make a 721 a useful wealth-transfer tool. Consult your own tax and estate advisors.

### Do I earn income from a 721 exchange?

OP units can pay distributions comparable to REIT dividends, creating hands-off income from the portfolio without active property management. That can replace rental income for an owner stepping back from landlording. Distributions are not guaranteed and may be reduced, suspended, or include return of capital.

### Does a 721 exchange offer liquidity?

It can. OP units may be converted into REIT shares, which for publicly traded REITs can be sold in portions. That can provide a liquidity path that direct real estate lacks. Conversion follows a lock-up and triggers deferred gain; non-traded REITs can have more limited liquidity. The feature is useful, but it is not immediate or assured.

### How does the 721 compare to a taxable sale or a 1031?

A taxable sale can provide liquidity and allow diversification, but it pays the tax and loses deferral. A 1031 defers gain but leaves the investor in direct, concentrated, illiquid real estate. A 721 combines deferral and diversification with possible liquidity and estate benefits, at the cost of direct control and the ability to keep exchanging.

### What are the trade-offs of a 721 exchange?

The primary trade-off is loss of direct control and flexibility. The REIT manages the portfolio, and OP units generally cannot be used for another 1031. Other considerations are REIT-management and performance dependence, tax on conversion to shares, and exposure to REIT value or share price. These costs matter most to owners who value direct control and continued trading.

### Is the step-up benefit guaranteed?

The step-up at death is current law and generally applies to OP units as to other inherited assets, so it may erase deferred gain if units are held until death. Future tax law can change. Investors should verify current rules with their CPA and estate attorney, including estate-tax and partnership-election considerations.

### Who benefits most from a 721 exchange?

The strategy can fit owners ready to leave direct real estate for diversified, passive, potentially more liquid REIT ownership. It can be especially relevant to owners focused on estate transfer, tired of management, concentrated in one property, or seeking eventual liquidity. It requires comfort with the loss of direct control and the inability to continue 1031 exchanges from OP units.

### Does converting units to shares lose the tax benefits?

Conversion triggers deferred gain on the units converted, ending deferral for that portion. The investor has still received the prior deferral, diversification, and possible income, and can convert gradually to control timing. Holding units preserves the deferral and may preserve the path to a step-up at death. Conversion trades continued deferral for liquidity.

### How do I know if the benefits outweigh the trade-offs for me?

Compare the benefits—deferral, diversification, estate planning, income, and liquidity—with loss of control and flexibility, REIT dependence, and conversion tax in light of your goals. An owner seeking passivity and diversified REIT ownership may find the exchange attractive; an owner who wants to manage and keep trading real estate may not. A suitability review and the owner’s CPA and attorney can evaluate the specific facts.

## Glossary

- **721 Exchange Tax Benefits:** Deferral, diversification, and estate planning, plus income and liquidity.
- **Tax Deferral:** Section 721 deferring gain when property is contributed for OP units.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax, all potentially deferred by a 721.
- **Diversification:** Spreading risk across a REIT portfolio instead of one property.
- **Concentration Risk:** Dependence on one property, which diversification can reduce.
- **Estate Planning:** Using a possible step-up and divisible units for wealth transfer.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** A death-time basis reset that may erase deferred gain on OP units.
- **Divisibility:** The ease of dividing units among heirs compared with a single property.
- **Passive Income:** Hands-off distributions on OP units, a bonus benefit.
- **Liquidity:** Conversion of units into potentially tradable REIT shares, a bonus benefit.
- **OP Units:** Operating-partnership units received in a 721 and carrying its benefits.
- **Distributions:** Income paid on OP units, comparable to REIT dividends.
- **Conversion:** Exchange of units for shares, triggering deferred gain.
- **Control Trade-Off:** Giving up direct-ownership control for the 721’s benefits.
- **One-Way Move:** The generally irreversible 721 structure; OP units cannot be 1031 exchanged out.
- **Wealth Transfer:** Passing real-estate wealth to heirs, eased by estate features.
