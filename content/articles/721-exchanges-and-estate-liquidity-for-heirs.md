# 721 Exchanges and Estate Liquidity for Heirs

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › 721 Exchanges and Estate Liquidity for Heirs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep thinking about the difference between leaving heirs an asset and leaving them choices. A valuable building can be a gift, but it can also leave a family short of cash and long on decisions.

Estate liquidity is the need for cash to pay estate taxes, expenses, or to divide an estate fairly. Real estate can be valuable yet hard to turn into cash quickly. A 721 exchange can convert property into OP units before death; heirs then receive a divisible interest that may be convertible to REIT shares, with a possible step-up making liquidity low-tax.

The first-order view is that heirs inherit a more liquid asset. The second-order view is whether the units actually convert into a traded REIT, whether redemption is limited, and how the estate plan, tax law, and family preferences fit together. Liquidity is a practical benefit, not a guarantee of cash on any schedule.

## The estate liquidity challenge

Heirs may need cash relatively soon after death for estate tax, legal and administrative expenses, equalizing distributions, or buyouts among beneficiaries. A single property is illiquid: sale takes time and a rushed sale can occur at a discount. Dividing one building can force a sale or create shared ownership and disputes.

The result can be an estate that is asset rich but cash poor. The heirs hold valuable property but must borrow, sell under pressure, or take other unfavorable steps to fund obligations. A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) can address that problem by changing the asset into [OP units](op-units-explained.html) before death.

## How OP units provide liquidity for heirs

OP units may be converted into REIT shares. For a traded REIT, the shares can be sold for cash, giving heirs a path to liquidity that direct property lacks. Units can also be sold in portions rather than through an all-or-nothing property sale.

Units are divisible. Each heir can receive a separate portion, then independently hold for income or convert and sell for cash. That avoids forcing a single decision on heirs with different needs.

## The step-up enabling low-tax liquidity

A [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can reset the inherited OP units’ basis to date-of-death value. If heirs convert and sell shortly after inheritance, the deferred gain may have been erased and the sale can create little or no income tax. This combines convertibility with low-tax liquidity.

The step-up is a tax benefit; access to cash is a separate liquidity benefit. They are related but distinct. Current law, estate facts, and tax treatment should be reviewed with the family’s CPA and estate attorney.

## Dividing and selling among heirs

A building is difficult to divide. OP units can be allocated in specific amounts among beneficiaries. An heir who wants income and continuing real-estate exposure can hold units and receive any distributions. An heir who needs cash can convert and sell that heir’s portion, without requiring every beneficiary to do the same.

This clean division resolves a common conflict: some heirs want to keep the investment, while others need or prefer cash. It also reduces the pressure toward shared-property disputes or a forced sale.

### Key Takeaways

- Estate liquidity means cash available for taxes, expenses, and fair division when heirs inherit an estate.
- OP units can be convertible to cash and divide cleanly among heirs, unlike a single illiquid property.
- A step-up can make post-inheritance conversion and sale low-tax by erasing deferred gain.
- Separate units let some heirs hold for income while others access cash, avoiding a forced common decision.

## Funding estate needs

Convertible units can help heirs fund estate tax if an estate has estate-tax exposure, avoiding a hurried property sale to meet an obligation due after death. They can also fund legal, administrative, and other estate expenses.

The units can support equalizing distributions or buyouts where the estate plan calls for cash to balance unequal assets or allow one heir to purchase another’s interest. The practical value is access to cash without needing to force a sale of an illiquid property.

## Comparing to illiquid property

Inheriting property directly can still bring a step-up, but it does not solve the asset’s liquidity or division problem. Cash needs can lead to borrowing or a discounted sale, and beneficiaries must decide together what to do with an indivisible asset.

Inheriting OP units can add convertibility, divisibility, and low-tax liquidity. The comparison therefore favors units for an owner concerned about heir cash needs and divergent family preferences, provided the REIT’s actual liquidity terms are appropriate.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the estate liquidity challenge?

It is the problem of heirs needing cash for taxes, expenses, or division while inheriting illiquid real estate. That can make them asset rich but cash poor and can lead to a rushed sale, borrowing, or other unfavorable measures. A 721 can make the inherited asset more liquid as OP units.

### How do OP units provide liquidity for heirs?

They can be converted into REIT shares and, for a traded REIT, sold for cash. Because units divide cleanly, each heir can convert and sell that heir’s own portion. This provides a path to cash that an illiquid property lacks.

### Does the step-up help with estate liquidity?

Yes. A stepped-up basis can erase deferred gain, so heirs who convert and sell shortly after inheritance may have little or no income tax. The step-up makes the unit liquidity more tax-efficient.

### How does this help divide the estate among heirs?

Each heir can receive separate units, unlike a single property that is hard to split. Each can hold for income or convert and sell for cash. That permits a clean division when beneficiaries have different preferences.

### Can OP units help pay estate taxes?

They can provide a source of cash if estate tax is owed, depending on estate size. Heirs may convert and sell units to fund tax without a forced property sale. This is especially relevant to estates with estate-tax exposure.

### Is inheriting OP units better than inheriting property?

For estate liquidity, generally yes. Both can receive a step-up, but OP units can add convertibility and divisibility. A property can leave heirs with forced-sale and division problems.

### Do all OP units provide good estate liquidity?

No. Units converting into a publicly traded REIT generally offer better market liquidity. Units in a non-traded REIT may be subject to limited redemption programs. The REIT type and actual terms matter.

### What if some heirs want to keep the investment?

Divisibility allows that. Heirs who want to hold can retain their units and receive distributions, while heirs who want cash can convert and sell their own portions. No common sale is required.

### Should estate liquidity be a reason to do a 721 exchange?

It can be a meaningful reason for owners worried about an illiquid, hard-to-divide estate, estate-tax exposure, or heirs with different needs. It should be evaluated with the 721’s other benefits and limitations and as part of the estate plan.

### Is the estate-liquidity benefit different from the step-up benefit?

Yes. The step-up is the tax benefit that may erase deferred gain. Estate liquidity is the practical ability to access cash. Convertible units provide liquidity; the step-up can make using it low-tax.

### What if my heirs disagree about keeping or selling?

Separate units let every heir choose. One can hold for distributions and another can convert and sell for cash. That is a major advantage over an undivided property where heirs may need to agree or force a sale.

### Does providing estate liquidity reduce the inheritance’s value?

No. Liquidity makes the value more accessible; it does not reduce the value itself. Heirs receive OP units representing the property’s value, potentially with deferred gain erased through a step-up, and can turn their portion into cash when needed.

## Glossary

- **Estate Liquidity:** Cash available to meet estate needs, potentially provided by OP units.
- **Liquidity Challenge:** Heirs needing cash while inheriting illiquid real estate.
- **Illiquid Property:** Real estate that cannot be quickly turned into cash.
- **Convertibility:** OP units’ ability to become sellable REIT shares.
- **Divisibility:** OP units dividing cleanly among heirs.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Death-time reset that can make heir liquidity low-tax.
- **Estate Tax:** A tax unit liquidity can help heirs fund.
- **Estate Expenses:** Settlement costs unit liquidity can cover.
- **Equalizing Distributions:** Cash to balance unequal inheritances, potentially from units.
- **Buyout:** One heir buying another’s share, funded with liquidity.
- **Asset Rich, Cash Poor:** Valuable illiquid assets but insufficient cash.
- **Forced Sale:** A rushed property sale to raise cash, which units may help avoid.
- **Traded REIT:** A REIT whose shares can provide the best heir liquidity.
- **Heir Liquidity:** Each heir’s access to cash from units.
- **Clean Division:** Division through units instead of shared property.
- **Low-Tax Liquidity:** Cash access with little tax due to a step-up.
