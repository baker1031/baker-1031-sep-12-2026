# DST Cash Flow vs. Appreciation: Setting Expectations


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I start by separating the yield an investor sees from the total return the investment may deliver. A DST's return has three drivers: current income, appreciation, and debt paydown. Judging a DST from projected distribution yield alone overlooks two of them.

First-order thinking asks whether the yield is high enough. Second-order thinking asks where that yield comes from, what is being traded away for it, how the debt behaves, and how much of the outcome waits for full-cycle sale. Asset classes can favor income or growth, but none of the projections are guarantees.

This guide covers each return driver, the limits of projections, income-oriented and growth-oriented property types, and how to form a balanced expectation. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. This is educational information; confirm current rules and your specific situation with a tax advisor.

## Income vs. Appreciation in DSTs

A DST's total return has three sources. **Current income** is the regular distribution funded by net cash flow: rent collected, less operating expenses, debt service, and reserves. It is the most visible part because it arrives during the hold and is easily stated as a yield.

**Appreciation** is growth in property value, realized when the sponsor sells at full cycle. **Debt paydown** is equity created as an amortizing loan reduces principal over the hold. That equity is returned at sale even if the property value is unchanged.

These components interact. A long-term lease with a stable tenant can support dependable income and limited appreciation. A repositioned property can distribute little during the work and aim for more value growth later. Debt paydown works in the background by converting rental cash flow into equity. Two DSTs with the same distribution yield can therefore produce very different total returns depending on appreciation and amortization.

## Why Projections Aren't Guarantees

Projected distribution yield, appreciation, and total return are modeled estimates. They rest on assumptions about rents, occupancy, expenses, interest costs, and sale price over the hold. Those assumptions can prove accurate, too optimistic, or too conservative because tenant performance, local rents, the broader economy, interest rates, and market conditions at sale are outside anyone's control.

A projected 6% distribution is a target, not a promise. If a major tenant leaves, occupancy falls, or expenses rise faster than rent, net cash flow can shrink and distributions can be reduced or suspended. A weak exit market can leave appreciation unrealized or produce a sale below projections, including a loss.

DSTs are non-promissory. No one guarantees projected income, return, or return of capital. Treating estimates as guarantees is a common and costly mistake. The right response is to examine the assumptions, accept the possibility of a shortfall, and size the investment accordingly.

## Asset Classes That Favor Income

Some asset classes favor steady current distributions over dramatic appreciation. Net-lease properties—including single-tenant retail, pharmacies, and industrial buildings—are commonly leased long-term to creditworthy tenants under triple-net terms, where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Long leases and tenant credit can produce predictable rent, but lease terms also tend to cap upside.

Self-storage can also produce dependable cash flow through many short-term tenants and low operating costs. Medical office is another income-oriented type: healthcare tenants often sign long leases, invest heavily in their space, and support stable occupancy through non-cyclical healthcare demand.

These DSTs can fit an investor focused on a regular distribution stream, including a retiree replacing cash flow from property sold in a 1031 exchange. The trade-off is usually lower appreciation potential. Stability is the feature; capped upside is the cost.

## Asset Classes That Favor Growth

Growth-oriented property types place more of the prospective return in appreciation. Value-add multifamily is a central example: a sponsor acquires an apartment property, renovates units and common areas, raises rents as leases turn, and seeks a higher sale value once improvements lift income. Distributions can be modest while capital goes into improvements, making return back-loaded into the full-cycle sale.

Development sits farther out on the risk curve. Construction and lease-up can generate little or no current income; potential return concentrates in the value of a finished, stabilized asset at sale or refinancing. Potential reward is higher, and so is risk.

Some industrial and logistics properties can favor growth when e-commerce and supply-chain reconfiguration support rent and value gains. These positions can suit investors who do not need distributions for current living expenses and can accept lower current income and more risk in pursuit of appreciation.

## Key Takeaways

- DST total return combines current income, appreciation at sale, and debt-paydown equity; yield is only one component.
- Return figures are assumptions-based projections, not guarantees. Distributions can be cut and appreciation may not materialize because DSTs are non-promissory.
- Net-lease, self-storage, and medical office commonly favor income. Value-add multifamily, development, and some industrial properties commonly favor growth.
- A balanced expectation matches the return profile to the investor's goals and considers all three drivers rather than chasing yield.

## The Quiet Role of Debt Paydown

Debt paydown is often missed because it does not appear in distribution yield and attracts less attention than appreciation. Most DSTs use non-recourse mortgage debt. If a loan amortizes, part of the rent servicing it reduces principal, and the trust's equity increases over time. That accumulated equity comes back to investors when the property sells even if the asset's market value stays flat.

Loan structure determines its size. An amortizing loan steadily builds equity; an interest-only loan builds none through paydown because principal stays unchanged to maturity. Two otherwise similar DSTs can therefore have different total returns simply because one loan amortizes and the other does not.

Debt paydown is relatively steadier than appreciation because it relies on the property generating enough income to service the amortizing loan, not on a market-value increase. It affects return as well as risk and deserves a place in the underwriting.

## Building a Balanced Expectation

Evaluate the full picture: current income, appreciation, and debt paydown. Then start with the investor's objective. Someone replacing income from a sold property may reasonably prefer income-favoring property types and a well-covered distribution. Someone seeking growth and not needing cash flow may accept lower yield for potential appreciation.

Hold projections at arm's length. Test tenant credit, lease terms, market rents, and the exit cap rate; these assumptions determine whether a projection is credible. Nothing is guaranteed.

Diversification can also help. Multiple DSTs with different profiles—some income-oriented and some growth-oriented—can provide a more balanced total position than putting all capital behind one projected outcome. The goal is not the largest headline yield. It is an expectation grounded in all three return drivers and an honest read of risk.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where does a DST's return come from?

From three sources: current income, appreciation, and debt paydown. Current income is distribution cash flow from rent after operating expenses, debt service, and reserves. Appreciation is property-value growth realized at sale. Debt paydown is equity built when an amortizing loan reduces principal. The components interact, so two DSTs with the same yield can deliver different total returns based on how much they appreciate and amortize. A full assessment looks beyond the headline yield.

### Are DST distributions guaranteed?

No. They are projections based on sponsor assumptions about rents, occupancy, expenses, and interest costs. If a major tenant leaves, occupancy falls, or expenses outrun rents, cash flow may fall and distributions may be reduced or suspended. DSTs are non-promissory; neither income nor return of capital is guaranteed. A projected distribution is a target, not a promise.

### What is the difference between income and appreciation in a DST?

Income is the cash distribution received during the hold from property net cash flow. Appreciation is property-value growth realized only when the sponsor sells at full cycle. An income-oriented property can provide steady distributions but modest appreciation; a repositioning may provide little current income while aiming for more value growth at sale. Debt paydown is the third component, quietly adding equity. The difference is both source and timing.

### What is debt paydown in a DST?

It is the equity built when a non-recourse amortizing loan repays principal over time. A portion of the rent used for debt service reduces the balance, increasing equity even if property value stays flat. The equity returns at sale. It does not appear in distribution yield. An amortizing loan produces it; an interest-only loan does not. Because it depends on loan service rather than market appreciation, it is one of the steadier pieces of total return.

### Why aren't DST projections guarantees?

They depend on uncertain future assumptions: rents, occupancy, operating costs, interest rates, tenant performance, sale price, the local market, and conditions at exit. Any may prove wrong. Distributions can be cut and appreciation can fail to appear or become a loss. Nothing about the income or return of capital is guaranteed. Scrutinize the assumptions and size the position for outcomes below projections.

### Which DST asset classes favor income?

Net-lease, self-storage, and medical office frequently do. Net-lease properties can use long-term triple-net leases with creditworthy tenants; self-storage can benefit from many short-term tenants and low operating costs; and medical office can benefit from long healthcare leases, tenant investment in space, and non-cyclical demand. The trade-off for more dependable income is generally lower appreciation potential. They can suit investors who need distributions to replace prior property cash flow.

### Which DST asset classes favor growth?

Value-add multifamily, development, and some industrial or logistics properties commonly do. Value-add multifamily uses renovations to raise rent and value; development creates value through construction and lease-up; and some industrial assets can benefit from e-commerce and supply-chain demand. Current income can be lower, particularly during renovation or construction, while return is more dependent on full-cycle sale. The potential upside comes with more risk and suits investors who can wait and do not need distributions for spending.

### What is a triple-net (NNN) lease?

It is a lease in which the tenant pays base rent plus the three major property expenses: real-estate taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The arrangement shifts much of the operating burden from landlord to tenant and can produce a predictable, low-management income stream. It is common in single-tenant net-lease DSTs with pharmacies, retail stores, or industrial buildings. Long leases and strong credit can support income stability, though fixed or modest rent escalation may limit appreciation.

### Should I judge a DST on its distribution yield alone?

No. Yield measures current income but ignores appreciation and debt paydown. Identical yields can conceal very different total returns. A high yield can also signal a riskier property, weak tenant credit, or aggressive assumptions, and it can be cut if performance declines. Review property type, income-versus-growth profile, loan structure, sponsor assumptions, and fit with the investor's objectives instead of chasing one number.

### How does loan structure affect DST returns?

It influences return and risk. An amortizing non-recourse loan pays principal over time and builds equity that returns at sale. An interest-only loan adds no equity through paydown. Thus, two otherwise identical DSTs can differ in total return solely because one loan amortizes. Term and maturity matter because a DST generally cannot refinance; fixed-rate, long-term, non-recourse debt can improve stability. Loan review is part of evaluating both potential return and resilience.

### Can a DST lose money?

Yes. Distributions can fall or stop if tenants leave, occupancy declines, or expenses outpace rent. Property can sell below its original cost, leaving investors with less than invested or, in a severe case, a substantial capital loss. Leverage amplifies loss because valuation declines fall first on equity. Illiquidity can prevent an exit before the sponsor sells. This is why projections are not guarantees and why sponsor quality, conservative underwriting, diversification, and only risking suitable capital matter.

### How do I build a balanced expectation for a DST?

Match the full return profile to the goal. An investor needing cash flow may favor net-lease or medical office and a well-covered distribution. An investor focused on growth may accept lower yield from value-add or development. Test tenant credit, lease terms, rents, and exit cap rate rather than accepting projections at face value. Diversify across income- and growth-oriented DSTs when capital permits so the overall position does not depend on one deal's estimate.

### Do income-focused DSTs have lower returns than growth DSTs?

Not necessarily. They distribute potential return differently. Income-focused positions tend to provide more regular distributions and lower appreciation potential, while growth positions distribute less current income and rely more on appreciation at sale. Total return depends on actual performance, which is uncertain. Growth can carry more risk because more relies on exit value; income can offer steadier cash flow but capped upside. The question is not which label is always better but which return shape and risk level fit the investor.

### Should I diversify across income and growth DSTs?

It can build a balanced position. A 1031 exchange can split proceeds among multiple replacement properties: for example, net-lease or medical office for current cash flow and value-add or industrial for appreciation potential. That can blend income and growth while spreading sponsor, property-type, market, and single-deal risk. Different trusts may also sell at different times, which can stagger full-cycle exits. The trade-off is more positions to monitor and smaller allocations to each.

## Glossary

**Total Return**  
The sum of current income, appreciation, and debt paydown over the hold.

**Current Income**  
The regular DST distributions from property net cash flow.

**Appreciation**  
Growth in property value, realized as gain at sale.

**Debt Paydown**  
Equity built as an amortizing loan reduces principal over time.

**Distribution Yield**  
Annual distributions as a percentage of invested capital.

**Net Cash Flow**  
Rent minus operating expenses, debt service, and reserves.

**Projection**  
A sponsor's modeled estimate of returns, not a guarantee.

**Non-Promissory**  
Describing an investment whose returns are not guaranteed.

**Net-Lease Property**  
A single-tenant property on a long lease, favoring steady income.

**Triple-Net (NNN) Lease**  
A lease where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

**Self-Storage**  
An income-favoring class with many short-term tenants and low costs.

**Medical Office**  
An income-favoring class with long, sticky healthcare leases.

**Value-Add Multifamily**  
A growth-favoring class renovated to raise rents and value.

**Development**  
A growth-favoring strategy with returns concentrated at sale.

**Amortizing Loan**  
A loan that pays down principal, building equity over time.

**Interest-Only Loan**  
A loan paying only interest, building no equity through paydown.

## DST cash flow vs. appreciation at a glance

How current income and long-term appreciation differ in emphasis and favored asset classes:

| Factor | Cash Flow (Income) | Appreciation (Growth) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it delivers | Regular distributions during the hold | Value gain realized at sale |
| Predictability | Steadier, but projections are not guaranteed | Less certain; depends on market and exit |
| Favored asset classes | Net-lease, stabilized multifamily | Growth-market multifamily, value-add |
| Role of debt paydown | Modest current effect | Builds equity quietly over the hold |
| Timing of return | Throughout the hold period | Concentrated at full-cycle sale |
| Investor goal served | Current spending income | Long-term wealth growth |

## Which should you choose?

Weight income-oriented DSTs—net-lease and stabilized assets—when distributions are needed for current spending and predictability matters more than upside.

Weight growth-oriented DSTs when the investor can wait for full-cycle sale and wants appreciation and debt paydown to build equity over time. Many investors seek balance by holding some of each and by remembering that projected distributions remain estimates, not guarantees.
