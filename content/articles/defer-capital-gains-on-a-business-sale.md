# Defer Capital Gains on a Business Sale: Your Accurate Options

I keep seeing a familiar assumption surface when a business sale gets close: “We will just do a 1031.” It is an understandable instinct. It is also usually the wrong tool.

Section 1031 applies to like-kind real property held for investment or business use. It does not defer gain on the goodwill, equipment, inventory, intangible assets, or company equity that account for most of an operating-business sale. A business owner needs to begin with the character of the gain and the structure of the deal, not with the hoped-for answer.

The accurate paths are narrower but useful: a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) under IRC §1400Z-2, an installment sale under IRC §453, and—where a qualifying C-corporation *stock* sale meets the rules—the QSBS exclusion under IRC §1202. The right mix turns on an asset sale versus a stock sale and the character of each gain. QOF interests are securities offered to accredited investors after suitability review. This is educational information, not tax advice; confirm the facts with your CPA.

First-order thinking sees a large taxable gain and asks how quickly it can be deferred. Second-order thinking asks which dollars are actually eligible, when each timing clock starts, what liquidity and control are being exchanged for, and whether the deal structure has already foreclosed a better outcome. The tax result is not separable from the transaction itself.

## Why a 1031 Exchange Doesn't Defer a Business-Sale Gain

A business sale can create gain from goodwill and other intangibles, equipment and other personal property, inventory, and often equity in the company itself. IRC §1031 defers gain only in an exchange of like-kind real property held for investment or business use. It does not cover goodwill, equipment, intangibles, inventory, or stock. In ordinary circumstances, an owner cannot “1031” the sale of the operating business.

There is one related real-estate distinction. If the business separately owned a building or land held for investment, that real property may potentially be exchanged under §1031 on its own. That does not make the operating business eligible. It simply means the real-property component and the operating-business components need to be analyzed separately.

That distinction is why business sellers need different deferral tools.

## The Lead Strategy: A Qualified Opportunity Fund for the Gain

For an eligible capital gain—including gain from selling business assets or equity—a QOF can be the most flexible tax-advantaged route. Under IRC §1400Z-2, an investor generally has **180 days** to invest the realized gain amount in a QOF and defer the tax. Many QOFs invest in real estate in designated Opportunity Zones, which can give a business seller real-estate exposure while the eligible gain is deferred. See [Opportunity Zone investing for stock and business-sale gains](opportunity-zone-investing-for-stock-and-business-sale-gains.html) for the mechanics.

Two details are important. First, the investor generally needs to reinvest the **realized gain**, not the entire sale proceeds. The remaining cash can stay available for other purposes. Second, a business sale can involve [Section 1231 gains](investing-section-1231-gains-in-an-opportunity-zone.html), from depreciable business property and real estate used in the business, or gain passed through a partnership or S corporation on a [K-1](investing-a-partnership-or-k-1-gain-in-a-qof.html). Both can qualify for Opportunity Zone treatment, but the **180-day** period may begin on different dates, including sometimes year-end for pass-through gain.

The potential signature benefit is a **10-year** hold: appreciation on the QOF itself can be tax-free. The original deferred gain is recognized later on the schedule set by statute. The 2025 “OZ 2.0” legislation made the program permanent with a rolling deferral, so the current timing and eligibility still need CPA confirmation.

> A 1031 moves real estate into real estate. A Qualified Opportunity Fund moves a gain—including a business-sale gain—into Opportunity Zone real estate. That is the bridge a business seller actually needs.

## Installment Sale: Spreading the Gain Over Time

When a seller finances part of the transaction through an earn-out or seller note, an installment sale under IRC §453 can recognize gain proportionally as payments arrive instead of all in the sale year. That can spread a large gain across several tax years and may keep the seller in lower brackets.

It is not a universal answer. Depreciation recapture on equipment is generally taxed up front, inventory and some other assets do not qualify for installment treatment, and the seller takes buyer-default risk. An installment sale can be a complement to a QOF or an alternative when the purchase price will be received over time. A CPA should confirm which components qualify.

## QSBS: A Potential Outright Exclusion for Stock Sales

QSBS—qualified small business stock under IRC §1202—can potentially exclude part or all of the gain on a qualifying C-corporation *stock* sale from federal tax, subject to holding-period and per-issuer dollar limits. This is an exclusion, not a deferral. When it applies, it can be more valuable than simply postponing tax.

The conditions are exacting. The transaction must involve qualifying C-corporation stock rather than assets. Entity type, the length of the holder’s ownership, the company’s gross-asset history, the nature of the business, and the stock-versus-asset structure all matter. Partnerships and S corporations do not qualify merely because the underlying business is small. Have a CPA test QSBS eligibility early, ideally before the deal closes, because deal structure affects qualification.

## Key Takeaways

- A 1031 exchange does **not** defer a business-sale gain. It covers like-kind real property; separately held business real estate can be a distinct exception.
- A QOF under IRC §1400Z-2 can defer eligible realized gain when it is invested within **180 days**, often into Opportunity Zone real estate, with potential tax-free QOF appreciation after **10 years**.
- An installment sale under IRC §453 spreads gain across the years payments are received, but items such as depreciation recapture can still be taxed up front.
- QSBS under IRC §1202 may exclude qualifying small-business-stock gain outright. The time to test it is before the deal structure is set.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use a 1031 exchange to defer the gain from selling my business?

Generally no. IRC §1031 applies only to like-kind real property held for investment or business use. Goodwill, intangibles, equipment, inventory, and equity in the business do not qualify, so an operating-business sale cannot be exchanged under §1031.

If the business separately owned investment real estate—a building or land—that property may potentially be exchanged on its own. The business-sale gain calls for other tools: a QOF, which can defer eligible realized gain invested within **180 days** under IRC §1400Z-2; an installment sale under IRC §453; or, for a qualifying C-corp stock sale, a QSBS exclusion under IRC §1202. Which path fits depends on deal structure. Confirm it with your CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How does a Qualified Opportunity Fund defer a business-sale gain?

Under IRC §1400Z-2, an eligible capital gain, including gain from business assets or equity, can generally be invested in a QOF within **180 days** to defer tax. Because many QOFs invest in Opportunity Zone real estate, the seller can put the gain into real-estate exposure while the gain is deferred.

Only the realized gain, not all sale proceeds, generally needs to be invested. Section 1231 gain and gain passed through on a partnership or S-corp K-1 can qualify, but their **180-day** clocks may have different starts, sometimes at year-end for pass-through gain. A QOF held at least **10 years** can make its own appreciation tax-free. The original deferred gain follows the statutory recognition schedule, which the 2025 OZ 2.0 legislation revised to a permanent, rolling deferral. Verify the current timing and eligibility with your CPA.

### What is the 180-day window and when does it start for a business sale?

The **180-day** window is the period in which a realized capital gain must be invested in a QOF to seek deferral under IRC §1400Z-2. For a direct sale, it generally starts on the sale date, when the gain is realized.

For a partnership, S corporation, or other pass-through entity reporting a K-1, the partner may have flexibility: the period can begin on the last day of the entity’s tax year or, in some cases, on the entity’s sale date. Section 1231 netting and multiple components can make the start later than closing, but they can also make it easy to miss. Identify a potentially suitable QOF before or around closing, and have a CPA confirm the clock for each component. Do not assume the timing.

### What is QSBS and could it apply to my business sale?

QSBS is qualified small business stock under IRC §1202. It may allow a partial or full federal exclusion of gain on qualifying C-corporation stock, subject to holding-period and per-issuer dollar limits. It is for a stock sale, not an asset sale, and it does not apply to a partnership or S corporation unless the structure was specifically arranged to qualify.

Eligibility depends on the entity type, holding period, gross-asset history, nature of the business, and deal structure. Because an exclusion eliminates tax instead of merely postponing it, it can be more valuable than deferral when it applies. A CPA should evaluate it before the transaction is finalized. If it does not apply, a QOF or installment sale may be the relevant deferral route.

### Can I combine these strategies?

Sometimes, but a CPA should coordinate the plan. If part of a sale qualifies for the QSBS exclusion under IRC §1202, that portion may be excluded; a separate, non-excluded portion of capital gain might then be invested in a QOF for deferral. An installment sale can spread recognition of gain that is neither excluded nor deferred.

The same dollar cannot be both excluded and deferred. The QOF **180-day** clock and installment timing can interact, while depreciation recapture and ordinary-income components are generally taxed without these benefits. Business-sale taxation depends on entity type, asset allocation, and deal structure. Model alternatives with your CPA before closing, then use Baker 1031 to evaluate particular QOF offerings if that investment route is part of the strategy.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF):** A fund investing in Opportunity Zones that can be eligible for Opportunity Zone tax benefits.
- **IRC §1400Z-2:** The Opportunity Zone statute that permits capital-gain deferral and a potential **10-year** exclusion on QOF appreciation.
- **180-Day Rule:** The window in which a realized gain must be invested in a QOF.
- **Installment Sale:** A sale reported over multiple years as payments are received under IRC §453.
- **QSBS:** Qualified small business stock, which can provide a potential gain exclusion under IRC §1202.
- **Section 1231 Gain:** Gain from selling depreciable business property and business real estate.
- **K-1 Gain:** Gain passed from a partnership or S corporation to its owners.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred, real-property-only exchange that does not cover a business sale.
- **Goodwill:** Intangible business value that is not eligible for §1031 deferral.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds under SEC Rule 501.

## A practical stance before committing capital

I would not let the prospect of deferral decide a business sale. Before closing, I would separate every component of gain, identify which timing rules apply, and test whether the QOF’s illiquidity, **10-year** horizon, and investment risks remain acceptable after the tax question is set aside. I would have the CPA settle the eligibility and structure first, then decide whether the investment itself earns a place in the plan.
