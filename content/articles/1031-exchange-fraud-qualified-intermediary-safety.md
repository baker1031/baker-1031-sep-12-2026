# 1031 Exchange Fraud & Qualified Intermediary Safety

I keep seeing exchange proceeds treated as though they sit in a bank account simply because a closing statement shows a familiar name. They may not. A qualified intermediary, or QI, is necessary in a standard deferred 1031 exchange, but the protections surrounding a QI are not the protections surrounding a bank deposit.

The QI holds the sale proceeds between the relinquished-property closing and the replacement-property closing so the investor does not have constructive receipt of the money. That period can last up to **180 days**, and the proceeds can be substantial. There is no federal licensing requirement for QIs, no FDIC insurance for exchange funds, and limited specific oversight in most states.

First-order thinking is that hiring any QI completes the procedural box. Second-order thinking is to ask who actually controls the cash, how it is segregated, what happens if the firm fails, and which controls stop one person from moving the money. The QI is essential. The fund-security arrangement is an investment-risk decision in its own right.

## The risk: QIs aren't regulated like banks

[Qualified intermediaries](insights.html) do not operate under bank-style federal regulation. There is no federal QI license, no federal oversight of how client exchange money is held, and no FDIC insurance for the funds. A few states have enacted some requirements, but most have little or no specific QI regulation.

That means the usual protection a person expects for money in a bank is not automatically present. If a QI becomes insolvent, misappropriates money, or invests client funds in assets that lose value, the investor can lose the exchange proceeds and may have limited recourse. The risk is meaningful because the amount is often the entire proceeds of a property sale and may be held for up to **180 days**.

This is not an accusation against all QIs. Many are financially sound, use secure fund arrangements, and have long clean records. The issue is that the investor and advisors must evaluate those safeguards themselves. A strong QI can be secure; a weak or dishonest one can create a severe problem.

## Notable QI failures and frauds

The risk is not theoretical. There have been failures and frauds in which QIs misappropriated or lost client exchange funds, preventing investors from completing their exchanges and costing them their sale proceeds. In some cases, client money was invested in risky or illiquid assets that lost value, especially around financial downturns. In others, principals stole the money outright.

The patterns are familiar: commingled accounts, risky investment of the float, or inadequate safeguards against misuse. When a QI failed or principals disappeared, clients often faced bankruptcy or litigation with little protection. Some recovered pennies on the dollar, if anything, even though the funds represented the sale of a property.

Those cases increased awareness and prompted some state action, but they did not erase the underlying gap. The lesson is not to avoid the QI; a QI is required for a standard exchange. The lesson is to examine financial soundness and fund security before the proceeds are wired.

> QI failures have occurred. Investors have sometimes recovered only pennies on the dollar after an intermediary misappropriated or lost exchange funds.

## How QI fraud happens

The most direct path is misappropriation. If a QI or its principals control commingled client money without segregation, dual authorization, or a qualified trust or escrow arrangement, a dishonest person can divert it. The absence of separation and controls creates the opportunity.

Another path is risky investment of the float, meaning the client money the QI is holding. A QI seeking to earn a return may place that money in risky or illiquid assets. If those investments fall in value or cannot be sold when needed, the QI may not be able to return the proceeds for the replacement closing. The investor loses access even if no one intended outright theft. Safe, liquid custody avoids that particular exposure.

Financial failure is the third path. If a weak QI mixes client proceeds with its operating money, insolvency can pull client funds into the firm’s bankruptcy estate. The clients may become unsecured creditors. Commingling, weak financials, risky handling, and absent controls are therefore not small administrative issues; they are the conditions that make losses possible.

## Vetting a QI's fund security

Start with custody. Client money should sit in separate, segregated accounts, ideally qualified escrow accounts or qualified trust accounts, rather than in the QI’s operating account or a pool with other client funds. Segregation helps protect the proceeds against the QI’s insolvency and misuse.

Then verify the financial protections. Ask about a fidelity bond that covers employee theft or misappropriation, errors-and-omissions insurance, and the firm’s financial strength. A sound balance sheet and a well-capitalized parent company matter. Some of the strongest QIs are subsidiaries of large financial institutions or title companies with established controls. Ask for the bonding amount, the insurance coverage, and the financial backing. A secure firm should be prepared to provide that information.

Also look for dual authorization on disbursements, qualified escrow or trust arrangements, and a clean track record without complaints or failures. The combination matters: segregated accounts, bonding, insurance, financial strength, and disbursement controls. Conduct this due diligence with your advisors before entrusting a QI with your proceeds.

## Red flags to watch

Commingling is a major warning sign. If the QI pools client funds with its operating money or other funds rather than using segregated accounts, treat that as a serious concern. A QI that cannot demonstrate fidelity bonding or errors-and-omissions coverage also lacks basic protection. Reluctance or inability to give clear information about fund security, finances, or safeguards is itself a warning.

Other red flags include promises of above-market interest on exchange money, weak or opaque financial statements, missing dual-authorization controls, and a history of complaints, failures, or litigation. A thinly capitalized standalone QI without strong institutional backing or robust controls may carry more risk than a well-capitalized firm supported by a bank or title company.

Pressure tactics and unusual fund-handling requests deserve attention as well. A legitimate QI follows ordinary secure practices. A red flag does not prove fraud, but it is a reason to investigate more deeply or select another QI with demonstrable protections.

## Key takeaways

- QIs are not federally regulated or insured like banks, so fund safety depends on the QI’s integrity and soundness.
- Losses can arise through misappropriation from commingled accounts, risky investment of funds, or a QI’s financial failure.
- Verify segregated qualified escrow or trust accounts, fidelity bonding, E&O insurance, financial strength, and dual authorization.
- Treat commingling, missing coverage, above-market interest promises, opaque financials, and a history of problems as red flags.

## Protecting yourself

Vetting is only the first layer. One of the strongest additional protections is to request a qualified escrow account or qualified trust account. These arrangements are permitted under the regulations and hold the proceeds in a segregated structure with legal protection, often involving a third-party escrow agent or trustee. They can reduce the chance that a QI’s insolvency or misconduct puts the money at risk.

Ask for dual authorization on fund disbursements as well. Requiring your authorization or another two-party control prevents the QI from moving money unilaterally. Some QIs offer accounts where the client’s signature is required. Holding proceeds at a sound, well-known financial institution rather than in the QI’s own accounts adds another layer of protection.

Finally, select a QI with institutional backing, strong financials, robust controls, and a clean record. A large-bank or title-company subsidiary can offer more financial strength than a weak standalone operation, though every firm still needs specific vetting. Combining a strong QI with qualified escrow or trust custody and dual authorization provides the best available protection for a large, time-sensitive pool of exchange proceeds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are qualified intermediaries regulated?

Not the way banks are. There is no federal licensing requirement, federal custody oversight, or FDIC insurance for QI-held exchange funds. Most states have little or no specific regulation, although a few have enacted requirements. Your funds’ safety therefore depends on the QI’s own soundness and integrity, making fund-security vetting your responsibility.

### Is my exchange money insured?

Not by the FDIC as bank deposits are. A secure QI can offer segregated qualified escrow or trust accounts, fidelity bonding against employee theft, errors-and-omissions insurance, and financial strength, but those are the QI’s protections rather than federal insurance. Verify the protections that apply to the specific QI.

### Has QI fraud actually happened?

Yes. There have been cases of misappropriated or lost exchange money that produced severe losses, sometimes with only pennies-on-the-dollar recovery. Some involved investments of client money that failed, especially in downturns; others involved theft. The absence of federal insurance can leave investors with limited recourse.

### How does QI fraud happen?

It can occur when a dishonest QI steals from commingled accounts, when a QI invests the float in risky assets that lose value, or when a weak QI’s insolvency absorbs commingled client money. The underlying conditions are commingling, poor controls, risky fund handling, and weak financials. Segregation, sound custody, controls, and financial strength address those conditions.

### How do I vet a QI's fund security?

Confirm segregated accounts, preferably qualified escrow or trust accounts, rather than commingled money. Verify a fidelity bond, errors-and-omissions insurance, a sound balance sheet or institutional backing, and dual authorization for disbursements. Ask for the bonding amount, insurance coverage, and financial backing. A secure QI should supply the details readily.

### What are the red flags of an unsafe QI?

Warnings include commingling, no bond or insurance, promises of above-market interest, weak or opaque finances, no dual authorization, reluctance to provide information, a thin standalone capital base, and complaints, failures, or litigation. Pressure tactics and unusual fund-handling requests deserve scrutiny. Investigate further or choose another demonstrably secure QI.

### What is a qualified escrow or trust account?

It is a regulation-permitted fund-holding arrangement that keeps exchange proceeds segregated with legal protection, often involving a third-party escrow agent or trustee. Compared with money in the QI’s own account, it can reduce exposure to misappropriation or QI insolvency and is among the strongest protections available during an exchange.

### How can I protect my funds during an exchange?

Request a qualified escrow or trust account, require dual authorization for disbursements, use a sound financial institution for custody, and choose a strong institutionally backed QI with controls and a clean record. A secure QI and these protective arrangements work together to maximize fund safety.

### Is a bigger QI safer?

Often, a QI that is a subsidiary of a large bank or title company has stronger financials, controls, and institutional oversight than a thin standalone firm. But size alone is not enough. Confirm segregation, bonding, insurance, and controls in every case. Institutional backing is a positive indicator, not a substitute for verification.

### What happens to my funds if my QI fails?

It depends on custody. Properly segregated funds in a qualified escrow or trust account should be protected from the QI’s insolvency and returned to you. Commingled funds can make you an unsecured creditor and can result in a partial or total loss. That is why fund arrangements are consequential.

### Should I just avoid using a QI?

No. A QI is required for a standard deferred exchange because your own control of the proceeds would be constructive receipt and would disqualify the exchange. The solution is not avoiding a QI; it is selecting a secure one and using segregation, insurance, financial strength, qualified escrow or trust custody, and dual authorization.

### Does Baker 1031 act as my QI?

No. Baker 1031 says it helps clients select and work with a secure independent QI, evaluate fund security, recognize red flags, use protective arrangements, and coordinate the exchange. Keeping the QI independent and choosing a strong firm are elements of sound exchange practice.

### Does a few states' QI regulation make those QIs safer?

A handful of states impose requirements such as bonding, insurance, or segregation. Those rules can add protection, but they vary and most states have little or no QI regulation. State regulation is a modest positive, not a reason to skip independent verification of segregated accounts, coverage, financial strength, and controls.

### How much interest should I expect on my exchange funds?

Modest, market-rate interest consistent with safe, liquid custody, such as money-market-type rates, is normal. Be cautious about above-market promises because they can imply the QI is taking investment risk with the proceeds. Protecting the money matters more than chasing a higher stated return.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party holding exchange funds; it is not federally regulated or insured like a bank.
- **Fund Security:** The measures used to protect client funds, including segregation, bonding, insurance, and controls.
- **Segregated Account:** A separate account that holds client funds apart from a QI’s operating funds.
- **Commingling:** Pooling client money with operating or other funds; a major red flag.
- **Qualified Escrow Account:** A protected fund-holding arrangement that provides added legal protection.
- **Qualified Trust Account:** A trust arrangement that holds exchange funds with legal protection.
- **Fidelity Bond:** Insurance that protects against employee theft or misappropriation.
- **Errors-and-Omissions Insurance:** Coverage for a QI’s professional errors.
- **Dual Authorization:** A control requiring two parties to release funds, limiting unilateral misuse.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Control over proceeds that disqualifies the exchange; the QI structure is intended to prevent it.
- **Misappropriation:** Diversion or theft of client funds, a mechanism of QI fraud.
- **Float:** Client money held by a QI, which becomes risky if it is invested for return.
- **Unsecured Creditor:** The position a client may have if commingled funds are caught in a QI insolvency.
- **Institutional Backing:** Support from a large bank or title company that can indicate financial strength.
- **Red Flag:** A warning sign of an unsafe QI, such as commingling or absent bonding.
- **Track Record:** A QI’s history; no failures or complaints can indicate safety.
