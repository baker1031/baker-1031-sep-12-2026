# Retirement Planning With Real Estate

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Definitive Guide · 2026 · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 30 min read**

I keep seeing the same retirement question dressed up in different numbers: can an owner keep the income from real estate while giving up the work? That question is not really about a building. It is about making capital useful without letting its operational demands take over the next chapter of life.

Real estate can replace a paycheck with passive, tax-sheltered income while equity continues to compound. This guide brings together the [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), [DSTs](dst-guide.html), [REITs](reits-guide.html), [721 UPREITs](721-exchange-guide.html), [mineral royalties](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html), and [Opportunity Zones](opportunity-zones-guide.html), and asks what each can do for retirement.

First-order thinking says that a high distribution rate, a tax deferral, or a passive label resolves the problem. Second-order thinking asks what is behind the check: liquidity, leverage, concentration, inflation, the durability of the income, and what happens to heirs. That is where a retirement plan becomes honest.

## The essentials

- A 1031 exchange can move active rental equity into passive DST, REIT, or royalty income while deferring tax; depreciation commonly shelters a portion of that income.
- DSTs and REITs can suit steady income. A 721 UPREIT can add diversification and later liquidity. Minerals may offer high yield, and Opportunity Zones are for long-horizon growth.
- Most of these holdings are illiquid for years. Keep a cash buffer outside them. Holding real estate to death can also give heirs a step-up in basis.

## 01 · Real Estate as a Retirement Engine

Real estate earns its place in a retirement plan for three familiar reasons. It produces income through rent and distributions. It can keep pace with inflation because rents and property values generally rise with prices over time. And it has tax features: depreciation can shelter income, a 1031 can defer gain while an owner repositions, and a step-up in basis can matter to heirs.

The task is to convert those features into passive, durable income. That usually means stepping back from hands-on ownership, not simply holding the same operating burden longer.

## 02 · The Tired-Landlord Problem

Direct rentals are a business. Leasing, repairs, compliance, capital projects, and tenant issues do not retire when the owner does. Income may still be welcome, but a concentrated bet on a few buildings can feel less appropriate late in the game.

Selling outright can bring capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture, erasing years of appreciation. The problem is therefore not merely how to sell. It is how to step back from management and diversify without handing a substantial part of the equity to the IRS.

The aim is to defer the tax rather than defer retirement—and to retire from the landlording. — Baker 1031 Research

## 03 · The 1031: The Bridge From Active to Passive

A 1031 exchange can make the transition possible. An owner sells actively managed investment property and directs the proceeds into passive replacement real estate, deferring gain and recapture. Equity that had been tied to a building the owner managed can then sit in a vehicle designed to distribute income.

That does not make every replacement equal. The choice still has to fit income needs, liquidity, and legacy planning.

## 04 · DSTs: Hands-Off Monthly Income

A Delaware Statutory Trust is a common answer to the tired-landlord problem. An investor can exchange into fractional interests in institutional real estate and receive monthly distributions while a professional sponsor handles the property. Because a DST is a pass-through, depreciation may shelter a meaningful portion of the cash flow, much as it did in direct ownership.

Diversifying among DSTs by sector, geography, and sponsor can reduce the dependence on one property or manager. See the full [DST guide](dst-guide.html).

## 05 · REITs & 721 UPREITs: Diversified Income With a Path to Liquidity

REITs, especially non-traded NAV REITs, can provide professionally managed and diversified real-estate income. REIT dividends carry a **20% deduction** that can lower their tax cost.

For retirement, the 721 UPREIT path deserves careful attention: exchange into a DST, then allow a REIT to acquire it for operating-partnership units. That can provide diversified income now and a path to liquidity later, because units may be converted to REIT shares and sold in pieces for later-retirement needs. It can also make estate division easier.

The trade-offs matter. A 721 move is one-way, and conversion is taxable. See the [721 guide](721-exchange-guide.html) and [REIT guide](reits-guide.html).

## 06 · Mineral & Royalty Income: High Yield, With Caveats

Producing mineral and royalty interests can be high-yielding real-property assets; the sector benchmark used here is near **9.6%** on average. Qualifying perpetual interests may be reachable through a 1031 exchange.

But high yield is not the same as a retirement foundation. Royalty income can decline as wells deplete and swing with commodity prices. It is better treated as one sleeve of a diversified plan. See the [mineral rights guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html).

## 07 · Opportunity Zones: The Long-Horizon Growth Bucket

Opportunity Zones are different. They are built for long-term, tax-free growth rather than current income. Appreciation can be excluded from tax after a ten-year hold, which can make an OZ fund a growth sleeve for someone a decade or more from needing the capital, or a legacy allocation.

It is not an income or step-up play. Deferred OZ gain does not receive a step-up at death. See the [estate guide](estate-planning-real-estate-guide.html) and the [Opportunity Zones guide](opportunity-zones-guide.html).

## 08 · Estimate Your Passive Income

Illustrative yield examples are DST ~**5%**, net lease ~**5.2%**, non-traded REIT ~**5%**, healthcare DST ~**6.4%**, minerals ~**9.6%**, and Opportunity Zone ~**1%** (growth). Yields and sheltered portions vary by vehicle, leverage, and year. Depreciation or return-of-capital shelter is tax-deferred, not tax-free. This is not investment advice.

## 09 · Will It Cover Your Spending?

The important question is not just “how much income?” It is whether the income covers spending without consuming principal. Living off cash flow preserves capital for later needs and for heirs.

An illustrative check uses a **$1,500,000** real-estate nest egg, **5.5%** expected yield, and a **$70,000** annual income need. It shows income generated, surplus or gap, and the nest egg needed. An income-only calculation ignores inflation, taxes, and fees. A real plan layers Social Security, other assets, and a cash buffer; it is not financial advice.

## 10 · Which Path Fits You?

Four questions can narrow the field: What is needed most—steady income, later liquidity, highest yield, or growth? Is retirement immediate, a few years out, or ten-plus years away? How important is liquidity later? And what is being reinvested?

A DST can fit someone seeking hands-off 1031 income now, with the trade-off of a **5–10** year hold. A non-traded REIT, often through 721, can fit someone wanting diversified income and a liquidity path, but the 721 is one-way and redemptions can be gated. Minerals can fit someone accepting commodity swings for higher current yield. An Opportunity Zone can fit a decade-plus growth horizon, with long illiquidity and no step-up on deferred gain.

## 11 · The Liquidity Trade-off

DSTs, non-traded REITs, private REITs, and Opportunity Zone funds are illiquid. Capital may be committed for years, and non-traded REIT redemptions can be gated. In retirement, an unexpected expense or market shock may not wait.

Three guardrails help: keep a cash and liquid-investment buffer outside these vehicles, often a year or more of expenses; do not over-concentrate net worth in a single illiquid holding; and consider laddering DSTs with different expected hold periods so capital can become available at intervals. Income is the point. The lock-up is part of the price.

## 12 · Real Estate in Retirement Accounts

These investments can be held in a self-directed IRA (SDIRA), but leverage introduces a tax issue. When an IRA owns debt-financed real estate—and most DSTs use a mortgage—the leveraged portion can create unrelated debt-financed income (UDFI), subject to unrelated business income tax (UBIT) inside the IRA.

REIT dividends are generally exempt from that tax, which can make REITs cleaner IRA holdings. A solo 401(k) has a special exemption from UDFI on leveraged real estate, unlike an SDIRA. The practical points are straightforward: REITs fit IRAs neatly; a leveraged DST in an IRA can owe UBIT on the financed share; and a solo 401(k) avoids that rule. Coordinate with the custodian and CPA before acting.

## 13 · Retirement Income Readiness

Use this **0/6** checklist:

- Keep a cash or liquid buffer outside illiquid real estate for emergencies, often a year or more of expenses.
- Understand that DST, REIT, and OZ holdings are illiquid and size them accordingly.
- Draw retirement income from more than one property or source.
- Ensure a meaningful part of real-estate income is tax-sheltered through depreciation, return of capital, or 199A.
- Account for inflation over a long retirement.
- Coordinate the income plan with a CPA or adviser and an estate plan that considers the step-up.

The first two are foundational. At **6/6**, revisit the plan as markets and laws change. At **4/6** or more, close the remaining gaps with an adviser. Below that, begin with liquidity and correctly sized illiquid holdings.

## 14 · Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I retire from being a landlord without a big tax bill?

Use a 1031 exchange to roll a rental into passive replacement real estate; a DST is a common choice. It can defer capital gains and depreciation recapture, move the owner away from management, and replace rent collection with distributions.

### Is real-estate income in retirement really passive?

Direct rentals are not; they are a business. DSTs, REITs, and 721 UPREIT units can be passive because a sponsor or REIT manages the property and the investor receives distributions, commonly monthly.

### Why is part of my DST income not taxed?

DST depreciation can shelter part of distributions, making that portion tax-deferred rather than currently taxed. REITs may also return capital and offer a **20%** deduction on ordinary dividends.

### What if I need my money back during retirement?

Plan for illiquidity. Keep a cash buffer outside these holdings, avoid over-concentration, and ladder DSTs with different hold periods. A 721 into a REIT can later provide a path to liquidity through share conversion and sales in pieces.

### Can I hold these in my IRA?

Yes, through a self-directed IRA, but a leveraged DST can trigger UBIT on its debt-financed portion. REIT dividends are generally exempt, and a solo 401(k) avoids the debt-financed-income rule. Check with the custodian and CPA first.

## 15 · Glossary

**Passive Income:** Income received without active management, including distributions from a DST, REIT, or royalty interest.

**Tired Landlord:** An owner ready to leave hands-on rental management while keeping the income.

**Depreciation Shelter:** The portion of real-estate income offset by depreciation and therefore tax-deferred.

**Laddering:** Staggering investments with different hold periods so capital becomes available at intervals.

**Self-Directed IRA (SDIRA):** An IRA that can hold alternative assets such as DSTs, REITs, and real estate.

**UDFI / UBIT:** Tax on debt-financed income earned inside an IRA, relevant to leveraged DSTs.

**Return of Capital:** A distribution that is not current income; it reduces basis and defers tax.

**Step-Up in Basis:** A reset of basis to fair-market value at death that can erase deferred gain for heirs.
