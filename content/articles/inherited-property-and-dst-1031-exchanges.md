# Inherited Property and DST 1031 Exchanges


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep coming back to the problem that sits behind many inherited properties: the tax question is often simpler than the family decision. Heirs may be deciding whether to keep, sell, or exchange a property while also sorting through management, different financial needs, and a building that cannot be divided in any practical way.

First-order thinking says an inherited property must need a 1031 exchange because it is real estate. Second-order thinking begins with the basis. A step-up to date-of-death value often changes the tax math; a prompt sale can produce little or no taxable gain. The more useful question may be whether a DST can turn one management-heavy, indivisible asset into passive, diversified, divisible income. This guide covers the inherited-property challenges, the step-up in basis, fractional DST interests, division among heirs, and family coordination. ## Challenges of Inherited Property

An inherited property can carry sentimental and financial value, yet confront heirs with work the original owner was equipped to do. Adult children with their own careers and homes may not want to become landlords or take on tenants, repairs, vacancies, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and the ongoing work of a rental. A property that suited the deceased owner can become an unwelcome burden for people who never chose it.

Multiple heirs can make the problem harder. One may want income or to preserve the property for sentimental reasons; another may want cash; a third may want another solution. Joint ownership usually prevents any one heir from acting alone. Different financial needs, timelines, emotions, and risk tolerances can make co-owning and co-managing a single apartment building strain family relationships.

The underlying constraint is simple: a building cannot be split into separate usable pieces for each heir. The family is left to co-own, co-manage, or sell it and split the proceeds. That combination of management burden, family conflict, ongoing responsibilities, and an indivisible asset is the reason a DST solution can be worth evaluating.

## Step-Up in Basis Considerations

Before considering a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), heirs should understand the [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html). Under Section 1014, inherited property’s basis is generally stepped up to fair market value as of the original owner’s date of death. The accumulated built-in capital gain is effectively erased: the heir’s basis becomes date-of-death value rather than the amount the original owner paid, potentially decades earlier.

That can be decisive. If the heirs sell soon after inheriting, sale price may be close to the stepped-up basis, leaving little or no taxable gain and little or no gain for a 1031 to defer. This is different from a long-held, appreciated property. Heirs should not assume they need a 1031 merely because they own real estate.

The step-up does not freeze the future. Appreciation after the step-up can produce a taxable gain on a later sale, and an incomplete step-up can alter the result. Other taxes and considerations, including estate tax at the estate level or state rules, may apply separately. Confirm basis, eligibility, and tax treatment with a CPA and attorney before acting; this is educational, not advice.

> The step-up is the fact heirs most often overlook: resetting inherited property to date-of-death value can mean a prompt sale produces almost no tax, so a 1031 is not automatically necessary just to defer a gain.

## Exchanging Into Fractional DST Interests

Even when a step-up reduces the tax reason for a 1031, a DST can still address the practical problem. Heirs who want real-estate exposure and income but not management can sell the inherited property and reinvest proceeds through a 1031 into [fractional beneficial interests in one or more DSTs](what-is-a-delaware-statutory-trust-dst-complete-guide.html). The sponsor handles tenants, maintenance, operations, and other management; the heirs own beneficial interests and receive their share of rental income as distributions.

That exchange can provide passivity, diversification across properties, sectors, and sponsors, and—critically—divisibility. If there is post-step-up appreciation, a 1031 can defer that taxable gain. If there is no gain to defer, heirs may sell and buy DSTs as a new investment without using the 1031 structure. The practical benefit remains the same: an unwanted, hard-to-divide building can become passive, professionally managed, diversified, divisible income.

That does not erase the trade-offs. DST distributions are projections that can be reduced or suspended, DSTs are illiquid, and interests are securities for accredited investors after a suitability review. Heirs should keep liquid reserves elsewhere and treat DSTs as one part of their finances.

## Dividing Interests Among Heirs

A single property cannot practically be divided: three children cannot each receive a third of an apartment complex. A DST changes the unit of ownership. Once an inherited building is exchanged into fractional beneficial interests, those interests can be allocated among heirs so each receives a separate share rather than a forced co-ownership position.

Each heir can then hold an independently owned DST interest that produces passive income. An heir seeking [DST income to draw on](dst-investing-for-retirement-income.html) can retain the interest; another can plan around the DST’s full-cycle exit. A diversified DST portfolio can be divided even more flexibly by allocating interests across several trusts in proportions the family agrees on. The result is autonomy without tying everyone to the decisions around a shared building.

This can defuse conflict, but it does not replace planning. The family must still agree through the sale and exchange, and an estate attorney should coordinate the division. In short, fractional interests can turn an indivisible, conflict-prone asset into cleanly divisible interests.

## Key Takeaways

- Inherited property can create a management burden and disagreement among heirs, made worse by the fact that one building cannot be practically divided.
- The step-up in basis generally resets inherited property to date-of-death value; a prompt sale may create little or no gain, so a 1031 may not be needed then.
- A 1031 into fractional DST interests can convert a management-heavy building into passive, diversified, divisible income.
- Fractional DST interests can be divided cleanly among heirs, but the family should coordinate with its estate attorney and CPA. This is educational, not advice.

## When a 1031 Into DSTs Makes Sense for Heirs

Heirs should separate the tax reason for a 1031 from the practical reason. If a prompt sale follows a full step-up, there may be little gain to defer; the family can sell and reinvest as it chooses, including buying DSTs without a 1031. The exchange becomes more relevant when the heirs hold the property and it appreciates after the step-up, or when the step-up did not fully erase gain.

But taxes are not the only consideration. A 1031 into DSTs may make sense for heirs who want to preserve real-estate income, avoid management, diversify, and divide ownership cleanly. For many families, passivity, diversification, divisibility, and less friction are the central reasons—not tax deferral.

The choice is among keeping the property, selling it, or exchanging it. Keeping can fit heirs who want the specific asset and are willing and able to manage it or hire management, accepting concentration and responsibility. Selling can fit a need for liquidity or a desire to move on; the step-up often makes a prompt sale tax-efficient. A DST exchange can fit heirs who want income without management and a clean division. Each choice should be assessed with the family’s CPA, estate attorney, and other advisors.

> For heirs, the question is often not “do we owe tax?”—the step-up can answer that—but “do we want to manage a building together?” A DST can permit a no while preserving an income-producing real-estate interest.

## Coordinating Family Decisions

Family coordination is often the hardest part. Joint heirs need to speak early, ideally with the estate attorney and CPA, about whether the family wants income, liquidity, the asset itself, or a clean division and separate paths. Differing financial needs, timelines, and emotions make this work necessary.

DSTs can give several heirs a workable path. After the sale and exchange, divisible interests can be allocated so an heir who wants income keeps that interest and no one remains locked into joint choices about one physical asset. In some situations, structures and planning approaches can divide proceeds so some heirs exchange into DSTs while others take cash, based on individual tax positions and preferences. Each heir’s share, other income and assets, state of residence, timing, and broader tax situation may differ.

The initial sale normally requires cooperation. Afterwards, fractional interests can make independent paths easier. Communicate early, review the details with the family’s tax and legal professionals, and do not assume every heir has the same tax outcome.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a 1031 exchange for inherited property?

Not necessarily. Section 1014 generally steps the basis up to the date-of-death fair market value, erasing the built-in gain accumulated during the deceased owner’s lifetime. A prompt sale can therefore generate little or no taxable gain, leaving little or no gain to defer through a 1031. A 1031 can become relevant if the property appreciates after the step-up. Heirs may also choose DSTs for passivity, diversification, and divisibility even when tax deferral is not the driver. Confirm your basis and tax situation with your CPA; this is educational, not advice.

### What is the step-up in basis for inherited property?

Section 1014 generally resets inherited property’s basis to fair market value as of the original owner’s death, rather than carrying over the original cost basis. The prior accumulated appreciation is effectively erased for the heir. If the property is sold soon after inheritance, gain is measured from that date-of-death value, often producing little or no taxable gain. This distinguishes inherited property from long-held appreciated property and is why a 1031 may not be needed immediately. Confirm the technical details with your CPA; this is educational, not advice.

### Can heirs exchange inherited property into a DST?

Yes, if it qualifies as investment or business-use real estate. A 1031 can convert the building into fractional interests in DSTs, where the sponsor manages the property. That can deliver passivity, diversification, and clean division among heirs. If there is post-step-up taxable gain, the 1031 can defer it; when the step-up erased the gain, the practical benefits may still matter. DSTs are securities for accredited investors after suitability review. Coordinate the 1031 timing with advisors.

### Why exchange into a DST if the step-up erased the gain?

The reasons can be practical rather than tax-driven. A DST can retain real-estate income without landlording, spread exposure across properties, sectors, and sponsors, and divide fractional interests among heirs. It can turn an unwanted, indivisible, management-heavy property into passive, diversified, divisible income. Heirs could also sell and buy DSTs with the proceeds without a 1031 if tax deferral is not needed. Discuss the appropriate structure with advisors; this is educational, not advice.

### How do DSTs help divide inherited property among heirs?

They replace one indivisible building with fractional beneficial interests. Those interests can be allocated so each heir receives an independent income-producing share, avoids co-managing the property, and is not tied to others’ decisions about the shared asset. Interests across several DSTs can be allocated in proportions the family agrees on. Coordinate the division with an estate attorney; this is educational, not advice.

### What if heirs disagree about what to do with inherited property?

Disagreement is common when joint owners have different needs, timelines, and emotions. One heir may want to keep the property, another to sell, and no one may act unilaterally. An exchange into DSTs followed by allocation of fractional interests can let income-seeking heirs keep an independently held interest while avoiding forced shared management or a sale no one agrees on. The family still needs early conversations with its estate attorney and CPA. This is educational, not advice.

### Is inherited property eligible for a 1031 exchange?

It can generally qualify when it is held for investment or business use, because both relinquished and replacement properties must be held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. A personal residence or property intended for personal use generally does not qualify. Even when the property qualifies, a prompt sale after the step-up may leave little or no gain to defer. Confirm eligibility and tax math with your CPA and attorney; the rules are technical.

### Do all heirs have to agree to do a DST exchange?

Joint owners generally need to coordinate the sale and exchange. Once proceeds are reinvested into fractional DST interests, the interests can be divided and heirs need not remain aligned forever. With attorney guidance, some planning approaches can allow some heirs to exchange into DSTs while others take cash, depending on their tax situations and preferences. Align early with the family’s advisors; this is educational, not advice.

### What are the challenges of inheriting rental property?

Heirs may inherit tenants, repairs, vacancies, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and a property they do not want to manage. They can disagree about keeping or selling, and an indivisible building cannot be divided in a practical way. The combination of management burden, family conflict, immediate responsibilities, and a single asset is why a DST can be considered as a way to create passive, diversified, divisible income.

### Does selling inherited property trigger a big tax bill?

Usually not when sold soon after inheritance, because the Section 1014 step-up measures gain from date-of-death fair market value rather than the original owner’s purchase price. Gain can arise from appreciation after the step-up or if the step-up did not fully reset basis. Estate-level taxes and state rules can also matter. Confirm the specifics with your CPA before selling; this is educational, not advice.

### Can heirs get passive income from inherited real estate?

Yes. Heirs can exchange into DSTs, or buy them with sale proceeds if the step-up removes the tax-deferral need. The sponsor handles tenants, maintenance, and operations; heirs own beneficial interests and receive their share of rental-income distributions. They can diversify across DSTs and divide interests among themselves. Distributions are not guaranteed and can be reduced or suspended; DSTs are illiquid, so retain liquid reserves elsewhere. DSTs are securities for accredited investors after suitability review.

### Should heirs keep, sell, or exchange inherited property?

It depends on the property, the heirs’ goals, and their tax positions. Keeping can fit heirs willing to manage or hire management and accept concentration. Selling can fit a need for liquidity; a prompt sale often produces little or no capital-gains tax because of the step-up. A DST exchange can fit heirs who want income, passivity, diversification, and a clean division. Evaluate all three paths with the estate attorney and CPA; this is educational, not advice.

### Are DSTs only useful for inherited property if there is a taxable gain?

No. Even if the step-up has erased gain, DSTs can provide passive professionally managed real estate exposure, diversification, and fractional interests that divide cleanly among heirs. In many inherited-property situations those practical benefits are the primary reason to use DSTs. Heirs can sell with little or no tax and invest proceeds into DSTs without using the 1031 structure. Consult advisors; this is educational, not advice.

### Does each heir’s tax situation differ with inherited property?

Yes. The effect can vary with each heir’s share, other income and assets, state of residence, choices, timing, and broader tax picture. One heir may take cash, another may pursue DST income, and another may prefer a different approach. The initial sale usually requires coordination, but fractional DST interests can permit separate paths afterward. Each heir should consult a CPA and coordinate with family legal and tax professionals; this is educational, not advice.

## Glossary

**Inherited Property:** Real estate passed to heirs at the owner's death.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The §1014 reset of basis to date-of-death value.

**Section 1014:** The tax-code section providing the step-up at death.

**Date-of-Death Value:** The fair market value used for the stepped-up basis.

**Basis:** The amount from which taxable gain on a sale is measured.

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Fractional Interest:** A divisible DST share that can be split among heirs.

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible real estate.

**Heirs:** Those who inherit the deceased owner's property.

**Co-Ownership:** Joint ownership of an indivisible inherited building.

**Indivisible Asset:** A single property that can't be practically split.

**Passive Income:** Income from real estate the heirs don't manage.

**Diversification:** Spreading proceeds across multiple DSTs and sectors.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a DST fits the accredited heir.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds.

**Estate Planning:** Arranging the transfer of wealth to heirs efficiently.
