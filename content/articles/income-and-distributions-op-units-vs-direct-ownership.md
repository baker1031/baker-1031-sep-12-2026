# Income & Distributions: OP Units vs. Direct Ownership

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about income that looks steady only because the work behind it is being overlooked. A rental check from one property may be substantial, but it reflects tenants, repairs, vacancy, expenses, debt service, and management decisions. After a 721 exchange, income comes as OP unit distributions from a REIT operating partnership instead.

First-order thinking treats that change as a choice between rent and a distribution. Second-order thinking compares control, concentration, fees, tax treatment, the work required, and what happens when income declines. A 721 exchange trades hands-on rental income for passive, diversified distributions. That can be useful, but it is a real change in the ownership experience.

## Two forms of real estate income

With direct ownership, the investor owns property, leases it to tenants, and receives rent after taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities, management, and any debt service. The owner can influence rent, expenses, and vacancy, but the income is tied to one property's performance.

With [OP units](op-units-explained.html), the investor receives passive distributions representing a proportionate interest in the REIT operating partnership's portfolio income. Following a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), those distributions are comparable to REIT dividends. The REIT manages the properties; the unit holder receives the income and has no management work.

The contrast is fundamental: direct rental income is active, concentrated, and controllable; OP-unit income is passive, diversified, and not controlled by the unit holder. The 721 exchange moves the owner from the first form to the second.

## Direct ownership income

Direct-ownership rental income is the rent left after operating costs and, where applicable, debt service. A capable owner can seek higher rents, reduce expenses, minimize vacancies, and select the property-level plan. A well-managed property in a strong location can generate substantial and growing income. The owner keeps the net income after expenses rather than paying REIT-level fees.

There are two important qualifications. First, the work must be done by the owner or paid for through a manager. Second, the income is concentrated. One major tenant leaving, a large repair, or a local downturn can sharply reduce or eliminate income from a single property.

Direct ownership can also benefit from depreciation. Depreciation deductions can shelter some current rental income, improving after-tax yield, although depreciation may be recaptured on sale. Property expenses and financed interest also reduce taxable rental income.

## OP unit distributions

OP unit distributions are fully passive. The REIT manages the properties, receives portfolio income, and makes distributions to unit holders. There are no tenants to find, maintenance problems to handle, or property management tasks for the holder. That can suit an owner who wants to step away from active landlording.

The distribution comes from many properties, not one asset. A vacancy or problem at one property affects only a part of the portfolio, so income is generally smoothed across the holdings. REITs often aim for regular distributions, which can add predictability.

The trade-off is control. The REIT sets distributions according to portfolio income and its distribution policy. The unit holder cannot independently raise rents, reduce property expenses, or optimize the payment. REIT management fees and other expenses are paid from portfolio income before distributions, so the distributions are net of those fees.

## Consistency and predictability

Diversification usually makes OP unit distributions more consistent than rent from one property. A single tenant departure, vacant period, major repair, or local downturn can hit a direct owner's income sharply. A diversified portfolio spreads those exposures across multiple assets.

That distinction can matter greatly to an income-dependent owner, including a retiree. A diversified distribution can be more reliable for recurring needs than a single property's variable rent. But it is not guaranteed. Distribution policy and portfolio income can change, and real estate income can fluctuate or decline with vacancies and market conditions. More stability is not certainty.

## Tax treatment of each

Direct rental income is generally reduced by property expenses, interest when financed, and depreciation. The depreciation shelter can reduce current tax on some or much of the rent, though recapture matters at sale.

OP unit distributions are taxed under partnership rules. They can include ordinary income and return-of-capital components with different treatment. A return-of-capital component, reflecting partnership depreciation, can be non-taxable and reduce the holder's basis. The income is often reported through a Schedule K-1. A CPA should compare the actual after-tax results.

Neither form has a blanket tax advantage on income. Both can reflect real-estate depreciation: direct ownership through depreciation against rent, and OP units through the components of partnership distributions. The mechanisms differ, as do the investor's facts.

## Trade-offs in income

The principal trade-off is control and potential yield against passivity, diversification, and predictability. A skilled owner who wants to keep working a property may prefer controllable rental income. An owner ready to stop managing may value passive distributions more.

Fees are another trade-off. Direct ownership has no REIT-level fees, though it has its own costs and may require a manager. OP distributions are reduced by the REIT's expenses, which are the price of professional management and a diversified portfolio.

The third trade-off is work versus freedom. Managing a property takes time and effort, or money for a manager. A distribution requires nothing from the holder. For some retirees and owners with other priorities, the freedom can outweigh the reduced control. For an active, skilled owner, the ability to optimize income may be the more valuable feature.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does OP unit income differ from rental income?

OP income is a passive distribution from a diversified REIT portfolio, comparable to a REIT dividend and requiring no management work. Rental income is rent from the investor's property, which can be controlled and optimized but is active and concentrated. A 721 exchange changes rental income into OP unit distributions.

### Are OP unit distributions passive?

Yes. The REIT manages the properties and pays distributions to unit holders. The holder does not deal with tenants, maintenance, or management. That is the defining difference from active rental income.

### Is OP unit income more stable than rental income?

Generally, it tends to be more consistent because a diversified portfolio smooths the effect of any one vacancy or property problem. Direct rental income depends on one property's occupancy and local conditions. More consistency is not a guarantee, however.

### Do I lose control of income in a 721 exchange?

To a degree, yes. The REIT determines distributions based on portfolio income and distribution policy. A direct owner can set rents and seek to manage expenses; an OP unit holder trades that control for passive, diversified income.

### Are distributions reduced by fees?

Yes. Management and other REIT expenses come out of portfolio income before distributions. Direct ownership avoids REIT-level fees, but the owner either does the management work or pays a manager. The question is whether professional management and diversification justify the fee drag.

### How are OP unit distributions taxed?

They are taxed under partnership rules and may contain ordinary-income and return-of-capital components. A return-of-capital component reflecting partnership depreciation can be non-taxable and reduce basis. A CPA generally handles the reporting, often through a Schedule K-1.

### Does rental income have tax advantages that OP units do not?

Both can carry depreciation-based tax efficiency. Rental income is sheltered by the property's depreciation; OP distributions can include a depreciation-related return-of-capital component. Neither has a universal advantage, so the comparison should be made after tax for the investor's facts.

### Will income rise or fall after a 721 exchange?

It depends on the property's net rental yield and the REIT's distribution yield, net of fees. A well-managed, high-yielding property may generate more. A lower-yielding or management-heavy property may generate less. Compare the actual net yields with an advisor.

### Which income form is better for retirement?

OP unit distributions often suit retirees who want passive, diversified, more predictable income without management work. A retiree with a well-managed, high-yielding property who is comfortable managing it or using a manager may still prefer direct ownership.

### Can I optimize OP unit income as I can rental income?

Not directly. The REIT controls portfolio decisions and distribution policy. Direct owners can work on rent and expenses. The exchange trades an owner's optimization control for professional portfolio management.

### Which form should I choose?

Choose based on goals. Direct ownership may fit someone who values control and potential yield and will handle the work. OP units may fit someone who values passivity, diversification, and predictability and accepts fees and less control.

### Can OP unit distributions grow over time?

They can grow if the REIT raises rents, improves properties, acquires more assets, and changes its distribution policy. Growth is not guaranteed. Direct rental income can also grow as rents rise, but it depends on the investor's property and management. Both reflect the performance of underlying real estate.

### What happens to income if OP units are converted to shares?

Converted REIT shares would generally pay REIT dividends with economically similar portfolio-linked income. The conversion triggers deferred gain. If the shares are later sold for cash, the real-estate income ends, though the cash can be reinvested elsewhere. Income continues while units or shares are held.

### Is either income guaranteed?

No. OP distributions depend on the REIT's portfolio income and distribution policy; rental income depends on occupancy and property performance. Both can fluctuate or decline. Diversification can reduce single-property exposure but cannot make income certain.

## Glossary

- **OP Unit Distributions:** Passive income from the REIT portfolio paid on OP units.
- **Rental Income:** Rent from a directly owned property, net of expenses.
- **Passive Income:** Hands-off distributions requiring no management.
- **Active Income:** Rental income requiring management to generate and optimize.
- **Diversified Income:** Income from many properties, generally less exposed to one property's rent.
- **Consistency:** The steadiness created when portfolio income is smoothed across properties.
- **Predictability:** The relative reliability of income from a diversified portfolio.
- **Depreciation Shelter:** Depreciation that reduces current tax on rental income.
- **Return of Capital:** A potentially tax-efficient distribution component reflecting depreciation and reducing basis.
- **Schedule K-1:** The tax form commonly reporting OP unit income.
- **REIT Fees:** REIT costs that reduce net distributions.
- **Net Yield:** Income after expenses or fees, compared between the two forms.
- **Control:** The ability to set rents and optimize income, present in direct ownership rather than OP units.
- **Concentration:** Direct ownership's reliance on one property's performance.
- **Distribution Policy:** The REIT's approach to determining distributions.
- **Income Trade-Off:** Control and yield versus passivity, diversification, and predictability.

## OP unit distributions vs. direct ownership income at a glance

| Factor | OP Unit Distributions | Direct Ownership Income |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income source | Distributions from the REIT partnership | Rent from the investor's property |
| Consistency | Diversified portfolio smooths income | Depends on one asset's occupancy |
| Predictability | More predictable across many properties | More variable; vacancies hit harder |
| Tax treatment | Pass-through partnership income | Rental income, sheltered by depreciation |
| Effort | Passive | Active management or a hired manager |
| Control over income | None; set by the partnership | Owner sets rents and decisions |

## Which should you choose?

OP unit distributions can suit investors seeking steadier, more predictable income from a diversified portfolio and a fully passive role. Direct ownership can suit investors who want control over rents and decisions and accept the sharper income effect of one property's vacancy. Compare both net of tax because their treatment differs.
