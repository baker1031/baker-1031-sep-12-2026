# REIT Glossary of Terms

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing REIT terminology create more fog than insight. The terms are mostly practical once they are put in the right groups: earnings, asset value, income, ownership structure, and liquidity.

This glossary explains the terms that matter most—FFO, AFFO, NAV; equity and mortgage REITs; payout ratio and yield; UPREIT and OP units; and non-traded and NAV REITs—in plain language. The goal is to make the jargon useful rather than ornamental.

First-order thinking is to stop at a yield, an FFO headline, or a reported NAV. Second-order thinking connects the terms: earnings to cash available for a distribution, value to price, structure to liquidity, and tax treatment to the investor’s actual goal.

## FFO, AFFO & NAV

FFO, or funds from operations, is the primary REIT earnings measure. It begins with net income, adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization—large non-cash charges that can make property-company net income appear too low—and subtracts property-sale gains that are not recurring operating income. FFO better describes recurring operating earnings than ordinary net income and is the usual starting point for REIT analysis.

AFFO, adjusted funds from operations, refines FFO by subtracting recurring maintenance capital expenditures and normalizing items such as straight-line rent. Since it recognizes spending that FFO does not, AFFO is commonly the better approximation of cash available for distributions and therefore central to distribution sustainability.

NAV, net asset value, is the asset lens. It estimates per-share property and other asset value less liabilities. For a listed REIT, compare market price with NAV to assess a premium or discount; for a non-traded REIT, periodic NAV commonly determines share value and redemption pricing. FFO answers what is earned, AFFO what may be sustainably paid, and NAV what the real estate is estimated to be worth.

## Equity vs. Mortgage REIT

An equity REIT owns and operates income-producing property—apartments, [warehouses](industrial-and-logistics-reits-explained.html), offices, retail centers, [healthcare facilities](healthcare-reits-explained.html), or data centers—and earns rents plus possible appreciation. This is the form most investors picture when they hear REIT.

A mortgage REIT, or mREIT, finances real estate through mortgages and mortgage-backed securities instead of owning buildings. It earns an interest-rate spread between asset income and borrowing cost. That often means higher yields but greater sensitivity to interest rates and higher risk. A hybrid REIT combines ownership and mortgage exposure. The distinction is not semantic: it tells an investor whether the return comes from property operations or financing spreads.

## Payout Ratio & Yield

Dividend yield is annual distribution per share divided by share price. It expresses the [income return on a REIT](income-reits-explained-investing-for-cash-flow.html) at the current price. REITs distribute most taxable income, so yields can exceed broad-stock-market yields. A high yield is not automatically attractive; it can reflect a falling share price, a risky payout, or a distribution that is not fully supported by operations.

The payout ratio measures how much earnings or cash flow is distributed. For a REIT, distribution divided by FFO—and preferably AFFO—is most meaningful. A ratio comfortably below **100%** of AFFO suggests cash coverage; one at or above **100%** warns that the distribution may be unsustainable and can precede a cut. Yield shows income; payout ratio tests durability.

## Key Takeaways

- FFO measures recurring REIT earnings, AFFO approximates distributable cash, and NAV estimates per-share real-estate value net of liabilities.
- Equity REITs own property and earn rent; mREITs finance real estate and earn an interest spread; hybrid REITs do both.
- Dividend yield is the income return, while an AFFO-based payout ratio tests whether the distribution is sustainable.
- UPREIT and OP units enable tax-deferred property contributions, while non-traded and NAV REITs are unlisted vehicles priced periodically at NAV.

## UPREIT & OP Units

An UPREIT, or umbrella partnership REIT, owns property through an operating partnership, or OP, rather than directly. The REIT is the operating partnership’s general partner and the partnership holds the real estate. This structure exists largely to allow tax-deferred property contributions.

OP units are interests in that partnership. A property owner can contribute real estate for OP units instead of cash or REIT shares, generally deferring gain in a **721 exchange**, also called an UPREIT transaction. The OP units can later convert to REIT shares or be redeemed, commonly triggering tax then. See [UPREIT](721-exchange-guide.html) and [OP units](op-units-explained.html) for the mechanics. The owner contributes property, defers the gain, receives distributions, and controls the timing of a later conversion.

## Non-Traded & NAV REIT

A non-traded REIT is SEC-registered but not exchange-listed. It owns or finances real estate under core REIT rules but does not have daily market pricing. It is periodically valued at NAV and generally offers only limited redemption liquidity, often capped around **2%** of NAV per month and **5%** per quarter, and subject to reduction or suspension. It is illiquid and typically available through a broker-dealer to suitable investors.

A NAV REIT is a modern non-traded REIT valued more frequently, often monthly, with offerings and redemptions at NAV and an open-ended life. It was designed to address older non-traded-REIT concerns through more frequent valuation, perpetual life, and often lower or more transparent fees. It remains illiquid compared with a listed REIT, and redemptions can still be capped, gated, or suspended.

## Structure & Tax Terms

The **90% distribution rule** requires a REIT to distribute at least **90%** of taxable income each year to qualify and avoid corporate-level income tax. Other qualification requirements include the **75%** asset and **75%** income tests, at least **100** shareholders, and the **5/50** rule limiting concentrated ownership. These constraints produce REITs’ high pass-through income character.

Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income, not qualified dividends, because the REIT generally did not pay corporate income tax first. Qualified REIT dividends can receive a **20%** Section 199A deduction, made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA. That deduction can reduce the effective top federal rate on eligible ordinary REIT dividends to roughly **29.6%**; it does not apply to capital-gain distributions or return of capital, and individual tax facts still govern. Form 1099-DIV reports ordinary income, capital-gain, and return-of-capital components. [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html) shares are securities, not like-kind real property, so they cannot be used directly in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html); the DST-then-721 path is an indirect bridge, not a direct share purchase.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is FFO?

Funds from operations is the standard recurring-earnings measure: net income plus real-estate depreciation and amortization, less gains on property sales. Depreciation can make net income look low even when properties hold or gain value, while sale gains are not recurring. FFO supports P/FFO, the REIT analogue to P/E, and is the starting point for AFFO, but it does not by itself prove distribution coverage.

### What is AFFO?

Adjusted FFO starts with FFO, deducts recurring maintenance capex, and normalizes straight-line rent. It is closer to cash available after upkeep and is often the preferred tool for evaluating distribution sustainability. Definitions vary by issuer, so read the reconciliation rather than comparing labels alone.

### What is NAV for a REIT?

NAV estimates per-share property and other asset value less debt and other liabilities. Analysts use appraisals or cap rates to estimate property value, add other assets, subtract liabilities, and divide by shares. It is central to non-traded REIT pricing and useful for listed REIT premium or discount analysis, but it depends on assumptions, can lag market conditions, and is not a guaranteed sale value.

### What is the difference between an equity REIT and a mortgage REIT?

Equity REITs own property and earn rents plus possible appreciation. Mortgage REITs finance property through mortgages and securities and earn an interest spread; their leverage and rate sensitivity can be much greater. Hybrid REITs do both.

### What is dividend yield for a REIT?

It is annual distribution per share divided by current share price. A **$2** annual distribution on a **$40** share price is a **5%** yield. Yield can look high because the price fell or a payout is risky, so assess AFFO coverage, debt, and funding rather than chasing it.

### What is a REIT payout ratio?

It is the portion of earnings or cash flow paid as distributions. AFFO-based payout is most useful: comfortably below **100%** suggests coverage; at or above **100%** can indicate a payout exceeding sustainable cash and a risk of reduction.

### What is an UPREIT?

It is a REIT that owns property through an operating partnership, where the REIT serves as general partner. It lets an owner contribute property for OP units and defer gain in a 721 exchange, with tax commonly recognized when units convert or redeem.

### What are OP units?

They are operating-partnership interests received for contributed property. They can defer capital-gains tax under a 721 transaction, provide distributions, and often convert to REIT shares later. Conversion or redemption can trigger tax; consult tax advisers.

### What is a non-traded REIT?

It is an SEC-registered but unlisted REIT. It follows core REIT rules, including the **90%** taxable-income distribution rule, is valued periodically at NAV, and has limited redemption liquidity, commonly capped around **2%** of NAV per month and **5%** per quarter, which the REIT can reduce or suspend. It can have higher upfront fees and valuation uncertainty than a listed vehicle, and is designed for longer-term investors comfortable with illiquidity.

### What is a NAV REIT?

It is a frequently NAV-valued, open-ended modern non-traded REIT. It commonly offers monthly valuation and periodic transactions at NAV, often with lower or more transparent fees than older vehicles. It remains illiquid, and redemptions are limited rather than assured.

### How are most REIT dividends taxed?

Most ordinary dividends are ordinary income, although qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction, potentially reducing the effective top rate to roughly **29.6%**. A 1099-DIV distinguishes ordinary income, capital gains, and return of capital; return of capital reduces cost basis rather than being currently taxed. Tax treatment depends on individual facts and can change.

### Why can't REIT shares be used in a 1031 exchange?

A 1031 exchange requires like-kind real property. A REIT share is a security, not direct real property. An owner can use a DST as qualifying property and later may enter an UPREIT through a 721 exchange, but that is not a direct 1031 into REIT shares.

### What is the 90% distribution rule?

It requires distribution of at least **90%** of taxable income to maintain REIT status and avoid corporate-level tax. Many REITs distribute close to **100%**. The rule supports high yields but does not guarantee any particular payment; it also limits funding growth from retained earnings, so REITs often raise capital through shares or debt.

### What is the Section 199A deduction for REIT dividends?

It is a **20%** deduction for qualified REIT dividends, reducing the effective federal rate on ordinary REIT income. The 2025 OBBBA made it permanent. Eligibility and actual tax results depend on the investor’s facts.

## Glossary

- **FFO:** Net income plus real estate depreciation, minus property gains.
- **AFFO:** FFO minus recurring capex and straight-line rent — distributable cash.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** Per-share value of a REIT's property net of liabilities.
- **Equity REIT:** A REIT that owns property and earns income from rents.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT that finances real estate and earns an interest spread.
- **Hybrid REIT:** A REIT that both owns property and holds mortgage debt.
- **Dividend Yield:** Annual distribution divided by share price — the income return.
- **Payout Ratio:** The share of FFO or AFFO paid out as distributions.
- **UPREIT:** A REIT that owns property through an operating partnership.
- **OP Units:** Operating-partnership interests received for contributed property.
- **721 Exchange:** Contributing property for OP units, deferring capital-gains tax.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** An SEC-registered but unlisted, illiquid, NAV-priced REIT.
- **NAV REIT:** A frequently valued, perpetual-life modern non-traded REIT.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
- **Price-to-FFO (P/FFO):** The REIT valuation multiple analogous to a P/E ratio.
