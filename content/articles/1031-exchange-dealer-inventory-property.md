# 1031 Exchange and Dealer / Inventory Property

I keep seeing investors focus on the property type when the harder question is how that property was held. A parcel of land does not carry a dealer label by itself. The owner’s purpose and activity do.

That distinction can decide whether a sale belongs in a 1031 exchange at all. First-order thinking is that a profitable real-estate sale should receive capital-gains treatment and exchange deferral. Second-order thinking asks whether the facts show an investment held for appreciation or income, or inventory held primarily for sale to customers as part of a business.

## What dealer property is

Dealer property is real estate held primarily for sale to customers in the ordinary course of a trade or business: inventory, much like a retailer’s stock-in-trade. A developer’s subdivided lots, a builder’s spec homes, and properties a flipper buys and quickly resells are familiar examples. They are held to sell at a business profit, not to produce rental income or appreciate over time.

The contrast is purposeful. A dealer buys, perhaps improves, and sells real estate as a business. An investor holds a capital asset for appreciation or income. The same parcel can be dealer inventory for a developer who subdivides and sells it, and investment property for an owner who holds it for appreciation. “Dealer property” is therefore a characterization of a particular owner’s holding and use, not a separate kind of real estate.

## Why it does not qualify

Section 1031 requires property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. Property held primarily for sale is specifically outside that treatment. It is business inventory, not investment property. See the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) for the broader exchange framework.

The cost is twofold. Dealer property cannot receive 1031 deferral, and its sale profit is ordinary business income rather than capital gain, generally taxed at ordinary rates rather than favorable capital-gains rates. The tax code reserves both the exchange framework and capital-gains treatment for investment, not the everyday business of selling real estate.

## The facts that signal dealer status

There is no bright-line test. The IRS and courts consider the total facts and circumstances. The signals include:

- frequent or numerous sales;
- subdividing land or making roads, utilities, and other improvements to make it saleable;
- active marketing and sales efforts;
- [short holding periods](1031-exchange-holding-period-how-long.html);
- a high share of the owner’s income from real-estate sales;
- acquiring property to resell rather than hold; and
- operating a real-estate development or sales business.

The inverse also matters. Infrequent sales, longer passive holds for appreciation, the absence of subdivision and development-for-sale work, and consistent investment treatment all point away from dealer status. None is decisive by itself. The pattern is what matters.

## Holding and documenting genuine investment property

If property may later be exchanged, hold and treat it as a genuine investment. Avoid subdividing or improving it for sale, active resale marketing, frequent turnover, and a quick buy-improve-sell pattern. A reasonable holding period for appreciation or income, combined with conduct that resembles a passive investor’s conduct, strengthens the investment characterization.

Mixed activities deserve special care. Someone who both develops and invests should keep investment holdings distinct from dealer inventory, potentially in different entities. Investments should be treated as passive holdings rather than subdivided or marketed for sale. Intent at acquisition matters, but conduct during the hold matters just as much.

Documentation should reflect the reality. Keep rental agreements where applicable, evidence of appreciation holding, records showing no subdivision or sale-driven improvements, and tax reporting consistent with investment treatment, including rental income, depreciation, and capital-gains treatment. Contemporaneous records carry more weight than statements assembled after a sale. For mixed activity, document the separation between investment holdings and development inventory.

### Key takeaways

- Dealer property is business inventory held for sale; it is ineligible for 1031 treatment and its gain is ordinary income.
- Status turns on the owner’s purpose and activity, assessed on all the facts and circumstances.
- Frequent sales, sale-driven development, marketing, short holds, and a sales business are important warning signs.
- Passive investment holding, clear segregation, and contemporaneous documentation protect the investment case.

## Borderline situations require judgment

The difficult cases are mixed ones: an investor who occasionally develops, or a developer who holds some assets as investments. The issue can be genuinely uncertain, and the consequences of getting it wrong are material: lost deferral plus ordinary-income tax.

This is a place for a tax adviser to assess the facts, the risk, and the structure before relying on exchange treatment. A developer can exchange property genuinely held as a distinct long-term investment; the lots or homes held as development inventory cannot. Building to rent is investment activity, while building spec homes to sell is dealer activity. Similarly, an improvement exchange can be a legitimate 1031 structure when the completed replacement will be held for investment; it is different from developing property for sale.

There is no fixed number of sales and no fixed holding period that removes the risk. A longer hold helps, but a short hold paired with subdivision, improvements, and active selling is a far stronger dealer pattern than the calendar alone. Flipping as a business is classic dealer activity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is dealer property in a 1031 exchange?
Real estate held primarily for sale to customers in the ordinary course of a business is dealer property or inventory. It is held for sale rather than investment, so it does not qualify for a 1031 exchange and its gain is ordinary income. The owner’s purpose and activity—not the property itself—determine the status.

### Why does dealer property not qualify for a 1031?
Section 1031 requires investment or productive-use property and excludes property primarily held for sale. Dealer gain is ordinary income rather than capital gain, so the owner loses both deferral and the favorable investment-property treatment.

### What signals dealer status?
Frequent sales, sale-driven subdivision and improvements, active marketing, short holds, real-estate-sale income, acquisition for resale, and a development or sales business are all signals weighed together.

### How can I avoid dealer classification?
Hold the property as a genuine investment: avoid active resale behavior, sell infrequently, hold for appreciation or income, segregate investments from dealer inventory if you also develop, and document the investment treatment.

### How do I document investment intent?
Keep contemporaneous records of rentals or appreciation holding, holding period, the absence of sale-driven activity, and consistent tax reporting such as rental income, depreciation, and capital-gain treatment. Document the distinction from any development property.

### Is there a fixed holding period or sale count?
No. Longer passive holding supports investment status, but there is no fixed period or number. The full activity pattern governs.

### Can a developer use a 1031 exchange?
Yes, for property genuinely held as a distinct investment and documented as such. Development inventory does not qualify. A tax adviser should help structure mixed activity.

### Does a build-to-rent project or improvement exchange create dealer status?
No, when the property is built or improved to hold for investment. Build-to-sell activity is dealer activity. The intent and subsequent holding distinguish an improvement exchange from development-for-sale.

## Glossary

- **Dealer Property:** Real estate held for sale as business inventory; ineligible for 1031 and taxed as ordinary income.
- **Inventory:** Property held for sale in a business, like a dealer’s stock-in-trade.
- **Dealer Status:** The purpose-and-activity characterization of holding for sale rather than investment.
- **Held for Investment / Held for Sale:** Holding for appreciation or income is qualifying; holding to sell in a business is disqualifying.
- **Facts and Circumstances:** The totality of factors used to distinguish dealer from investor status.
- **Subdivision:** Dividing land into lots for sale, a dealer-status signal.
- **Ordinary Income / Capital Asset:** Dealer-sale income is ordinary income; investment property is a capital asset eligible for capital-gains and 1031 treatment.
- **Flipping:** Buying and quickly reselling property for business profit, a classic dealer activity.
- **Holding Period:** How long property is held; longer periods support investment characterization.
- **Segregation:** Keeping investment property separate from dealer inventory.
- **Investment Intent / Documentation:** Genuine investment purpose, supported by conduct and records of use, holding, and tax reporting.
- **Development-for-Sale:** Improving property to sell, distinguished from investment holding.
- **Improvement Exchange:** A 1031 structure improving replacement property that will be held for investment.
