# REITs and UBIT in Retirement Accounts

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the same concern from retirement-account investors: if real estate produces income and uses leverage, will holding it in an IRA create an unexpected tax bill? It is a sensible question. The answer turns less on the label “real estate” and more on the structure that produces the income.

For ordinary publicly traded REIT shares, the usual worry is generally misplaced. Their distributions are dividends, which are normally passive, excluded income for these rules. A REIT may borrow at the entity level, but that debt does not normally pass through to its shareholders as unrelated debt-financed income. The harder cases tend to be certain private or partnership structures, or real estate the IRA owns directly with debt.

That distinction matters. This is educational information, not tax advice. Confirm the current rules and the treatment of a specific holding with your tax advisor and IRA custodian. Before placing an unlisted vehicle in a retirement account, it also helps to understand the [private and non-traded REIT structures](reits-guide.html) involved.

## What UBIT Is

UBIT is the unrelated business income tax. It can apply when a tax-exempt account, including an IRA, earns income from an active trade or business that is unrelated to the account’s exempt purpose. The policy is straightforward: a tax-exempt account should not be able to operate an active business free of tax simply because it sits inside that account. The tax is calculated on unrelated business taxable income, or UBTI.

Most conventional investment income is excluded from UBTI. Dividends, interest, capital gains, and most rents are generally treated as passive, excluded income. That is why ordinary stock and bond holdings in an IRA ordinarily do not raise a UBIT issue. The tax treatment of [REIT dividends](reit-taxation-how-reit-dividends-are-taxed.html) is more consequential in a taxable account than in this narrow UBIT question.

There is a related concept: UDFI, or unrelated debt-financed income. It can pull otherwise excluded income into UBTI when the account itself holds property that is debt financed. An IRA that buys real estate directly with a mortgage is the usual illustration. These rules are aimed at active-business income and direct account-level leverage, not ordinary passive dividends.

First-order thinking says “income-producing real estate in an IRA must be taxable.” Second-order thinking asks what the IRA actually owns and how the income reaches it. That is where the outcome changes.

## When REITs Trigger UBIT

For the great majority of investors holding publicly traded REIT shares in an IRA, REITs do not trigger UBIT. The distributions are dividends, and dividends are excluded from UBTI. A standard traded-REIT position can therefore produce no UBIT year after year.

The concern usually returns when investors notice that the REIT itself borrows to acquire properties. But the corporate form separates the shareholder from that property-level borrowing. The UDFI rules apply when the tax-exempt account holds debt-financed property directly. An IRA holding REIT stock holds stock in a corporation; the IRA receives a dividend from that corporation, not direct debt-financed income. The REIT’s entity-level leverage stays at the entity level, and the dividend remains excluded.

Specific facts can vary, so verify the current rules with your tax advisor and IRA custodian. But the practical rule is simple: a publicly traded REIT’s dividend is generally excluded, and its own borrowing does not ordinarily flow into the IRA as taxable debt-financed income.

## Traded REITs vs. Private Vehicles

The cleanest UBIT result is usually a public REIT share, though a true non-traded REIT that pays dividends generally has the same basic dividend character. The useful distinction is not merely REIT versus non-REIT. It is excluded dividend income versus directly held debt-financed or partnership-flow-through income.

An IRA that invests in a real estate partnership or an LLC taxed as a partnership can receive a Schedule K-1. That K-1 may report UBTI. If the partnership uses leverage, the debt-financed part of income can flow through as UDFI. Likewise, an IRA that buys real property directly using a mortgage can generate UDFI. Some private funds operating an active business can generate UBTI as well.

The issue is structure, not the mere presence of real estate or debt. A true public or non-traded REIT generally pays an excluded dividend. A partnership, direct mortgage, or other flow-through arrangement can require a different analysis. Confirm what the vehicle actually is before relying on a broad “REIT” label.

## Key Takeaways

- UBIT applies to active-business or directly debt-financed income earned by a tax-exempt account such as an IRA.
- Publicly traded REIT dividends are excluded passive income, so traded REIT shares generally do not trigger UBIT in an IRA.
- A REIT’s entity-level leverage does not normally pass through to shareholders as UDFI; the corporate structure keeps the dividend excluded.
- The more meaningful UBIT and UDFI risks sit in certain private or partnership vehicles and directly debt-financed IRA investments, not ordinary traded REITs.

## Avoiding UBIT Surprises

The best safeguard is to understand what the IRA is actually holding. With ordinary publicly traded REIT shares and REIT funds, the dividends are generally excluded and UBIT is not the main issue. The larger placement question often concerns dividend taxation across [taxable and tax-advantaged accounts](reits-in-taxable-vs-tax-advantaged-accounts.html).

The situations that deserve more scrutiny include a [self-directed IRA invested in a real estate partnership or LLC](investing-in-reits-with-a-self-directed-ira.html), a private fund using leverage and reporting K-1 income, and directly owned property financed with a mortgage. In those cases, UBTI or UDFI can arise. If an IRA’s UBTI exceeds the annual threshold, the IRA may owe UBIT and may need to file Form 990-T.

Before buying a private or leveraged vehicle inside a self-directed IRA, ask a few direct questions. Will the account receive excluded dividends or partnership flow-through income? Does the vehicle use leverage in a way that creates UDFI for the IRA? Will a Schedule K-1 arrive, and could it report UBTI? A custodian and CPA can help answer those questions before tax time.

The surprise is usually not the ordinary traded REIT. It is the self-directed detour into a leveraged private fund, partnership, or directly mortgaged property, where account-level debt or flow-through income can quietly alter the tax result.

## Coordinating With Your Custodian

Your IRA custodian and CPA are the appropriate parties to confirm UBIT treatment. For a public REIT in a standard brokerage IRA, little coordination is usually required: dividends are excluded and the brokerage handles routine reporting. A self-directed IRA holding private real estate vehicles is different. The custodian may help explain alternative-asset paperwork, whether a K-1 is expected, whether UBTI may be reported, and whether Form 990-T could be required.

When UBIT applies, the IRA itself pays it from account funds; it is not paid by you personally. That makes the question economic as well as procedural. Catching possible UBTI or UDFI before investing lets you decide whether the after-UBIT return still makes sense and permits correct filing if the investment proceeds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is UBIT?

UBIT is the unrelated business income tax that can apply to a tax-exempt account such as an IRA when it earns active-business income unrelated to its exempt purpose. It is calculated on UBTI. Dividends, interest, capital gains, and most rents are generally passive excluded income, so conventional stock-and-bond IRA holdings do not normally create UBIT. UDFI can pull income from directly debt-financed account property into UBTI. UBIT targets active-business and direct-leverage income, not ordinary passive dividends. This is educational information, not tax advice; confirm with your tax advisor.

### Do REITs trigger UBIT in an IRA?

Generally, no. Publicly traded REIT distributions are dividends, which are excluded passive income for UBTI purposes. The REIT’s own borrowing does not normally change that result because the shareholder owns stock in a corporation rather than debt-financed property directly. An ordinary traded REIT in an IRA is therefore generally UBIT-safe. Certain private or leveraged vehicles require a separate review with your tax advisor.

### Why doesn't a REIT's leverage create UBIT for shareholders?

UDFI rules generally apply when the IRA itself owns debt-financed property, such as real estate bought with a mortgage. A REIT shareholder owns stock in the REIT, not the underlying property. The REIT’s entity-level borrowing does not pass through to the shareholder as UDFI, and the shareholder receives an excluded dividend. That corporate separation is why public REITs can be a UBIT-friendly way to gain leveraged real estate exposure in an IRA. Confirm current rules with your tax advisor.

### What is UDFI?

UDFI means unrelated debt-financed income. It can make otherwise excluded income count as UBTI when a tax-exempt account earns income or gain from property acquired or held with account-level debt. For example, a self-directed IRA that buys real estate with a mortgage can have the debt-financed portion of income and gain pulled into UBTI, potentially creating UBIT. Public REIT leverage is at the REIT entity level, not the IRA level, so a public REIT dividend does not ordinarily create shareholder UDFI. This is educational information, not tax advice.

### Are non-traded REITs subject to UBIT?

Generally, a true non-traded REIT that pays dividends has the same broad result as a public REIT: dividend income is excluded and normally does not generate UBTI. The key is the character of income, not whether the REIT is exchange listed. Entity-level REIT leverage does not ordinarily pass to shareholders as UDFI. Still, confirm the specific structure. Some private real estate offerings are partnerships rather than REITs and can have different UBIT consequences. Ask your tax advisor and custodian.

### When can real estate in an IRA trigger UBIT?

The usual triggers involve direct leverage or partnership flow-through rather than ordinary REIT shares. Directly owned property financed by an IRA mortgage can create UDFI on the debt-financed part of income and gain. A partnership or LLC taxed as a partnership can report UBTI on a Schedule K-1, and partnership leverage can produce UDFI. A private fund operating an active business can also generate UBTI. By contrast, ordinary public and non-traded REIT dividends are generally excluded. Verify the current rules with your tax advisor.

### What is the difference between a REIT and a real estate partnership for UBIT?

A REIT is a corporation. Its distribution is generally a dividend, an excluded passive-income category, and its entity-level leverage does not normally pass through to the IRA. A real estate partnership or an LLC taxed as a partnership is a flow-through entity. It can pass income character and debt financing to partners on a Schedule K-1, including UBTI or UDFI. That is why the corporate REIT form is generally UBIT-friendly while a partnership can expose an IRA to UBIT. This is educational information, not tax advice.

### Do I need to file anything for UBIT on my REITs?

For ordinary publicly traded REIT shares in an IRA, generally no: the dividends are excluded, no UBTI is generated, and routine brokerage reporting applies. A filing may arise only if the IRA generates UBTI above the annual threshold, such as from a leveraged private fund, a partnership K-1 reporting UBTI, or directly debt-financed property. The IRA may then file Form 990-T and pay any UBIT from its own funds, usually with custodian and CPA coordination.

### Is a self-directed IRA more likely to have UBIT issues with real estate?

It can be, not because the self-directed IRA is inherently different, but because it can own alternative assets. A standard brokerage IRA usually owns traded securities, including public REITs with excluded dividends. A self-directed IRA can own directly held real estate, private funds, partnerships, and LLCs—the kinds of structures that can create UBTI or UDFI. A self-directed IRA holding only public REITs has no more UBIT exposure than a brokerage IRA holding the same shares. Coordinate with the custodian and CPA.

### How do I avoid UBIT surprises in my IRA?

Know the holding and how its income is characterized before investing. Public REIT shares and REIT funds generally produce excluded dividends. For a partnership or LLC, a leveraged private fund, or mortgaged direct property in a self-directed IRA, ask whether income is a dividend or flow-through income, whether leverage creates UDFI, and whether a K-1 could report UBTI. Your custodian and CPA can answer before the investment and before tax time. Confirm current tax rules with your advisor.

### Does UBIT defeat the purpose of holding REITs in an IRA?

For ordinary traded REITs, generally no. REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, which can be tax-inefficient in a taxable account, and an IRA can shelter that annual income without a UBIT offset because the dividends are excluded from UBTI. UBIT can reduce the after-tax return only when an IRA holds a structure that produces UBTI or UDFI, such as a leveraged private real estate fund or directly mortgaged property. That difference is why vehicle selection matters.

### Who pays the UBIT if my IRA owes it?

The IRA pays UBIT from its own funds, not you personally. The IRA’s UBTI above the annual threshold is taxed and Form 990-T is filed for the IRA. That is different from the normal traditional-IRA sequence, where tax is generally paid personally when distributions are taken. UBIT taxes certain income inside the account currently, reducing account value and tax-advantaged growth. Custodian and CPA coordination is customary. Ordinary traded REIT dividends do not create this result.

### Are REITs a tax-efficient way to hold real estate in an IRA?

Publicly traded REITs can be a tax-efficient way to obtain diversified income-producing real estate exposure inside an IRA because their distributions are excluded dividends for UBTI purposes and entity-level leverage does not normally create shareholder UDFI. Directly mortgaged property or a leveraged real estate partnership can create UBTI or UDFI and trigger a Form 990-T. That difference is part of the appeal of REITs in retirement accounts. Confirm the specifics of every holding with your custodian and CPA.

## Glossary

**UBIT:** Unrelated business income tax; tax on certain income in a tax-exempt account.

**UBTI:** Unrelated business taxable income; the income on which UBIT is calculated.

**UDFI:** Unrelated debt-financed income; income from account-level leverage that can be pulled into UBTI.

**Excluded Income:** Passive income, including dividends, interest, and gains, that does not generate UBTI.

**Tax-Exempt Account:** An account such as an IRA that is generally not taxed currently.

**Publicly Traded REIT:** An exchange-listed REIT whose dividends are generally excluded from UBTI.

**Non-Traded REIT:** An unlisted REIT whose dividends are also generally excluded from UBTI.

**Entity-Level Leverage:** Debt used by the REIT itself that does not normally pass through to shareholders.

**Self-Directed IRA:** An IRA able to hold alternative assets such as private real estate.

**Real Estate Partnership:** A flow-through vehicle that can pass UBTI or UDFI to an IRA.

**Schedule K-1:** The form reporting partnership flow-through income, which can include UBTI.

**Form 990-T:** The return an IRA files to report and pay UBIT.

**Dividend:** A REIT distribution treated as excluded income for UBIT purposes.

**Custodian:** The institution that holds and administers IRA assets.

**Passive Income:** Investment income excluded from UBTI, unlike active-business income.

**Debt-Financed Property:** Property acquired with debt that can generate UDFI in an IRA.
