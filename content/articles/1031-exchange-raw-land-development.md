# 1031 Exchange for Raw Land Development

I keep seeing raw land described as a simple 1031 asset because it is real property and can appreciate for years without much activity. The important word is “can.” The same parcel may be qualifying investment property in one owner's hands and nonqualifying dealer inventory in another's.

First-order thinking is, “It is land, so it is like-kind.” Second-order thinking asks why it was held, what has been done to it, how often it has been sold, and whether the facts look like an investment or a business inventory cycle. The land itself does not answer those questions. The owner's purpose and activity do.

## Investment land vs. development inventory

[Raw land](1031-exchange-for-land-and-vacant-lots.html) held for investment can qualify for a 1031 exchange. Investment land is held for appreciation or income: for example, an investor buys it expecting its value to rise or holds inherited land without operating an active business of developing and selling it. As a capital asset held for investment, it can be exchanged or sold after appreciation.

Development inventory is different. A developer may buy land, subdivide it, add roads or utilities, and sell lots to customers in the ordinary course of business. That land is inventory, not a capital asset. It is dealer property, generally taxed as ordinary income when sold, and it does not qualify for a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) because it is held for sale rather than investment or productive use.

The same parcel can sit on either side of the line. It is investment property for an owner holding it for appreciation and development inventory for an owner subdividing it for sale. That held-for-investment distinction is the first question in every land exchange.

## The held-for-investment test

The test is facts and circumstances. The issue is whether the land is held primarily for investment or productive use, or primarily for sale to customers in the ordinary course of business. There is no single deciding fact; the IRS and courts consider the owner's actual conduct.

Factors that support investment treatment include passive holding for appreciation, a longer holding period, no subdivision or development work, infrequent sales, and treatment of the land as an investment. Factors that point toward dealer status include subdivision, roads or utilities added to make lots saleable, active marketing, frequent sales, and a development or land-sales business.

The substance of activity controls over stated intent. A passive investor who sells rarely is very different from a developer subdividing and marketing lots. The difficult cases live between those extremes, and they should be reviewed with a tax adviser before a sale creates a problem.

> The same parcel can be investment property or dealer inventory depending on how it is held. Subdivide, improve, and actively sell, and it looks like inventory; hold for appreciation, and it qualifies.

## Dealer-status risk

Dealer status is the central land-exchange risk. If land thought to be investment property is characterized as dealer inventory, the exchange can be disallowed because the relinquished property did not qualify. The gain may then be taxed at higher ordinary-income rates rather than capital-gains rates. The owner loses both deferral and favorable capital treatment.

The risk is clearest where an owner buys, subdivides, improves, and sells lots. It can also arise where an investor's actions blur the line through some subdivision, improvement, or frequent sales. The relevant question is the activity, not whether the owner considers himself or herself an investor.

An investor who wants investment treatment should avoid frequent quick sales, subdivision and improvements aimed at resale, and active lot marketing. Passive holding, infrequent sales, and investment treatment support the position. An owner who both invests and develops may be better served by segregating investment land from development inventory—potentially holding it separately—and confirming characterization with a tax adviser.

## Exchanging land for income property

A common use of a land exchange is moving appreciated, non-income-producing investment land into income-producing real estate. Raw land can tie up capital and create carrying costs such as property taxes and sometimes financing, while producing no current income. A qualifying exchange can move that value into rentals, commercial property, or passive DSTs while deferring qualifying gain.

The economic change is straightforward: a static, cash-consuming holding becomes a productive asset that can generate rental income. For investors whose wealth is concentrated in appreciated idle land, that can be a meaningful repositioning. DSTs can be especially convenient because they offer a turnkey route to passive, diversified, income-producing real estate without sourcing and managing a direct building.

The gating requirement remains the same. Investment land can be exchanged into income property; dealer inventory cannot. The tax benefit does not cure an investment-status problem, so confirm qualification before treating the land-to-income plan as available.

## Planning considerations

Start with characterization. With a tax adviser, assess the way the land has been held and used against the dealer-status factors. If the facts are borderline, obtain advice about the risk before selling. Dealer land cannot be rescued through later exchange paperwork.

If the land qualifies, follow the ordinary process: engage a [qualified intermediary](insights.html) before sale, identify replacement property in writing within 45 days with a backup, close within 180 days, and reinvest into equal-or-greater-value replacement property. The replacement can be more investment land, income property, or a passive DST, depending on the owner's goal.

For a landowner near the dealer line, advance planning can strengthen the investment story: hold passively, avoid development-for-sale activity, separate any inventory from the investment land, and document the investment purpose. Resolve the eligibility question before the sale, not under exchange-clock pressure afterward.

## Key Takeaways

- Raw land held for investment can qualify for 1031; land held as development inventory generally does not.
- The facts-and-circumstances test focuses on holding purpose and activity; subdivision, improvements, marketing, and frequent sales can signal dealer status.
- Dealer status can eliminate deferral and produce ordinary-income tax.
- A qualifying landowner can exchange appreciated idle land into direct income property or DSTs, but qualification comes first.

## Improvement exchanges and land

Developing land for sale and improving replacement land for investment are not the same. A developer holding land as inventory for sale generally has dealer property. An improvement, or construction, exchange uses exchange funds to build or improve a replacement property inside the 180-day period. That can be a legitimate 1031 structure.

For example, an investor can acquire raw land or another property to improve, use exchange funds for construction, and have the completed, paid-for improvements count toward the replacement property's value. The resulting property must be held for investment, not sold as inventory.

The distinction is critical: building for post-exchange investment through a properly structured improvement exchange is different from developing for sale. Structure and post-exchange use matter, so an investor considering construction should work with experienced counsel and an advisor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does raw land qualify for a 1031 exchange?
Yes, when held for investment, appreciation, or income. Land held primarily for development and customer sale as dealer inventory generally does not qualify. The same parcel can differ based on use.

### What's the difference between investment land and development inventory?
Investment land is a capital asset held passively for appreciation or income. Development inventory is land held for sale to customers by a developer, is ordinary-income dealer property, and does not qualify.

### What is the held-for-investment test for land?
It is the facts-and-circumstances determination of investment purpose versus sale purpose. Passive, long, non-subdivided holding and infrequent sales support investment; subdivision, improvements for sale, marketing, and frequent sales support dealer status.

### What is dealer-status risk?
It is the risk that purported investment land is characterized as dealer inventory, disallowing 1031 deferral and taxing the gain at ordinary-income rates. It is especially high for development or frequent sales.

### How do I avoid dealer status on my land?
Avoid frequent quick sales, sale-focused subdivision or improvements, and active marketing. Hold passively for appreciation, sell infrequently, separate investing from development activity, and consult a tax adviser.

### Can I exchange raw land for income property?
Yes, if the land qualifies as investment property. A 1031 can convert appreciated idle land into rentals, commercial real estate, or DSTs that produce income while deferring qualifying gain.

### Does developing land disqualify it from 1031?
Developing land held as inventory for sale generally makes it dealer property. Building on replacement land in an improvement exchange for post-exchange investment is a distinct, legitimate structure.

### What is an improvement exchange for land?
It is a structure using exchange funds to build or improve replacement property, potentially raw land, within 180 days. The improved property is held for investment, not as inventory, and requires careful structuring.

### Can the same land be investment or dealer property?
Yes. Character depends on the owner's purpose and conduct. An investor holding for appreciation and a developer subdividing for sale can hold the same parcel with different tax character, so mixed operations need separation and care.

### How is dealer land taxed?
Dealer land is generally ordinary income when sold, at higher rates than capital gain, and does not receive 1031 deferral. That is the double disadvantage compared with qualifying investment land.

### What's the first step in a raw land exchange?
Confirm investment status with a tax adviser. Review holding and use against dealer factors before engaging the QI and beginning the exchange process.

### Should I get tax advice for a land exchange?
Yes, especially near the dealer line. The test is fact-specific, dealer consequences are substantial, and improvement-exchange structure needs technical guidance.

### How long should I hold raw land before exchanging it?
There is no fixed minimum. A longer passive holding period supports investment treatment; a short holding combined with subdivision and sales activity points toward dealer status.

### Can I subdivide land a little and still qualify?
It is risky. Even limited subdivision combined with improvements and active sales can shift characterization. A tax adviser should assess the full facts.

### Is exchanging idle land into a DST a good move?
For a qualifying land investor, it can convert a cash-consuming, non-income asset into passive, diversified income property in one turnkey step. The DST trade-offs and the land's investment status both still matter.

### What if I inherited raw land and want to exchange it?
Inherited land receives a stepped-up basis, so a prompt sale may have little gain and little need for a 1031. It can qualify if investment-held; later appreciation or a desire to move into income property may make an exchange useful.

### Can I exchange land into a building and vice versa?
Yes. Raw investment land is like-kind to improved investment real estate. Land can be exchanged for a building or a building for land if both properties are held for investment.

## Glossary

- **Raw Land:** Undeveloped land; eligible when investment-held, not dealer inventory.
- **Investment Land:** Land held for appreciation or income as a capital asset.
- **Development Inventory:** Customer-sale business land that does not qualify.
- **Dealer Property:** Property held primarily for customer sale, producing ordinary income and ineligible for 1031.
- **Held-for-Investment Test:** Facts-and-circumstances test of investment versus sale purpose.
- **Dealer Status:** Characterization as holding land for sale rather than investment.
- **Subdivision:** Dividing land into lots for sale, a dealer-status signal.
- **Capital Asset:** Investment property eligible for capital-gains and 1031 treatment unlike inventory.
- **Ordinary Income:** Income taxed at regular rates, which applies to dealer land sales.
- **Improvement Exchange:** 1031 structure to build or improve investment replacement property.
- **Held for Productive Use:** A Section 1031 qualifying purpose alongside investment.
- **Income Property:** Real estate that generates rental income, a common land replacement.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** Passive income-producing replacement for a qualifying land exchange.
- **Facts and Circumstances:** The total holding and use pattern determining investor versus dealer status.
- **Carrying Costs:** Costs such as property taxes on idle land.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** Independent party that holds proceeds so the seller avoids constructive receipt.
