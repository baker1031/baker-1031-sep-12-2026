# The Role of Your Attorney in a 1031 Exchange

I keep noticing how often the word “attorney” arrives late in an exchange conversation. The sale is moving, the identification clock has started, and someone suddenly asks whether the ownership on the old property matches the proposed ownership on the new one. That is usually the wrong moment to discover a structural problem.

The first-order question is whether an attorney is required. A straightforward, single-owner exchange of one investment property for another may run well with a qualified intermediary and CPA. The second-order question is whether an ownership, partner, contract, or securities issue is sitting beneath an otherwise ordinary transaction. When it is, legal structure—not exchange paperwork—is the real risk.

## When you need an attorney

An attorney is not required for every 1031 exchange. In a straightforward exchange—one property for another, one owner, no entity complication—a qualified intermediary (QI) can handle the mechanics and standard exchange documents, while a CPA can handle tax modeling and reporting. Separate legal counsel may not add much to that simple fact pattern.

The threshold changes when legal structure begins to matter. Entity and title issues can affect the same-taxpayer rule. Partnership owners may have different goals, with some seeking tax deferral and others wanting cash. A reverse, improvement, or related-party exchange may require special legal arrangements. Purchase and sale contracts may be non-standard. And an exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) creates securities documents and investor-rights questions, even though the sponsor and broker-dealer handle much of that work.

The decision is therefore about complexity, not ceremony. Legal fees can be small relative to the cost of a legally flawed exchange, including a disqualification. When the structure is uncertain, consult an attorney early enough to change it. The right objective is not to involve more professionals than needed; it is to bring the right expertise to the part of the transaction that can actually fail.

## Entity and title structuring

The same-taxpayer rule is a central legal issue. The taxpayer that sells the relinquished property must be the taxpayer that acquires the replacement property. That sounds simple until the property is held in an LLC, partnership, trust, or by multiple owners.

An attorney examines the existing title, the taxpayer behind it, and how replacement property should be titled. The question may be whether the replacement should remain in the same entity, whether a change in ownership is contemplated, or whether the entity is treated as its owner for tax purposes. A single-member LLC is typically disregarded for tax, meaning the member is the taxpayer. A multi-member LLC or partnership is generally a separate taxpayer, which can change the structure the exchange needs.

The point is not merely formal. A same-taxpayer-rule violation can disqualify the exchange. Careful entity and title structuring lets the actual taxpayer sell and acquire in a consistent way, particularly where ownership is shared or layered through an entity.

> The attorney helps ensure that the same taxpayer sells and buys, navigating entity and title issues that can otherwise disqualify the exchange.

## Reviewing exchange documents

The QI supplies the standard exchange agreement, assignments, and identification forms. An attorney can review those documents, as well as purchase and sale agreements, to confirm that the documents are legally sound, that exchange cooperation language is appropriate, and that the investor understands the QI's obligations and the investor's rights.

Document review is especially useful for non-standard, high-value, or complex transactions. It can cover the purchase and sale contracts, the exchange agreement with the QI, and documents associated with a reverse exchange, improvement exchange, entity arrangement, or other special structure. The attorney's job is to see whether the papers fit the client's interests and the planned legal structure—not to replace the QI's mechanics or the CPA's tax work.

For a routine exchange with a reputable QI, standard documents may be sufficient. The more unusual the contract or the greater the stakes, the more valuable it is to have legal eyes on what the standard form does not address.

## Drop-and-swap and partnership issues

Partnership exchanges are among the clearest cases for an attorney. A partnership or multi-member LLC, rather than its individual partners, is usually the taxpayer. If the partnership owns the property, it ordinarily must exchange as a whole. That does not work cleanly when one partner wants to defer gain and another wants to cash out and pay tax.

A **drop-and-swap** can address that mismatch. Before the exchange, the partnership distributes the property to the individual partners, often as tenants-in-common (TIC) interests. Each owner can then choose to exchange or cash out. A **swap-and-drop** reverses the order: the partnership exchanges first and distributes afterward.

Neither label makes the analysis routine. Timing of the distribution, the held-for-investment requirement, and same-taxpayer considerations carry legal and tax consequences. The IRS can scrutinize a partnership that drops property to partners just before a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), asking whether the partners, rather than the partnership, truly held the property for investment. An attorney structures the transaction and coordinates with the CPA on the tax effects. The QI handles exchange mechanics; it does not replace legal advice on a partnership restructuring.

**Key takeaways:**

- An attorney is often unnecessary for a simple exchange but valuable for entity issues, partnerships, complex transactions, and document review.
- Entity and title structuring helps preserve the same-taxpayer rule, especially for entities and multiple owners.
- Drop-and-swap and swap-and-drop structures can let partners with different objectives exchange or cash out individually, but they require legal and tax analysis.
- The attorney can review documents and coordinate with the QI, CPA, and securities professionals on legal issues.

## DST and securities documentation

An exchange into a DST or another securities replacement has a different allocation of responsibilities. The DST is a securities investment. The sponsor and broker-dealer provide the offering documents, including the [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html) (PPM) and subscription documents, and the broker-dealer conducts the suitability process.

An attorney can review those documents for an investor who wants legal counsel on the DST investment or has questions about the legal structure and investor rights. The attorney's role is typically lighter than in a direct-property transaction because the DST is pre-packaged and much of the structuring and documentation is handled by the sponsor and broker-dealer. Lighter does not mean irrelevant. Optional legal review remains available where the investor's facts warrant it.

This is another place where first-order thinking can mislead. A pre-packaged investment can reduce direct-property structuring work, but it does not erase the need to understand the offering documents, risks, illiquidity, and the investor's rights. The PPM controls the terms of the offering.

## Coordinating the professional team

An attorney works with, rather than in place of, the rest of the exchange team. The QI handles the exchange process and documents. The CPA addresses the tax consequences, including modeling boot, basis, and Form 8824. The attorney handles legal structuring, title, entity, contracts, and special arrangements. An agent or securities professional handles the transaction side.

Coordination matters when those roles overlap. A drop-and-swap may require the attorney to structure ownership, the CPA to analyze tax treatment, and the QI to execute the exchange mechanics. An attorney can also coordinate with [the CPA who handles the exchange's tax side](role-of-your-cpa-in-a-1031-exchange.html) and the QI so exchange documents support, rather than contradict, the legal arrangement.

The goal is not a committee. It is clear responsibility: legal, tax, mechanics, and transaction execution should be aligned before the deadlines force a choice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need an attorney for a 1031 exchange?

Not always. A straightforward, single-owner exchange of one property for another, using a QI and CPA, often does not need separate counsel. Entity and title issues, partnerships, reverse or improvement exchanges, related-party exchanges, and non-standard documents are situations where an attorney is valuable. When uncertain, consult an attorney about whether legal structuring is needed.

### When is an attorney necessary?

An attorney is often appropriate for entity and title questions that affect the same-taxpayer rule; partnerships with different partner goals; reverse, improvement, and related-party transactions; non-standard contracts; document review; and legal structuring generally. A simple exchange may not need counsel, while a complex exchange benefits from it.

### What does the attorney do with entity and title issues?

The attorney helps make sure the taxpayer that sells the relinquished property is the same taxpayer that acquires the replacement property. Property in an LLC, partnership, trust, or multiple-owner arrangement may need careful title and entity analysis, including the difference between a disregarded entity and a separate taxpayer. A violation can disqualify the exchange.

### What is a drop-and-swap?

It is a partnership structure in which property is dropped from the partnership to individual partners, often as TIC interests, before the exchange. Each partner can then exchange or cash out according to that partner's goal. A swap-and-drop does the reverse. These structures create legal and tax issues that an attorney and CPA should address.

### Why do partnerships need special handling in exchanges?

The partnership is the taxpayer, so it would ordinarily exchange as a whole. That does not accommodate partners with different goals. A drop-and-swap or swap-and-drop can restructure the ownership, but timing, held-for-investment, and same-taxpayer issues make partnership exchanges attorney territory.

### Does the attorney review my exchange documents?

Yes. An attorney can review purchase and sale contracts for exchange language and protection, the QI exchange agreement for rights and obligations, and non-standard documents for complex structures. A simple exchange may rely on the QI's standard documents, but complex or high-value deals can benefit from legal review.

### What's the attorney's role with DSTs?

It is usually lighter than with direct-property structuring because the sponsor and broker-dealer handle the pre-packaged DST structure, documents, and suitability process. An investor may still engage counsel to review the PPM and subscription documents or to answer legal questions about the investment.

### How does the attorney coordinate with my other advisors?

The attorney coordinates with the QI on exchange documents and structure, with the CPA on the tax consequences of legal arrangements such as a drop-and-swap, and with the agent or securities professional on the transaction. This helps align legal, tax, mechanical, and transaction work.

### Is an attorney expensive relative to the benefit?

For a complex exchange, legal cost is usually small relative to the risk of a flawed structure, disqualified deferral, or other legal problem. For a simple exchange that does not need an attorney, the cost can be avoided. The appropriate level of involvement follows the complexity.

### Can the attorney handle the tax aspects too?

Some attorneys, particularly tax attorneys, can address both legal and tax matters. More commonly, the CPA handles tax calculations, boot, basis, and Form 8824, while the attorney handles entity, title, document, and drop-and-swap structuring. A tax attorney may bridge the two roles, but CPA-attorney coordination is common.

### Should I involve the attorney early?

For a complex exchange, yes. Entity, title, and partnership arrangements often must be structured before the exchange starts. Early involvement is particularly important for the timing of a drop-and-swap. A simple exchange may not require it.

### Why is the timing of a drop-and-swap legally sensitive?

The IRS may examine whether the party conducting the exchange held the property for investment. If a partnership distributes property to partners immediately before an exchange, the IRS may question whether the partners had the required holding purpose. Timing affects that analysis, so the attorney structures it to manage legal risk.

### Can an attorney help with a reverse or improvement exchange?

Yes. Reverse exchanges acquire replacement property before the relinquished property is sold, and improvement exchanges use exchange funds to improve replacement property. These advanced arrangements often involve an Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT) and specific legal documents. An attorney can coordinate with the QI and CPA on the structure.

### Does my attorney coordinate with my QI on documents?

Yes. The [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) provides the standard exchange agreement, assignment, and identification documents. The attorney can review them and make sure they align with a non-standard entity, drop-and-swap, reverse-exchange, or similar arrangement. The QI handles mechanics; the attorney handles legal structuring and review.

### Is a tax attorney different from a CPA for an exchange?

A CPA generally focuses on tax calculations and reporting. A tax attorney focuses on legal tax structuring, legal advice, and representation. Many exchanges use a CPA and, when needed, a real-estate or tax attorney for the legal side. A tax attorney can be particularly useful in contested related-party or partnership matters.

## Glossary

- **Attorney:** Legal counsel for complex exchange entity, title, document, and structuring issues.
- **Same-Taxpayer Rule:** The requirement that the same taxpayer sells and buys, maintained through proper entity and title structuring.
- **Entity Structuring:** Arranging LLC, partnership, trust, or other ownership so the exchange meets its taxpayer requirements.
- **Title:** The way property ownership is held, which can affect the exchange structure.
- **Drop-and-Swap:** Distribution of partnership property to partners before an exchange so each may exchange or cash out.
- **Swap-and-Drop:** Exchanging first and distributing afterward; the reverse sequence of a drop-and-swap.
- **Partnership Exchange:** An exchange involving partnership-held property, which is complex when partners have different objectives.
- **Tenants-in-Common (TIC):** A co-ownership form that may be used when property is distributed to partners in a drop-and-swap.
- **Document Review:** An attorney's review of contracts and exchange documents for legal soundness and client protection.
- **Disregarded Entity:** An entity, such as a single-member LLC, generally treated as its owner for tax purposes and relevant to titling.
- **Held-for-Investment Requirement:** The holding standard examined in drop-and-swap timing analysis.
- **Private Placement Memorandum (PPM):** The DST offering document that describes terms and risks and may be reviewed by an attorney.
- **Subscription Documents:** DST purchase documents handled by the sponsor and broker-dealer, with optional legal review.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that handles exchange mechanics and coordinates documents with the attorney as needed.
- **Reverse Exchange:** A complex exchange in which the replacement is acquired first and that may need legal help.
- **Related-Party Exchange:** An exchange involving related parties and legal nuances that an attorney can address.
