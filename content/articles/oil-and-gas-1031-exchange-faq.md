# Oil & Gas 1031 Exchange FAQ: 25 Questions Answered

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Mineral & Royalty · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read**

I have been thinking about how a mineral sale can look straightforward until someone asks what, exactly, is being sold. A royalty, a mineral interest, an override, a production payment, and a working interest can all be described casually as “oil and gas.” They do not necessarily receive the same tax treatment.

This reference brings the recurring questions into one place: eligibility, deadlines, DSTs, boot, depletion, working interests, and ORRIs. It is designed to help an owner find the right question quickly and then take the actual documents to a CPA, attorney, and adviser. Oil-and-gas 1031 analysis is genuinely fact-dependent. The recurring tests are simple to state but not to skip: Is the right a perpetual real-property interest? Was the exchange set up before the sale? Were proceeds fully reinvested in qualifying replacement property?

First-order thinking treats every “mineral right” as like-kind real estate and treats a large estimated tax bill as a reason to rush. Second-order thinking begins with the conveyance, duration, valuation, taxpayer, timing, and liquidity of the replacement. Those details decide whether a plan is resilient or merely sounds familiar.

For a longer discussion, see our [complete guide to mineral rights and royalty 1031 exchanges](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html).

## Eligibility basics

The first question is whether the specific interest is real property held for investment. In most producing states, minerals and the rights to them are real property, and the IRS generally follows that characterization. Most fee mineral interests and perpetual royalties therefore qualify. The important exceptions are interests treated as something other than continuing real property: production payments, which IRC Section 636 treats as loans; many fixed-term interests; and the tangible equipment in a working interest, which is personal property and has not qualified since the 2017 tax law.

The label does not settle the issue. “Royalty” and “mineral rights” can describe both qualifying and nonqualifying interests. Nature and duration matter. A perpetual royalty is usually a clean case; a short-dated override or production payment may not be. Before a sale, a tax adviser or oil-and-gas attorney should characterize the interest from the actual conveyance documents. That step cannot be repaired afterward.

The unifying principle is perpetuity plus real-property character. The more an interest resembles an open-ended ownership stake in producing real property, the more comfortably it may qualify. The more it resembles a time-limited or debt-like claim, the more uncertain it becomes.

## Process and deadlines

The process generally follows any other 1031 exchange, but mineral ownership adds valuation, sourcing, and income-timing issues. Engage an independent [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) before closing. Sale proceeds must go to the QI, not to the seller. From closing, there are [45 days to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days to close](45-day-180-day-deadlines-oil-gas-1031.html). Both clocks start together, are absolute, and do not extend for weekends or holidays.

Mineral valuations can be harder to support than a building valuation, so a defensible appraisal matters both to the sale and to equal-or-greater-value planning. Replacement mineral interests can be thin and slow to source. That is why a fast-closing DST backup is often nearly standard practice. Royalty checks tied to pre-closing production can arrive after closing; they must be routed under the QI's and CPA's instructions so they do not become receipt of exchange funds.

Preparation is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the difference between having a primary option and a certain-to-close backup before the 45-day window runs out, versus discovering after the sale that a thin market leaves little room to recover.

## Income, DSTs, and taxes

Owners commonly exchange minerals for two connected reasons: to defer a substantial tax bill and to turn a volatile, depleting, concentrated income stream into something more stable and passive. A sale can trigger a four-layer stack: federal capital gains, the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html) for higher-income owners, state income tax, and depletion recapture from deductions that reduced basis. A qualifying exchange defers those layers, carries the basis forward, and may ultimately be affected by a step-up in basis at death.

For owners seeking passive replacement property, a DST is a common route. A royalty-pool DST keeps exposure to minerals while spreading it across wells, operators, and basins. A real estate DST shifts the owner into property. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, DST interests can be qualifying replacement property. They close quickly, and royalty-pool DSTs generally pass depletion through. The trade is control of a direct interest for diversification, passivity, and easier execution.

The tax and income questions involve more than the headline gain. They include [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), depletion, DST eligibility, the 3.8% NIIT, and the difference between a direct royalty check and a DST distribution. The results depend on the actual interest and taxpayer, so a CPA should model them.

Owners who want to exchange a volatile mineral stream into passive property may also want to read [retiring from oil and gas through a 1031 exchange](retiring-from-oil-and-gas-1031-into-passive-real-estate.html).

## Advanced and edge-case questions

Reverse and improvement exchanges are available for minerals as they are for other real estate. A reverse exchange acquires the replacement before the relinquished interest is sold. An improvement exchange uses exchange funds to build or improve replacement property. Both use a parking arrangement under Revenue Procedure 2000-37, are more complex and costly, and require careful coordination with experienced counsel and the QI.

Estate and ownership issues need attention before closing. Inherited minerals generally receive a stepped-up basis at death, which can reduce or remove the gain and change whether an exchange is worthwhile. Partnerships and co-owned interests raise same-taxpayer questions and may need restructuring before a sale. Trusts and family entities need their titling reviewed so the taxpayer selling the relinquished interest is the taxpayer acquiring the replacement. These are planning issues; they are difficult or impossible to fix after a sale.

Some owners use a repeated-exchange, step-up-at-death approach often called “swap till you drop.” A DST investor may also, in some structures, roll into a REIT operating partnership at the end of a DST's life through a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html). That can offer further diversification and potential liquidity, but it is typically a one-way move that ends future 1031 eligibility for that interest. Qualified Opportunity Funds are a different deferral tool: they can defer gain from any source, rather than only real property, and come with their own rules and timelines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are mineral rights eligible for a 1031 exchange?

Usually, when the specific interest is a perpetual interest in investment real property. Most fee mineral interests meet that description, and most state law treats minerals as real property. Production payments, which are treated as debt, do not. Equipment in a working interest is personal property and has been ineligible since 2017.

### Are oil and gas royalties eligible?

Yes, when they are perpetual. A perpetual royalty is a continuing real-property interest and is the cleanest oil-and-gas asset for 1031 treatment. Revenue Ruling 68-226 and Revenue Ruling 73-428 support that conclusion. Term royalties and production payments generally do not qualify because they lack the duration of a real-property interest.

### Can I exchange minerals for regular real estate?

Yes. A qualifying perpetual mineral or royalty interest is like-kind to virtually any investment real property: apartments, commercial buildings, farmland, or a DST. The common character is investment real property. That lets an owner convert a depleting, volatile mineral interest into more diversified real estate while deferring tax.

### Does a working interest qualify?

Only in part. The mineral real-property component can qualify, but tangible equipment—such as wellheads, pumps, and tanks—is personal property and no longer qualifies after 2017. The equipment must be defensibly allocated, carved out, and taxed. A working-interest exchange can therefore defer most, but not all, of the gain.

### Does an overriding royalty interest (ORRI) qualify?

It can. Revenue Ruling 72-117 treats overrides as real property, but an ORRI is tied to a lease, so duration must be examined. A long-lived, production-held override is more comfortable. A short-dated or capped override can resemble a term interest and may not qualify. Obtain a tax adviser's view of the specific instrument.

### Does a net profits interest (NPI) qualify?

It is fact-specific. An NPI pays a share of net, rather than gross, proceeds. It shares some royalty characteristics, but its treatment depends on structure and duration. Some NPIs are real property; others resemble contractual or term interests that are not. Have it characterized before relying on 1031 treatment.

### Why don't production payments qualify?

IRC Section 636 treats a production payment—a right to a specified sum or volume of production—as a mortgage loan rather than a real-property interest. Debt is not like-kind to real estate, so a production payment generally cannot be exchanged under Section 1031 regardless of the document's label. Economics control.

### How did the 2017 tax law affect mineral exchanges?

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act limited Section 1031 to real property and the limitation was later made permanent. Pure fee mineral and perpetual royalty interests, which are entirely real property, are not affected. Equipment in a working interest is personal property and must be carved out as a taxable component.

### What is the first step in a mineral exchange?

First, have a tax adviser or oil-and-gas attorney read the conveyance documents and determine whether the interest is a perpetual real-property interest. That is the gating step and cannot be fixed after the sale. Only then should the owner engage a QI and plan replacement property.

### Do I need a qualified intermediary?

Yes. An independent QI must receive sale proceeds before closing so the seller never has constructive receipt. If money reaches the seller or an account the seller controls, the exchange is disqualified and there is no fix. The QI should be engaged before closing and should be comfortable with oil-and-gas closings.

### What are the deadlines?

From the sale closing, there are 45 days to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days to close. Both clocks run at the same time, are absolute, and do not move for weekends or holidays. Because mineral valuations and replacement markets can be slow, identify a fast-closing backup.

### What are the identification rules?

The 3-property rule permits up to three properties of any value. The 200% rule permits any number of properties with aggregate value up to 200% of relinquished value. The 95% rule permits any number, but requires acquisition of 95% of the identified value. Many owners use the 3-property rule: a primary replacement plus one or two backups, often including a fast-closing DST.

### Why is a backup so important for mineral exchanges?

Replacement minerals can be slow and uncertain to source, and no new property can be added after day 45. Identifying a royalty-pool or real estate DST beside the primary option gives an owner a way to pivot if the primary stalls. In thin oil-and-gas markets, that backup can be close to essential.

### How are trailing royalty checks handled?

Royalty proceeds from pre-closing periods often arrive after a sale closes. They should be routed according to the QI's and CPA's instructions so they do not become receipt of exchange funds and jeopardize the exchange. Determine who receives the income and how before closing.

### What is boot, and how do I avoid it?

Boot is cash or non-like-kind value received rather than reinvested, and it is taxable up to the amount of gain. To avoid it, reinvest all net proceeds into equal-or-greater-value replacement real property. Mineral interests are often unencumbered, so full deferral may simply require reinvesting all cash rather than replacing debt.

### What tax does a mineral sale trigger?

The stack can include federal capital gains tax up to 20%, the 3.8% NIIT for higher-income owners, state income tax, and depletion recapture. For long-held, low-basis interests, the combined bill can approach or exceed one third of proceeds. A qualifying 1031 exchange defers all of it.

### What is depletion recapture?

Depletion recapture is gain on sale attributable to earlier depletion deductions. Royalty and mineral owners often deduct depletion—commonly 15% of gross income—during ownership, reducing basis. The lower basis increases gain at sale and recaptures the earlier benefit. For long-held interests it can be a large share of the taxable amount, and a qualifying 1031 can defer it.

### What is a royalty-pool DST?

A royalty-pool DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust holding a diversified portfolio of mineral royalty interests and sold as fractional interests to accredited investors. It is turnkey replacement property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, can diversify wells and basins, and can close quickly. It appeals to owners who want to remain in minerals but reduce concentration and the difficulty of sourcing a direct replacement.

### Can I exchange into a DST and stay in minerals?

Yes. A royalty-pool DST stays in oil and gas while diversifying across many interests and generally passing depletion through. A real estate DST moves the owner from minerals into property. Both may qualify under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 and can close quickly, making the choice depend on whether the owner wants to retain energy exposure.

### Does depletion pass through a royalty DST?

Generally, yes. Investors are treated as owning direct interests in underlying real property, so depletion deductions typically pass through and can shelter part of the distributions. That preservation is a reason royalty-pool DSTs can appeal to income-focused mineral owners, much as depreciation passes through a real estate DST.

### Who can invest in an oil and gas DST?

Accredited investors can invest because DSTs are securities offered under a private placement memorandum through a broker-dealer. Accreditation generally involves SEC income or net-worth thresholds. Any purchase is subject to a suitability review of financial circumstances and objectives.

### How does royalty income compare to DST distributions?

Direct royalty income can vary, decline as wells deplete, swing with commodity prices, and remain concentrated in a specific interest. DST distributions are typically more stable and diversified, particularly for a real estate DST, but depend on the sponsor and underlying assets and include fees. Many exchanges seek to trade volatility and concentration for steadier, diversified income; that is not a promise of a distribution or return.

### Can I exchange inherited minerals?

Yes, if the inherited interest is qualifying perpetual real property. Inheritance produces a stepped-up basis at death, but later depletion and appreciation can create gain again by sale. Whether to exchange or sell depends on that gain and the owner's goals. A CPA can quantify it.

### Is there a minimum size for a mineral exchange?

There is no statutory minimum. QI fees, professional advice, and any DST load can make a very small exchange less efficient. For larger interests, where the four-layer tax may reach five or six figures, deferral can outweigh the costs. The comparison is taxpayer-specific.

### When should I start planning?

Before listing the interest or signing anything. Eligibility, QI engagement, replacement options, and a backup must all be established before sale funds are received. Earlier involvement of the CPA, QI, and adviser leaves more options open and makes the process smoother.

### Can I do a reverse exchange with minerals?

Yes. A reverse exchange acquires the replacement before the relinquished interest is sold, using a parking arrangement under Revenue Procedure 2000-37. An exchange accommodation titleholder holds one property until the sale completes. It is more complex and costly than a forward exchange and requires experienced counsel, but it can work when an ideal replacement appears first.

### Can I build or improve replacement property with mineral exchange funds?

Yes, through an improvement or construction exchange. Exchange funds can build or renovate a replacement property. Work must be completed and paid for within the 180-day window, and the improved value counts toward the replacement. The structure is more complex, uses a titleholder to park the property, and needs advance QI planning.

### How do inherited minerals affect the exchange decision?

The date-of-death step-up can sharply reduce gain. If little gain remains, an exchange may not justify its cost. If depletion and appreciation since inheritance have created substantial gain, a 1031 can defer it. A CPA should quantify post-step-up gain before a decision.

### Can a partnership or co-owned mineral interest be exchanged?

Yes, but the same-taxpayer rule applies: the taxpayer or entity that sells must acquire the replacement. Partners who want separate paths may need a pre-sale drop-and-swap, which carries timing and holding-period risk. Co-owned interests and family entities need a title review before sale so the taxpayer stays consistent on both legs.

### Can I roll a mineral exchange eventually into a REIT?

Indirectly. Some DSTs allow an investor, at the end of the DST life cycle, to roll into a REIT operating partnership through a 721 exchange, or UPREIT. The result can be additional diversification and potential liquidity. It is generally one-way and ends future 1031 eligibility for that interest, so it is an advanced planning step.

### How does a 1031 compare to a Qualified Opportunity Fund for mineral sellers?

They are different tools. A 1031 defers gain by reinvesting full proceeds in like-kind real property. A Qualified Opportunity Fund can defer gain from any source by reinvesting just the gain and can make a decade of appreciation tax-free, but it has separate rules and timelines. A CPA can model the appropriate comparison.

### What is “swap till you drop”?

It is a strategy of exchanging repeatedly rather than selling for cash, allowing deferred gain to roll forward. At death, heirs may receive a stepped-up basis that erases the deferred gain. Some mineral owners plan to defer during life and pass the asset to heirs with the embedded gain removed.

### Does a 1031 exchange eliminate the tax or just postpone it?

It postpones it. Deferred gain carries forward in basis and would be recognized on a future taxable sale that is not an exchange. Continued exchanges plus a step-up in basis at death can make the deferral permanent for heirs, but a single exchange is a deferral rather than an immediate elimination.

### Can I exchange minerals located in multiple states?

Yes. U.S. real property is like-kind to U.S. real property in another state, so minerals in multiple states can be relinquished for replacement real estate or a DST elsewhere. State tax rules and possible clawback provisions should be reviewed with a CPA, but the multi-state location itself does not block the exchange.

### What if part of my interest qualifies and part doesn't?

Exchange the qualifying real-property portion and recognize gain on the rest. In a working interest, equipment is taxable while the mineral component may defer. In a mixed portfolio, perpetual royalties can exchange while production payments or short-dated overrides may not. Determine the split before sale, not when preparing the return.

### Is this FAQ a substitute for professional advice?

No. Eligibility depends on the conveyance, duration, and individual tax circumstances. This FAQ is a way to identify the right questions. The actual determination belongs with a qualified tax adviser, attorney, and CPA, ideally before the interest is listed for sale.

## Glossary

- **1031 Exchange:** A transaction deferring tax on the sale of investment real property reinvested into like-kind real property.
- **Mineral Interest:** Ownership of minerals beneath a tract; generally real property eligible for 1031.
- **Royalty Interest:** A cost-free right to a share of production; perpetual royalties qualify as real property.
- **Working Interest:** The operating interest bearing production costs; it includes real property plus taxable equipment.
- **Overriding Royalty Interest (ORRI):** A royalty carved from a working interest and tied to the lease term; eligibility depends on duration.
- **Net Profits Interest (NPI):** A share of net proceeds from a mineral property; eligibility is fact-specific.
- **Production Payment:** A right to a set sum or production volume, treated as a loan under IRC §636 and ineligible.
- **Perpetual Interest:** An open-ended interest with the duration of a real-property fee estate.
- **Depletion Recapture:** Sale gain attributable to previous depletion deductions that reduced basis.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party holding proceeds so the seller does not receive them constructively.
- **45-Day / 180-Day Deadlines:** The windows to identify in 45 days and close in 180 days after sale.
- **Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received; it is taxable up to gain.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A securitized fractional real-property interest that can qualify as 1031 replacement property.
- **Royalty-Pool DST:** A DST holding diversified mineral royalty interests as turnkey replacement property.
- **Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The ruling treating a DST interest as direct ownership of underlying real property.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of basis to fair market value at death, which can erase deferred gain for heirs.
