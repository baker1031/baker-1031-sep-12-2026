# REIT Bear Markets: History & Lessons

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been revisiting how quickly an apparently durable income asset can become a risk asset when credit, demand, or rates change. REITs have endured significant bear markets. This educational guide looks at 2007–2009, the 2020 COVID crash, and the 2022 rising-rate selloff—what drove them, how recoveries differed, and the lessons they offer.

First-order thinking sees a price decline and assumes all real estate securities share the same fate. Second-order thinking asks what caused it, which balance sheets are exposed, which tenants are under pressure, and whether the investor’s time horizon can survive the drawdown. History informs expectations; it does not predict the next result.

## Key Takeaways

- REITs have endured notable bear markets: the 2007–2009 financial crisis, the 2020 COVID crash, and the 2022 rising-rate selloff.
- Their triggers differed: credit and leverage stress, pandemic shutdowns, and rapid rate hikes.
- Deep drawdowns have historically been followed by recoveries over time, with meaningful sector divergence. Past performance does not guarantee future results.
- Diversification, strong balance sheets, a long horizon, and avoiding panic-selling at bottoms are recurring lessons.

Public REITs, like other traded assets, can have sharp, sustained declines. In 2007–2009 they fell hard amid a credit crunch and leverage concerns; in 2020 they fell rapidly amid pandemic shutdowns; and in 2022 they sold off as rates rose. The asset class has recovered from prior episodes, but every event has had a different trigger and recovery pattern. Use the history to [learn how the asset class behaves under stress](reit-risks-every-investor-should-know.html) before treating it as a forecast. Our [in-depth REIT guide](reits-guide.html) explains how public, non-traded, and private REITs can differ through a market cycle.

## Notable REIT Bear Markets

The 2007–2009 global financial crisis was a credit-driven REIT decline among the most severe in REIT history. Frozen lending and concern over leverage drove a deep fall, followed by recovery over subsequent years as credit conditions normalized and property fundamentals stabilized.

The 2020 COVID crash began in early 2020 and was swift and deep, unfolding over a matter of weeks after shutdowns disrupted operations. Its recovery showed major sector divergence: hotels and retail lagged as tenants closed and rent collection became uncertain; industrial and data-center REITs recovered faster or even grew as e-commerce and digital demand held up or grew.

The 2022 selloff was primarily rate driven. Prices fell as the Federal Reserve moved rates rapidly to [combat inflation](which-reit-sectors-best-hedge-inflation.html). The mechanics were clear: [higher rates raise borrowing costs, pressure property values, and make REIT yields less attractive relative to bonds](how-interest-rates-affect-reit-returns.html). Unlike 2008–09, underlying properties often remained occupied and collecting rent; the discount rate applied to that income changed.

## What Triggered Each

The 2007–2009 event was a credit and leverage shock. Credit markets froze, debt became more expensive or unavailable, and REITs relying on refinancing and borrowed capital were vulnerable. High leverage and near-term maturities magnified the stress.

COVID was a demand and operating shock. Hotels emptied, retailers closed, and rent collection became uncertain in affected sectors. The market repriced the operating risk almost immediately.

The 2022 event was a rate-hike cycle. Higher financing costs, pressure on valuation, and more competitive bond yields lowered REIT prices. The trigger shaped the depth of the decline and the path of recovery in each episode.

## Drawdown & Recovery Patterns

A drawdown is the peak-to-trough loss before recovery. REITs have had deep drawdowns in crises—at times among the sharpest equity-sector declines—but recoveries have occurred over time. The 2007–2009 recovery took years after balance sheets and credit markets were damaged. The 2020 decline was much faster, and resilient sectors recovered more quickly. There is no fixed recovery period to count on.

Sector divergence is a central pattern. In 2020, industrial and data-center REITs recovered faster or even grew, while hotels and retail lagged. A credit shock can take longer to repair than a sentiment- or rate-driven decline, but no sector is permanently defensive and no historical pattern guarantees future results.

## What This History Suggests

REITs are capable of sharp price declines even though they own real assets and can pay dividends. An investor expecting REITs to be stable or bond-like is likely to be surprised during a crisis. The trigger matters as much as the magnitude. Credit stress tests leverage and refinancing; a pandemic can test property demand and operations; a rate shock can pressure valuation and relative yield even when occupancy holds.

Across episodes, REITs entering stress with lower leverage, durable tenants, and well-laddered maturities have tended to be better positioned. That is a historical observation, not a promise.

## Lessons for Investors

Diversify across REIT sectors and individual REITs so one hotel shutdown, office weakness, tenant problem, or debt profile does not control the whole allocation. Funds, ETFs, or a deliberately spread set of holdings can help, but diversification does not eliminate a broad rate-driven decline.

Favor balance-sheet strength. Lower leverage and debt maturities spread over time reduce refinancing pressure and the risk of selling assets or raising capital under distress.

Keep a horizon consistent with the potential drawdown. Bear markets can be emotionally difficult because prices decline while the outlook is uncertain. A suitably sized allocation—one that does not force a sale for other needs—makes discipline more practical. Avoid panic-selling at a trough merely to end the discomfort; selling there can lock in losses and miss a recovery.

## Staying the Course

Preparation precedes behavior. A diversified allocation, strong underlying balance sheets, and capital that is not needed in the near term provide a better foundation for holding through volatility. Reassess fundamentals when conditions change, but distinguish a true impairment from the urge to react to a declining quote. No approach guarantees a result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Have REITs experienced bear markets?

Yes. Like other public-market assets, REITs have endured bear markets—periods of sharp, sustained price declines. The 2007–2009 financial crisis, the early-2020 COVID crash, and the 2022 rate selloff were significant episodes. The COVID decline was swift and deep over a matter of weeks. The episodes involved credit and leverage stress, pandemic shutdowns, and rising rates respectively. Each had some recovery over time, but REITs can decline sharply and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### What were the most notable REIT bear markets?

2007–2009 was among the most severe REIT declines in history, a credit-market and leverage event; early 2020 was a shutdown-driven fall over weeks with hotels and retail lagging and industrial and data centers more resilient; 2022 was a rate-driven decline as the Federal Reserve raised rates. Each had a distinct trigger and recovery shape.

### What caused the 2007–2009 REIT decline?

Credit markets froze, the cost and availability of debt deteriorated, and concerns over leveraged REITs’ ability to refinance matured debt intensified. REITs with high leverage and near-term maturities were especially exposed, and the sector fell among the most sharply of any equity sector during the crisis. Recovery followed as credit and property fundamentals stabilized.

### What happened to REITs during the 2020 COVID crash?

Prices fell swiftly and deeply over weeks as shutdowns hurt hotel and retail operations, tenants closed, occupancy collapsed, and rent collection became uncertain. Industrial and data-center REITs benefited from e-commerce and digital demand, recovered relatively quickly, and the hardest-hit hotel and retail sectors took longer. The episode showed why REITs are not a monolith and why sector diversification matters.

### Why did REITs fall in 2022?

Rapid rate increases raised borrowing costs, pressured property valuations, and made bond yields more competitive. The decline was rate driven rather than a credit crisis or broad demand collapse, though rate sensitivity remains a defining REIT risk.

### How long did REITs take to recover from past bear markets?

It varied. The 2007–2009 recovery took years; resilient sectors recovered more quickly after the fast 2020 decline while hotels and retail lagged. The trigger, sector, balance-sheet damage, and market conditions all affect timing. There is no fixed recovery schedule.

### Are some REIT sectors more resilient in downturns?

Resilience depends on the shock. Industrial and data centers did better than hotels and retail in the highly sector-specific 2020 demand shock, while the 2022 rate selloff affected REITs broadly. A different event can produce a different result, so diversification is preferable to assuming any one sector will always be defensive.

### Should I sell REITs during a bear market?

There is no universal answer. Reassess fundamentals, leverage, liquidity needs, and the reason for the decline, but avoid reflexive panic-selling at a bottom. A long-horizon allocation that is diversified and suitably sized is easier to hold; no strategy eliminates risk.

### Does diversification help in REIT bear markets?

It can reduce the impact of sector-specific or company-specific shocks by spreading exposure across sectors and REITs. It did not prevent broad 2022 rate-driven weakness, but it can reduce concentration in a vulnerable property type, tenant base, or balance sheet.

### Why does balance-sheet strength matter for REITs?

Debt amplifies gains and losses. In 2007–2009, high leverage and near-term maturities created refinancing risk when lending froze. Lower leverage and well-laddered maturities can reduce, not eliminate, forced-sale and capital-raising risk.

### What is a REIT drawdown?

It is the peak-to-trough decline in value. A 30% drawdown means a price fell 30% from its high before beginning recovery. Deep drawdowns require larger subsequent gains to recover fully, which is why they measure the worst-case holding experience in a bear market, not a prediction of the future.

### Are REITs riskier than the broad stock market?

Risk varies by period, sector, leverage, and interest-rate environment. REITs can be volatile, especially under credit and rate stress, but they also have distinct property-income drivers and real estate has historically had only moderate correlation with stocks over long periods. Compare risks rather than assuming a universal ranking.

### What is the biggest lesson from REIT bear markets?

Expect volatility, understand the trigger, diversify, favor balance-sheet quality, match the allocation to a long horizon, and avoid making a fear-driven sale without reassessing fundamentals. Structure, diversification, and discipline have historically mattered more than trying to time the market. History provides perspective, not a forecast.

### How can I stay invested through a REIT bear market?

Prepare beforehand: diversify, analyze leverage and debt maturities, avoid sizing an allocation so large that a drawdown forces a sale, and define the conditions that would justify a fundamentals-based reassessment. Emotional comfort cannot be manufactured in the middle of a crisis.

## Glossary

### Bear Market

A period of sharp, sustained decline in asset prices.

### Drawdown

The peak-to-trough decline in an investment's value.

### Recovery

The rebound in value following a bear-market low.

### Global Financial Crisis

The 2007–2009 credit crisis that hit leveraged REITs hard.

### COVID Crash

The swift, deep 2020 pandemic-driven market decline.

### Rising-Rate Selloff

The 2022 REIT decline driven by rapid rate hikes.

### Leverage

The use of debt, which amplifies both gains and losses.

### Credit Crunch

A freeze in lending that raises borrowing cost and risk.

### Sector Divergence

Different REIT sectors recovering at different speeds.

### Interest-Rate Sensitivity

REIT prices tending to fall when rates rise.

### Refinancing Risk

The risk of being unable to roll over maturing debt.

### Industrial REIT

A REIT owning warehouses and logistics, resilient in 2020.

### Data Center REIT

A REIT owning data centers, resilient in 2020.

### Diversification

Spreading risk across sectors and individual REITs.

### Time Horizon

How long an investor plans to hold an investment.

### Panic-Selling

Selling at a bottom out of fear, locking in losses.
