# 1031 vs. 721 Exchange: Key Differences and When to Use Each


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how easily “tax deferral” can make two very different decisions sound identical. A 1031 exchange and a 721 exchange can both defer capital-gains tax, but they take an owner to different places: one keeps the owner in direct real estate; the other moves the owner into REIT ownership.

First-order thinking sees one tax result. Second-order thinking asks what remains after the deferral: who controls the property, how much liquidity exists in practice, whether diversification has increased, and whether the investor can change course later. This guide compares the 1031 and 721 exchanges, their differences in control, liquidity, diversification, and flexibility, and the 1031-then-721 path that uses both.

## The core difference

The core difference is what the investor exchanges into and ultimately holds. A 1031 exchange trades like-kind real property for other like-kind real property. The investor exchanges property for one or more replacement properties and remains a direct real-estate owner. A 721 exchange contributes property to a REIT's operating partnership for [OP units](op-units-explained.html). The investor receives a partnership interest—units in the REIT's operating partnership—not direct real estate.

That distinction flows through every later choice. Because the 1031 keeps the investor in direct real property, it retains the characteristics of direct ownership: control and the ability to 1031 again. Real property can be exchanged repeatedly. Because the 721 moves the investor into REIT units, a partnership interest, it offers the characteristics of REIT ownership: diversification, liquidity, and passivity. But the investor loses the ability to 1031 because the units are not real property. The 721 is generally a one-way move.

Put plainly, a 1031 keeps the investor a direct property owner; a 721 makes the investor a REIT investor. Control, flexibility, liquidity, and diversification follow from that choice.

## What each defers and how

Both exchanges defer [capital-gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html), but they do so through separate code sections and mechanisms. A 1031 exchange defers gain under Section **1031** through an exchange of like-kind real property; the gain carries into the replacement property's basis. A 721 exchange defers gain under Section **721** by contributing property to a partnership for an interest; the gain carries into the OP units' basis. Both use carryover basis to defer gain, but they lead to different holdings: replacement property for the 1031 and OP units for the 721.

They also share the step-up benefit. Holding replacement property or OP units until death can erase the deferred gain through a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html). That can make both useful tax- and estate-planning tools.

The transaction mechanics are not the same. A 1031 is an exchange of like-kind real property with [**45/180-day deadlines**](1031-exchange-timeline.html) and a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html). A 721 is a contribution to a partnership: there is no like-kind requirement and no **45/180** deadline for the contribution itself, but the property must be one the REIT will accept.

> Both defer gain through carryover basis and both offer the step-up at death. But the 1031 exchanges real property, with a **45/180-day** clock and a QI; the 721 contributes property to a partnership for units.

## Control and flexibility

Control and flexibility differ sharply. With a 1031 exchange, the investor owns the replacement real property directly and makes the management, financing, sale, and exchange decisions. The investor can complete another 1031 with the replacement property, continue exchanging real estate, and reposition as goals change.

With a 721 exchange, the investor gives up control. The investor holds OP units in the REIT operating partnership while the REIT manages the properties and makes the decisions. The investor is a passive unit holder, not the controller of the real estate. Flexibility also ends in an important sense: OP units generally cannot be exchanged through a 1031. The investor can [convert units to shares (taxable)](tax-consequences-of-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html) or hold them, but cannot move back into direct real estate through a 1031.

This is a central trade. The 1031 preserves control and the ability to continue exchanging. The 721 trades both for the potential benefits of REIT ownership—diversification, liquidity, and passivity. An owner who values control and flexibility may fit the 1031; an owner ready to exchange them for REIT benefits may fit the 721.

## Liquidity and diversification

Liquidity and diversification favor the 721, which is much of its appeal. A 1031 leaves the investor in direct real estate, which is illiquid: a sale takes time and is all-or-nothing. It is often concentrated in particular properties. An investor can diversify a 1031 across several replacement properties, but direct real estate remains relatively illiquid and takes work to diversify.

By contrast, OP units can be converted into REIT shares. For public REITs, the shares are liquid, trade in the market, and can be sold in portions, creating a path to liquidity that direct real estate lacks. The units also represent a stake in the REIT's diversified portfolio—many properties, property types, and markets. That can provide broader diversification than a single property or even several properties.

Yet liquidity and diversification have risks that deserve equal weight. Many UPREIT exits lead to [non-traded REITs](721-exchanges-and-non-traded-reits.html), where redemption programs are limited, capped, and can be suspended at the sponsor's discretion. Units may not be readily sellable when cash is needed. REITs also carry leverage, valuation, and distribution-sustainability risk; distributions are not guaranteed; and converting OP units to shares is a taxable event. Those facts belong beside the diversification benefit, not after it.

## The one-way door of 721

A critical factor is the [one-way nature of a 721](721-exchange-one-way-door-losing-1031-eligibility.html). Once completed, it generally cannot be reversed through a 1031. OP units are a partnership interest rather than like-kind real property, so an investor cannot 1031 out of them. The choices are to hold the units with the gain deferred, convert them to REIT shares and recognize tax, or hold them until death and use the step-up.

That makes a 721 an endpoint: a commitment to REIT ownership rather than a temporary or reversible step. The investor cannot change course and return to direct real estate through a tax-free 1031; converting units to shares or cash first recognizes gain. The contrast with a 1031 is clear: the 1031 keeps options open because direct real property can be exchanged again.

Finality is not inherently a drawback. For an owner who wants REIT ownership for diversification, liquidity, passivity, and estate planning, it can be the intended result. But it has to be understood before the exchange, because the investor is making a generally permanent transition into REIT ownership.

## Key Takeaways

- Core difference: a 1031 exchanges like-kind real property and keeps the investor in direct real estate; a 721 contributes property to a REIT for units and moves the investor into REIT ownership.
- Both defer gain through carryover basis and offer the step-up at death, but through different code sections and mechanics.
- The 1031 keeps control and flexibility, including the ability to exchange again; the 721 offers liquidity and diversification but is generally a one-way move.
- A powerful combination is the 1031-then-721 path: exchange into a DST, then later UPREIT the DST into a REIT through a 721 exchange.

## When to use each (and the 1031-then-721 path)

Use a 1031 exchange when the goal is to remain in direct real estate: retain control, retain the ability to keep exchanging, reposition among properties, and build or trade a portfolio. It fits owners who want to stay active or semi-active direct real-estate investors while deferring tax and keeping options open.

Use a 721 exchange when the goal is to transition into REIT ownership: gain diversification, liquidity, and passivity, including for estate planning, and accept the one-way nature and loss of control. It can fit an owner ready to exit direct real estate into a diversified, passive, more liquid REIT as an endpoint.

There is also the [1031-then-721 path](1031-to-721-exchange.html). The investor first completes a [1031 exchange into a DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html), deferring gain and acquiring passive real estate. Later, if the DST property is acquired by a REIT in a 721 exchange—sometimes through a planned “UPREIT exit”—the DST interest is converted into OP units. The route moves from direct property, through a DST, to REIT ownership, with tax deferral throughout. It lets an owner eventually reach diversified, liquid REIT ownership on a tax-deferred basis. In practice, many owners use the 1031 during active investing years and treat the two-step as an eventual exit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a 1031 and a 721 exchange?

A 1031 exchange trades like-kind real property for other real property and keeps the investor in direct real estate with control and the ability to exchange again. A 721 exchange contributes property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units and moves the investor into REIT ownership with diversification, liquidity, and passivity, but generally as a one-way move because OP units cannot be exchanged through a 1031. Both defer gain through carryover basis and offer the step-up at death, but they produce different outcomes: direct real estate versus REIT ownership.

### Do both the 1031 and 721 defer taxes?

Yes. Both defer capital-gains tax through carryover basis, but under different code sections: the 1031 under Section 1031 through an exchange of like-kind real property, and the 721 under Section 721 through a contribution of property to a partnership for an interest. Both also offer the step-up in basis at death, which can erase deferred gain. They are tax-deferral tools, with potential tax elimination through the step-up, but their transaction structures and resulting holdings differ.

### Which gives me more control?

The 1031. The investor owns replacement real property directly and retains control over management, financing, and when to sell or exchange. A 721 gives up that control: the investor holds OP units in the REIT operating partnership, the REIT manages the properties, and the investor is a passive unit holder. If control of the real estate matters, the 1031 keeps it; the 721 trades it for passive REIT ownership.

### Which offers more liquidity and diversification?

The 721. OP units are convertible into REIT shares, which are liquid for public REITs and sellable in portions, and they represent a stake in the REIT's whole diversified portfolio of properties, types, and markets. A 1031 retains direct real estate, which is illiquid and concentrated, though the investor can diversify among multiple replacements. The 721 offers liquidity and diversification at the cost of control and flexibility.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange after a 721 exchange?

Generally, no. OP units received in a 721 are a partnership interest, not like-kind real property, so they cannot be exchanged through a 1031. The investor can hold the units with gain deferred, convert them to REIT shares and recognize tax, or hold them until death for the step-up. The 721 is therefore generally a one-way transition into REIT ownership; it cannot be reversed through a tax-free 1031. A 1031, by contrast, keeps the investor able to exchange again.

### When should I use a 1031 exchange?

Use a 1031 when the goal is continued direct real-estate ownership: retaining control, flexibility to keep exchanging, the ability to reposition among properties, or the opportunity to build a portfolio. It fits owners who want to remain active or semi-active direct real-estate investors and defer tax while keeping options open. It is the choice for staying in direct real estate.

### When should I use a 721 exchange?

Use a 721 when the goal is a transition into REIT ownership: diversification, liquidity, passivity, and estate planning, coupled with readiness to accept the one-way nature and loss of control. It fits an owner ready to exit direct real estate into a diversified, passive, more liquid REIT as an endpoint. It is the choice for the REIT exit.

### What is the 1031-then-721 path?

It is a combined route. The investor completes a 1031 exchange into a DST, defers gain, and gains passive real estate. Later, when the DST property is acquired by a REIT through a 721 exchange, sometimes a planned UPREIT exit, the investor's DST interest converts into OP units. The route goes from direct property, through a DST, to REIT ownership, all tax-deferred. It combines the 1031 and 721 and can provide diversified, liquid REIT ownership on a tax-deferred basis.

### Is the 721 better than the 1031?

Neither is universally better; they serve different goals. The 1031 is better for remaining in direct real estate with control, flexibility, and continued exchanges. The 721 is better for a transition into REIT ownership with diversification, liquidity, passivity, and estate planning as an endpoint. The right choice depends on whether the investor wants direct ownership or a REIT. Many owners use the 1031 while investing actively and the 721, often through the 1031-then-721 path, as an eventual exit. Match the tool to the goal and stage.

### Can I diversify with a 1031 exchange too?

Yes, to a degree. A 1031 investor can acquire multiple replacement properties or DSTs and diversify across properties, types, and markets while remaining in direct real estate. But a 721 can provide broader, immediate diversification through a stake in the REIT's whole portfolio and adds liquidity through convertible units. Both can diversify; the 721 offers broader diversification plus liquidity, while a 1031's diversification remains within direct, illiquid real-estate ownership.

### Does the step-up at death apply to both?

Yes. Replacement property from a 1031 and OP units from a 721 can receive a step-up in basis at death, which can erase deferred gain for heirs. Both can therefore ultimately eliminate the deferred gain if held until death. The estate-planning benefit applies whether the investor stays in direct real estate or transitions into REIT units.

### How do I decide between them?

Start with the goal. If the investor wants direct real estate, control, flexibility, and continued exchanges, the 1031 fits. If the investor wants REIT ownership, diversification, liquidity, passivity, estate planning, and an endpoint, the 721 fits. The investor's stage matters too: many owners use a 1031 while actively investing and a 721 for their eventual exit. The 1031-then-721 path can lead to REIT ownership through a DST. Weigh control and flexibility against diversification and liquidity with the investor's advisors; we can help with the decision.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange**  
Trading like-kind real property to defer gain while staying in direct real estate.

**721 Exchange**  
Contributing property to a REIT for OP units and moving into REIT ownership.

**Section 1031**  
The like-kind exchange provision for real-property-to-real-property trades.

**Section 721**  
The partnership-contribution provision for property-to-OP-units.

**Carryover Basis**  
The basis-deferral mechanism shared by both exchanges.

**Step-Up in Basis**  
The death-time reset available for both replacement property and OP units.

**Control**  
Direct-ownership decision-making, retained with a 1031 and given up with a 721.

**Flexibility**  
The ability to exchange again, retained with a 1031 and lost with a 721.

**Liquidity**  
Convertibility to tradable shares, offered by a 721 and not inherent in a 1031.

**Diversification**  
Exposure to a portfolio, broad with a 721's REIT units.

**One-Way Move**  
The 721's generally irreversible nature: OP units cannot be exchanged through a 1031.

**OP Units**  
The partnership interest received through a 721, not eligible for a 1031.

**1031-then-721 Path**  
Exchanging into a DST and later UPREITing into a REIT through a 721.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)**  
A passive 1031 vehicle, often the step toward a 721 REIT exit.

**UPREIT Exit**  
A REIT acquiring a property or DST through a 721 exchange.

**Endpoint**  
The 721 as a final transition into REIT ownership.

## 1031 vs. 721 exchange at a glance

The core differences between a 1031 exchange and a 721 (UPREIT) exchange, side by side:

| Factor | 1031 Exchange | 721 Exchange (UPREIT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you give | Relinquished real property | Real property (often via a DST) |
| What you receive | Replacement real property (or DST interest) | Operating partnership (OP) units in a REIT |
| Future 1031 eligibility | Preserved — you can keep exchanging | Lost — OP units cannot be 1031'd |
| Deadlines | Strict 45-day / 180-day clock | No 45/180 clock for the contribution itself |
| Diversification | Property-by-property | Instant, across the REIT's whole portfolio |
| Liquidity | Illiquid until you sell or exchange | OP units convert to REIT shares over time |
| Step-up at death | Yes | Yes |

## Which should you choose?

Use a 1031 exchange when the goal is to remain invested in real property and preserve the option to defer again later. The door stays open.

Choose a 721 exchange when the goal is diversification, passive income, and estate liquidity and the investor accepts surrendering future 1031 eligibility. Many investors combine the two: a 1031 into a DST now, then a 721 into the REIT later.
