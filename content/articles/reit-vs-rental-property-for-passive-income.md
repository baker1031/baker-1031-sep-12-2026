# REIT vs. Rental Property for Passive Income

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing “passive real estate income” used for two investments that ask very different things of the owner. One buys a share and delegates operations. The other buys a business with tenants, repairs, debt, and decisions.

If you want passive income from real estate, REITs and rental property are the usual choices. This guide compares which is truly hands-off, income predictability, liquidity and minimums, tax differences, control and leverage, and how to choose an approach.

First-order thinking asks which option has the higher headline yield. Second-order thinking asks what work, concentration, liquidity, tax treatment, and downside are being accepted to earn it. Neither is universally better; the fit depends on effort tolerance, capital, control, and tax goals.

## Truly Passive vs. Hands-On

A REIT is genuinely passive for the shareholder. Management acquires and sells properties, finds and manages tenants, handles maintenance, financing, and daily operations. The investor owns shares and receives any declared dividends, much like owning a stock.

A rental property is hands-on, even when it is called passive income. The owner screens tenants, collects rent, handles repairs and inconvenient calls, manages vacancies, financing, insurance, and legal requirements. A property manager reduces the work but still requires oversight, decisions, and fees that reduce income. A rental is closer to a part-time business than a passive holding.

## Income Predictability

A REIT generally owns a diversified portfolio—often many properties, tenants, markets, and sometimes sectors—and pays dividends from aggregate income. One vacancy has limited effect. Dividends can still be cut if the broader portfolio struggles, but income is less exposed to any single property or tenant.

A single rental has concentrated income. When it is occupied and the tenant pays, cash flow can be strong. A vacancy produces no rent while mortgage, taxes, and insurance continue; a problem tenant or major repair can erase months of profit. Multiple rentals diversify some of that risk, but also multiply management work and rarely match a REIT’s immediate breadth.

## Liquidity & Minimums

A publicly traded REIT can be bought or sold on a trading day, sometimes for the price of one share—tens or hundreds of dollars. There is no mortgage qualification, closing process, or large down payment. It is divisible, easy to scale, and liquid at a market price.

A rental requires a substantial down payment, commonly **20-25%** for investment property, plus closing costs, reserves, and mortgage qualification. Selling can take months, costs money, and cannot be done in pieces. It is a large, illiquid, all-or-nothing commitment. Non-traded REITs are also illiquid, so the liquidity advantage described here is for publicly traded REITs.

## Tax Differences

Taxes often favor direct rental ownership. A landlord can use depreciation deductions, deduct repairs, management fees, insurance, and mortgage interest, and may use a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) to defer capital gain and depreciation recapture by reinvesting in like-kind real estate. The deferral can be repeated and a step-up at death can eliminate deferred tax.

REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income because the REIT generally paid no corporate tax. Eligible qualified REIT dividends can receive a **20%** Section 199A deduction, and part of a distribution can be tax-deferred return of capital that reduces basis. REIT shares are securities, however: they are not 1031-eligible and the investor cannot deduct property depreciation against other income. A tax-advantaged account can narrow the dividend-tax difference.

## Control and Leverage

A rental owner controls the property: tenant choice, rent, renovation, timing of sale, and the mortgage. Direct leverage can amplify returns on invested equity when the property performs, but it also amplifies losses and makes debt a personal responsibility.

A REIT shareholder has no control over individual assets, leverage, tenants, or operations. That loss of control is the price of professional management and simplicity. REIT leverage is held at the entity level, not as a mortgage in the shareholder’s name.

## Key Takeaways

- A REIT is genuinely passive; a rental property remains hands-on, even with a property manager.
- REIT dividends are diversified across a portfolio, while a single rental is exposed to one property, tenant, vacancy, and repair risk.
- Public REITs are low-minimum and liquid; rentals require substantial capital and are slow and costly to sell.
- Rentals offer depreciation, expense deductions, direct leverage, and 1031 eligibility; REITs offer passivity, diversification, and liquidity.

## Choosing Your Approach

A REIT may suit an investor who values passive, diversified, liquid real-estate exposure and does not want tenants, repairs, or a mortgage in their name. A rental may suit someone who values control, direct leverage, tax advantages, and the possibility of adding value, and who has the capital, time, skill, and temperament for the work.

There is a middle route for a landlord who no longer wants to manage: selling a rental and exchanging into a passive Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can preserve 1031 deferral while removing day-to-day ownership. The route must be assessed with tax and legal advisers because qualification, timing, and suitability matter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a REIT or a rental property more passive?

A REIT is more passive. The professional manager handles assets, tenants, maintenance, financing, and operations; the investor owns shares. A rental requires tenant selection, rent collection, repairs, vacancies, financing, insurance, compliance, and oversight of a manager if one is hired. Management fees reduce rental income, and the owner remains responsible.

### Which provides more predictable income, a REIT or a rental?

A REIT usually provides more diversified and relatively steady income because many properties and tenants support its dividend. A single rental can produce strong cash flow but is exposed to a vacancy, one tenant, and irregular repairs while fixed costs continue. A REIT dividend can still be cut, so neither income stream is guaranteed.

### How much money do I need to start with a REIT versus a rental?

A public REIT can be started with the price of one share. A rental generally requires a sizable down payment—often **20-25%** for an investment property—plus closing costs, cash reserves, and mortgage qualification. The difference in required capital can be tens of thousands of dollars or more.

### Is a REIT or a rental property more liquid?

A publicly traded REIT is more liquid because shares can be sold on a trading day at a market price. A rental sale can take months, involves transaction costs, and cannot be partially sold. Non-traded REITs are illiquid too, with limited and potentially suspended redemption programs.

### What are the tax advantages of a rental property over a REIT?

Rental ownership can provide depreciation deductions, write-offs for repairs, management, insurance, and interest, and 1031 eligibility. A landlord may defer capital gain and depreciation recapture by exchanging into like-kind real estate, potentially repeatedly and with a possible step-up at death. REIT shares do not provide direct depreciation deductions or 1031 eligibility.

### How are REIT dividends taxed compared to rental income?

Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, although eligible qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction and some distributions may be return of capital. Rental income can be offset by depreciation and operating-expense deductions. Results depend on individual facts; ask a CPA to apply the rules.

### Can I lose money in either a REIT or a rental property?

Yes. REIT shares can fall, distributions can be cut, and non-traded REITs can be illiquid. A rental can have a vacancy, bad tenant, repair, declining rents, property-value loss, financing problem, or negative cash flow. Leverage can magnify gains and losses in either the underlying real estate or a rental owner’s equity.

### Do I need to manage a REIT like I manage a rental?

No. A REIT shareholder does not run operations. A rental owner makes or oversees operational decisions and bears the responsibility. Hiring a manager changes the workload, but not the owner’s ultimate responsibility or the cost of the service.

### Can I use a 1031 exchange with a rental but not a REIT?

Generally, yes. A rental held for investment can qualify as like-kind real property; REIT shares are securities and cannot be exchanged directly under Section 1031. A landlord may exchange into a passive DST, and a DST may later enter a 721 transaction with a REIT, but this is not a direct 1031 into REIT shares.

### Is owning a rental property worth the extra work?

It depends. The work can be worthwhile for someone who values control, leverage, tax deductions, and 1031 eligibility and has the time and aptitude for ownership. It may not be worthwhile for someone who prefers time, peace of mind, liquidity, or a hands-off investment. A tired landlord can explore a 1031 exchange into a DST with advisers.

### Can I invest in both REITs and rental property?

Yes. A portfolio can use REITs as a passive, liquid, diversified real-estate allocation and rentals for direct control, leverage, and tax attributes. The mix should reflect effort tolerance, capital, control, tax goals, and sensible diversification rather than an either/or rule.

### What if I own a rental but no longer want to manage it?

Selling triggers gain and depreciation recapture; hiring a manager reduces income and still requires oversight. A 1031 exchange into a professionally managed DST can defer gain and recapture while removing day-to-day management, provided the exchange and investment satisfy applicable rules. A DST may later roll into a REIT through a 721 transaction, but no outcome is guaranteed.

### Which is better for a beginner, a REIT or a rental property?

For many beginners, a public REIT is a lower-capital, diversified, liquid starting point without operating responsibility. A rental requires capital, financing, management ability, and tolerance for concentrated risk. A motivated beginner with sufficient resources can own a rental, but it is a bigger and more hands-on commitment.

### How much income can I expect from a REIT versus a rental property?

Neither amount is guaranteed. REIT income comes through dividends that vary by sector and issuer and can be cut. Rental income is rent less mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, and vacancy losses; it can be higher, zero, or negative and is work-dependent. Compare realistic after-expense and after-tax figures, not headline yield.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns or finances income-producing real estate, owned via shares.
- **Rental Property:** Directly owned real estate rented to tenants for income.
- **Passive Income:** Income requiring little ongoing effort — true for a REIT, not a rental.
- **Property Manager:** A hired firm that runs a rental, for a fee, under your oversight.
- **Vacancy Risk:** The risk of lost rent when a rental sits empty.
- **Diversification:** A REIT's spread across many properties versus a single rental.
- **Liquidity:** The ease of selling — high for traded REITs, low for rentals.
- **Down Payment:** The upfront capital a rental purchase requires.
- **Leverage:** Using a mortgage to amplify returns (and risk) on a rental.
- **Depreciation Deduction:** A paper expense rental owners deduct against rental income.
- **Expense Deductions:** Repairs, management, insurance, and interest a rental owner deducts.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind real estate (rentals qualify; REIT shares don't).
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
- **Return of Capital (ROC):** A tax-deferred part of REIT distributions that reduces basis.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** Passive, 1031-eligible real estate — a middle ground for tired landlords.
- **Tax-Advantaged Account:** An IRA or 401(k) where REIT dividend taxation matters less.

## REIT vs. rental property at a glance

How a REIT and a directly owned rental compare for an investor seeking passive income:

| Factor | REIT | Rental Property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How passive | Truly passive | Hands-on, even with a manager |
| Income predictability | Diversified dividends | Tied to one property's occupancy |
| Liquidity | Public: daily; non-traded: limited | Illiquid; sale takes months |
| Minimum to start | Low (shares) | Full down payment + reserves |
| Leverage | Indirect, at the fund level | Direct mortgage in your name |
| Control | None | Full control of the asset |
| Tax | Dividends; 20% QBI may apply | Depreciation, write-offs, 1031 on sale |

## Which should you choose?

Choose a REIT when you want genuinely passive, diversified, liquid exposure to real estate without tenants, repairs, or a mortgage in your name.

Choose a rental when you want control, direct leverage, hands-on tax advantages, and the option to 1031 the gain later. If a rental has become a burden, a 1031 into a DST can preserve real-estate exposure while making ownership passive.
