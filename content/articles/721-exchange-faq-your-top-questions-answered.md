# 721 Exchange FAQ: Your Top Questions Answered

I keep hearing one version of the same question from property owners: “Can I stop managing the building without writing a large check to the government first?” A 721 exchange can be part of the answer, but it changes much more than the tax timing. It changes what the owner holds, who controls the real estate, the liquidity path, and the estate plan.

Baker 1031 Research updated this 721 Exchange FAQ in June 2026. It is a 16 min read. The questions below cover the basics, tax, OP units and liquidity, process, suitability, and risk. A 721 exchange is sophisticated and generally irreversible, so clarity about the trade-offs matters before an owner contributes property for securities.

First-order thinking asks whether Section 721 defers gain. Second-order thinking asks when the gain returns, whether the REIT is strong, how much control is surrendered, what shares are really worth, and whether the owner’s capital needs can tolerate the liquidity terms. The right answer is never only tax.

## The basics: what and why

A 721 exchange, also called an UPREIT conversion, is a tax-deferred contribution of property to a REIT operating partnership for [operating partnership units](op-units-explained.html), or OP units. Instead of selling and paying tax or completing a 1031 into another direct property, the owner receives an interest in the REIT’s operating partnership.

Owners commonly consider it to defer [capital gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html), diversify a single property into the REIT’s portfolio, obtain a possible liquidity path through convertible units, move from active management to passive ownership, and support estate planning with divisible units and a possible step-up. The structure depends on Section 721 and an UPREIT: a REIT that owns real estate through an operating partnership.

That is the basic exchange: direct property becomes tax-deferred REIT ownership. The rest of the decision is about whether the REIT, its structure, and its risks suit the owner.

## The tax questions

Section 721 can defer the four-layer tax stack: capital gain, depreciation recapture, [NIIT](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. The property’s basis carries into the OP units as carryover basis, so the gain is deferred at contribution rather than paid then.

The deferred tax is generally triggered when OP units are converted to REIT shares or redeemed for cash. It may also be affected if the partnership disposes of contributed property in a taxable way. Until a trigger occurs, the units continue to hold the embedded gain. If they are held until death, a step-up in basis can potentially erase the deferred gain under current law.

The 721 and the 1031 both defer gain and can benefit from a step-up. The 1031 exchanges like-kind real property and keeps the owner in direct real estate with the ability to exchange again. The 721 contributes property for a partnership interest and is generally a one-way move into REIT ownership.

## The OP units and liquidity questions

OP units are the holder’s partnership interest in the operating partnership that owns the REIT’s real estate. They generally pay distributions comparable to REIT dividends and represent economic exposure to the portfolio.

They are not themselves market-traded. Liquidity usually comes by converting units to REIT shares after a holding period, often around a year. In a public REIT, shares may be sold on the market. The holder can convert gradually to spread recognition of gain, but conversion is the taxable step. In a non-traded REIT, liquidity is generally more limited and can depend on a redemption program rather than an active market.

The path is units → shares → cash. It is a useful option, not on-demand liquidity, and it comes with the tax cost of conversion.

## The process questions

The process begins with suitability: determine whether the owner’s goals fit a passive REIT endpoint. Next, find a willing UPREIT or, where direct contribution is not feasible, a DST structured for a 721 exit. The property is valued to determine units, the contribution is documented, the owner receives OP units, and those units are held for distributions or later conversion.

A standalone 721 exchange has no statutory 45- and 180-day deadlines because it is a partnership contribution rather than a like-kind exchange. Its timetable is driven by the parties, diligence, valuation, and negotiation. If an investor first completes a 1031 into a DST and later moves through a 721, the 1031 portion has the 45- and 180-day rules; the later 721 exit does not.

Timing can take weeks or months, followed by a holding period before unit conversion. Each transaction depends on its complexity, the relevant REIT, the DST path if any, and the parties’ pace.

## The suitability questions

The 721 can suit an owner ready to leave direct real estate for diversified, passive, and potentially more liquid REIT ownership. Common candidates include the tired landlord, an estate planner, an owner highly concentrated in a few properties, and someone who wants a measured route to cash.

It generally does not suit an active investor who values direct control and the flexibility to keep exchanging real property through Section 1031. The loss of direct decision-making and one-way nature are features, not minor details.

OP units are securities and are generally offered to [accredited investors](insights.html) after a suitability review. Accreditation and suitability requirements must be confirmed for the specific offering.

## The risk questions

The principal risks are the one-way nature of the contribution, loss of control, REIT performance and management risk, tax on conversion, market and valuation risk, fees, and sponsor risk. A traded REIT can bring share-price volatility. A non-traded REIT can bring valuation uncertainty and limited program-based liquidity. Neither is a fixed-value substitute for direct property.

The 721 is generally not reversible. The investor cannot usually return to direct property tax-free because OP units are not 1031-eligible real property. If the holding loses value, the investor bears that outcome. The REIT’s operating performance, its properties, its sponsor, and the broader market all matter.

Risk can be reduced—but not eliminated—by confirming the fit before contributing, selecting a strong REIT and sponsor, understanding fees and liquidity, planning conversion tax, and working with advisors. The better choice is the one that makes the total risk and return package acceptable, not merely the one that delays tax.

### Key takeaways

- A 721 exchange is a tax-deferred contribution of property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units.
- Section 721 defers capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax; conversion generally triggers the deferred gain and a step-up may eliminate it at death.
- OP units can pay distributions and convert to shares, while a standalone 721 has no 1031-style deadlines.
- The structure suits owners seeking passive REIT ownership, not active control, and has real one-way, control, performance, market, fee, and tax risks.

## Other common questions

An owner cannot 1031 directly into REIT shares or OP units because they are not like-kind real property. A common route is the DST bridge: a 1031 into a DST, followed later by a 721 into a REIT. This is often the practical route from direct property to tax-deferred REIT ownership.

Property for a direct 721 must be investment or productive-use real estate a REIT will accept based on property type, quality, size, location, financials, and portfolio strategy. A REIT’s willingness to take the property is often the binding constraint. Personal-use property and dealer property do not qualify. The DST bridge can help when a direct REIT contribution is unavailable.

The investor gives up direct control because the REIT manages the properties. Professional help is important: a securities-licensed financial professional for fit and suitability, a CPA for tax, and an attorney for documentation and structuring. This is a meaningful, long-term transaction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 721 exchange in simple terms?

It is a tax-deferred contribution of property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units under Section 721. Instead of selling or doing a 1031 into another property, the owner becomes a REIT partnership investor. It is also called an UPREIT conversion.

### Does a 721 exchange defer taxes?

Yes. Section 721 generally defers capital gain, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax at contribution, with the gain carried into OP units through carryover basis. Tax is generally triggered on conversion to shares or cash. Units held until death can potentially receive a step-up that erases deferred gain under current law.

### What are OP units?

They are operating partnership units: the holder’s partnership stake in the entity owning the REIT’s real estate. They represent the investor’s interest in the portfolio, typically pay distributions, and may convert to REIT shares after a holding period.

### How do I get my money out after a 721 exchange?

Typically by converting OP units into REIT shares after a holding period, often around a year, and selling those shares for cash in the case of a public REIT. Conversion triggers deferred gain. Gradual conversion can spread tax recognition. A non-traded REIT normally has a more limited, program-based liquidity path.

### Does a 721 exchange have deadlines?

A standalone 721 has no fixed statutory deadlines, unlike a 1031’s 45/180-day rules. It is a partnership contribution with a parties-driven timeline. A 1031-first DST bridge still has the 1031 deadlines on the first step; the 721 exit does not.

### Who should do a 721 exchange?

Owners ready to move from direct property to diversified, passive, potentially more liquid REIT ownership: tired landlords, estate planners, concentrated owners, and liquidity seekers. It usually does not fit an active investor who values control and continued 1031 flexibility. It generally requires accreditation and suitability.

### Can I undo a 721 exchange?

Generally, no. OP units are partnership interests and cannot usually be exchanged into direct real estate tax-free under Section 1031. Returning to direct property generally requires taxable conversion or sale and purchase with after-tax proceeds.

### What are the main risks of a 721 exchange?

The key risks include an irreversible loss of 1031 eligibility, loss of direct control, REIT performance and management risk, tax on conversion, traded-share price volatility or non-traded valuation uncertainty, fees, sponsor risk, and limited liquidity. Careful selection and planning can reduce risk but cannot eliminate it.

### Can I 1031 directly into a REIT?

No. REIT shares and OP units are not like-kind real property. An owner can instead 1031 into a DST that is like-kind real property and later use the DST bridge to a 721 REIT transaction.

### What property qualifies for a 721 exchange?

Investment or productive-use real estate that a REIT will accept based on its property type, quality, size, location, financials, and portfolio needs. Personal-use and dealer property do not qualify. Even a tax-eligible property may not work if the relevant REIT does not want it.

### Is a 721 exchange better than selling?

It can be more tax-efficient for an owner who wants long-term REIT ownership because tax is deferred and may be eliminated by a step-up, instead of paying a potential third or more at sale. A sale provides flexible after-tax proceeds and does not commit the owner to a REIT. The better decision depends on the owner’s objectives.

### Do I need professional help for a 721 exchange?

Yes. The strategy combines securities, tax mechanics, valuation, and legal documentation, and it is generally irreversible. Use a securities-licensed advisor for fit and suitability, a CPA for tax, and an attorney for structuring and documents.

## Glossary

### 721 Exchange

A tax-deferred contribution of property to a REIT partnership for OP units.

### UPREIT Conversion

Another name for a 721 exchange.

### Section 721

The provision generally deferring gain on the contribution.

### OP Units

The partnership stake received in a 721 exchange that typically pays distributions.

### Carryover Basis

The basis that carries deferred gain from the property into the units.

### Conversion

Exchanging units for shares, generally triggering deferred gain.

### Step-Up in Basis

The basis reset at death that can eliminate deferred gain.

### Four-Layer Tax Stack

Capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax, which Section 721 can defer.

### One-Way Move

The generally irreversible nature of a 721 contribution.

### DST Bridge

Using a DST to reach a REIT when a direct 1031 into a REIT does not work.

### Liquidity Path

Units → shares → cash, the route to accessing wealth.

### Distributions

Income paid on OP units, comparable to REIT dividends.

### Suitability Review

The assessment of whether a 721 fits the investor’s circumstances.

### Accredited Investor

The status typically required for the securities involved.

### REIT Acceptance

The REIT’s willingness to accept a property, often the binding constraint.

### Tired Landlord

A common 721 candidate who is ready to stop managing property.
