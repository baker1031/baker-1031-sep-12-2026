# Commercial vs. Residential Mortgage REITs

I have been looking at how quickly a high dividend can pull attention away from the balance sheet that supports it. Mortgage REITs are a clean example. The yield is visible. The rate, credit, funding, and leverage risks underneath it require more work.

Mortgage REITs, or mREITs, do not generally own buildings and collect rent the way equity REITs do. They finance real estate by holding mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. Their business is to borrow, invest in higher-yielding mortgage assets, and earn the difference between the asset yield and their cost of borrowing.

First-order thinking is, “Which mREIT has the highest yield?” Second-order thinking is, “What type of mortgage assets produce that yield, how much leverage is involved, can the spread hold, and is the principal risk a rate move, a refinancing wave, or a borrower default?” The label “mortgage REIT” tells only part of the story.

Residential and commercial mortgage REITs finance different property and carry different dominant risks. Residential mREITs invest in home and apartment mortgages and residential mortgage-backed securities (RMBS). Commercial mREITs invest in commercial mortgages, commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS), and transitional or bridge loans on income-producing property. Both use leverage. Both can be more volatile and higher risk than an equity REIT.

This is educational information, not investment advice. Verify current details and evaluate each REIT on its own terms.

## Two kinds of mortgage REITs

The common engine is the net interest spread. A mortgage REIT borrows at one rate, buys or originates higher-yielding mortgage assets, and earns the difference. It generally distributes most of that income as dividends under the REIT rules.

Leverage enlarges the engine. A mortgage REIT can borrow several dollars for every dollar of equity, use the borrowed funds to own more mortgage assets, and multiply the spread earned on its equity. That is one reason mREIT yields can be high. It is also why book value and dividends can move sharply when the asset value or funding cost changes.

The key distinction is what the REIT finances:

- A **residential mortgage REIT** owns residential mortgages and RMBS on houses and apartments.
- A **commercial mortgage REIT** owns or originates commercial mortgages, CMBS, and loans on income-producing commercial property such as offices, hotels, retail, industrial, and multifamily property.

Residential mREITs are mainly shaped by interest-rate and prepayment conditions. Commercial mREITs are mainly shaped by commercial-property credit conditions. The underlying real estate sets the risk profile.

## Residential mREITs: agency versus non-agency

Within residential mREITs, the important split is agency versus non-agency.

**Agency mREITs** invest in RMBS guaranteed by government-sponsored entities such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, or by a government agency such as Ginnie Mae. The guarantee means credit risk is minimal: if a homeowner defaults, the guarantee covers principal. The investor has not eliminated risk; the primary exposure has shifted away from homeowner default.

Agency mREITs commonly borrow short term to hold longer-term, fixed-rate mortgage securities. Rising rates can reduce the value of those holdings and compress the net interest spread. Falling rates can produce a different problem: homeowners refinance or pay off mortgages early, returning principal when prevailing yields are lower. The REIT then has to reinvest at less attractive rates. That is prepayment risk.

**Non-agency mREITs** hold residential mortgages or RMBS without a government guarantee. They retain the interest-rate and prepayment exposures and add credit risk: the risk that borrowers default and the REIT bears the loss. In return, non-agency assets can offer higher yields.

An agency mREIT may have almost no credit risk because the loans are guaranteed, but it can still have large interest-rate and prepayment exposure. A non-agency mREIT adds credit risk to the mix. Both are leveraged, and leverage can make book value move sharply.

## Commercial mortgage REITs

Commercial mREITs finance income-producing commercial real estate through originated and held commercial mortgages, CMBS, and bridge loans. A bridge loan is a shorter-term loan on a property being repositioned, renovated, or lease-stabilized. Rather than financing thousands of small home loans, a commercial mREIT often holds larger loans tied to individual commercial properties or pools of them.

The central risk is commercial-property credit risk. A borrower may default if property income falls short because of vacancy, falling rents, or an inability to refinance. If the collateral is not worth enough to repay the loan, the lender has a loss. Commercial mREIT results therefore depend heavily on property income, occupancy, refinancing markets, and the health of the property sectors to which they are exposed.

Many commercial mREITs make floating-rate loans. A floating-rate loan resets its interest rate as benchmark rates move, reducing pure fixed-rate duration exposure. That can make commercial mREITs less exposed to rate-duration risk than agency mREITs holding longer-term fixed-rate securities. But it does not make rate changes irrelevant. Higher rates can raise a borrower’s debt-service costs, make refinancing harder when a loan matures, raise the REIT’s own borrowing cost, and narrow the spread. In other words, the rate question often returns as a credit question.

Commercial mREITs are, at their core, leveraged commercial-real-estate credit investments.

## The risk profiles compared

An agency residential mREIT generally carries minimal credit risk but concentrated rate and prepayment risk. Its results can move with the yield curve, rate volatility, and refinancing waves. It often uses substantial leverage to earn a competitive return from low-credit-risk assets, so even without widespread defaults, book value can swing.

A commercial mREIT generally carries commercial-property credit risk. Its results depend on whether borrowers can service and repay loans, which depends on occupancy, property income, rents, valuations, and access to refinancing. Floating-rate loans can reduce duration risk, but they can also increase debt-service burdens on borrowers when rates rise.

A non-agency residential mREIT sits between those profiles. It has residential rate and prepayment risk, plus residential credit risk.

Every type uses leverage. That leverage magnifies gains and losses, making mREITs generally more volatile and higher risk than equity REITs that own buildings and collect rent. The comparison is not simply residential versus commercial; it is rate and prepayment risk versus credit risk, or both.

## Leverage and the interest-rate spread

The spread-and-leverage model explains both the appeal and the vulnerability. An mREIT often borrows short term, including through repurchase agreements, and invests the proceeds in higher-yielding mortgage assets. The gap between asset yield and borrowing cost is the net interest spread.

If short-term borrowing costs rise relative to asset yields, the spread narrows. A flat or inverted yield curve can make that worse. When the assets themselves lose value—because rates rise for an agency mREIT or because commercial credit deteriorates for a commercial mREIT—leverage magnifies the reduction in book value.

Funding structure matters as well. A decline in asset values can prompt margin calls under short-term funding arrangements. The REIT may then have to sell assets at depressed prices to meet lender demands, locking in losses. That is why a headline dividend is not bond-like income. A mortgage REIT dividend can be cut when income or book value declines.

Mortgage REITs can generate high yields because the spread is leveraged. The same leverage magnifies losses when spreads compress or assets fall. That is the essential tradeoff.

## How to choose between them

There is no categorically safer mREIT type. The choice is about which risk an investor is prepared to bear and how that exposure fits the broader portfolio.

- An **agency residential mREIT** can fit an investor who wants minimal credit risk and can accept interest-rate and prepayment volatility.
- A **commercial mREIT**, especially one with floating-rate loans, can fit an investor who prefers commercial-property credit exposure to fixed-rate duration exposure and has a constructive view on commercial real estate fundamentals.
- A **non-agency residential mREIT** can fit an investor seeking residential credit exposure and potentially higher yield while accepting both rate/prepayment and default risk.

High yield is compensation for risk, not a free lunch. Dividends can be cut, book value can swing, and leverage can turn a modest adverse move into a large equity loss. Position size and diversification across types can help manage concentration, but they do not remove the risk.

## Key takeaways

- Mortgage REITs split into residential financing for homes and apartments and commercial financing for income-producing property.
- Agency residential mREITs are government backed, have minimal credit risk, and are chiefly exposed to interest-rate and prepayment risk. Non-agency mREITs add credit risk.
- Commercial mREITs are chiefly exposed to commercial-property credit risk and often use floating-rate loans, which reduce duration exposure while emphasizing credit and refinancing conditions.
- All mortgage REITs use leverage, which amplifies returns and losses and makes them more volatile and higher risk than equity REITs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a mortgage REIT?

A mortgage REIT finances real estate rather than owning property directly. It holds mortgages and mortgage-backed securities, earns the net interest spread between asset income and borrowing cost, and generally distributes most of that income as dividends. It often uses leverage—several dollars borrowed per equity dollar—to enlarge the portfolio. Residential mREITs finance homes and apartments through mortgages and RMBS; commercial mREITs finance commercial property through mortgages, CMBS, and bridge loans. It is a leveraged real-estate-debt investment, not a property owner.

### What is the difference between residential and commercial mortgage REITs?

Residential mREITs invest in home/apartment mortgages and RMBS. Their main exposures are interest rates and prepayments, with credit risk added for non-agency assets. Commercial mREITs finance income-producing property through commercial mortgages, CMBS, and bridge loans. Their principal exposure is commercial-property credit—borrower defaults, property income, occupancy, collateral values, and refinancing. Both earn a spread and use leverage.

### What is an agency mortgage REIT?

An agency mREIT is a residential mREIT holding RMBS guaranteed by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, or Ginnie Mae. The guarantee means minimal credit risk, because principal is covered after default. The main risks are interest-rate risk and prepayment risk: short-term borrowing against longer-term fixed-rate assets can lose value when rates rise, and falling rates can cause early refinancing and lower-yield reinvestment. Leverage can make book value volatile.

### What is the difference between agency and non-agency mortgage REITs?

Agency and non-agency mREITs are both residential. Agency mREITs hold guaranteed RMBS and have minimal credit risk but concentrated interest-rate and prepayment risk. Non-agency mREITs hold unguaranteed mortgages or RMBS, so they also bear borrower-default risk. The additional credit risk can bring higher yields, but both types remain leveraged and more volatile than equity REITs.

### What is a commercial mortgage REIT?

A commercial mREIT finances offices, hotels, retail centers, industrial property, and multifamily property through commercial mortgages, CMBS, and bridge loans. The loans are often larger individual-property loans or pools. Its central risk is that property income, occupancy, rents, or refinancing fail to support the borrower’s debt service and the collateral value does not cover the loan. Floating-rate lending may reduce duration exposure, but leverage and commercial credit risk remain.

### What is CMBS?

CMBS means commercial mortgage-backed securities: bonds backed by pools of commercial mortgages on income-producing property. The securities are often sold in tranches with different priority of payment. Junior tranches bear more credit risk and may have higher yield; senior tranches have more protection. A commercial mREIT can use CMBS to hold commercial-real-estate credit exposure in securitized form.

### Why are mortgage REITs riskier than equity REITs?

Mortgage REITs borrow heavily to amplify their spread, so leverage magnifies losses as well as gains. Agency mREITs are sensitive to rates, yield curves, and prepayments. Commercial mREITs are sensitive to property-credit conditions. Many mREITs also use short-term funding, including repurchase agreements; falling asset values can create margin calls and forced sales. Equity REITs own buildings and collect rent, which is generally a less leveraged structure. High yields are compensation for genuine risk, and dividends and book values can be volatile.

### What is prepayment risk in a mortgage REIT?

Prepayment risk is the risk that homeowners refinance or pay off their mortgages earlier than expected. It mainly affects residential mREITs, especially agency mREITs. When rates fall, early principal returns can force the REIT to reinvest at lower yields, reducing expected income and compressing the spread.

### How do mortgage REITs make money?

They borrow money, often short term through repurchase agreements, and invest in higher-yielding mortgage assets. The gap between what the assets earn and what funding costs is the net interest spread. Leverage multiplies the spread earned on equity and can support high dividends, but it also magnifies the impact of spread compression and falling asset values.

### Are commercial mortgage REITs affected by interest rates?

Yes. Floating-rate commercial loans reset as benchmark rates move, so they can reduce pure duration exposure compared with longer-term fixed-rate agency RMBS. But higher rates can raise a borrower’s debt-service cost, strain property cash flow, make refinancing harder at maturity, raise the REIT’s own funding costs, and affect the spread. The main effect is often through commercial credit and refinancing rather than fixed-rate duration.

### Which is safer, a residential or commercial mortgage REIT?

Neither is universally safer. An agency residential mREIT has minimal credit risk but concentrated rate, prepayment, and leverage risk. A commercial mREIT has commercial-property credit risk and often less duration exposure when loans float. A non-agency residential mREIT has both rate/prepayment and credit risk. The answer depends on the environment, an investor’s view of rates versus credit, and risk tolerance. Sizing and diversification can help manage exposure but do not eliminate risk.

### Do mortgage REITs pay high dividends?

They often do, sometimes more than equity REITs, because a leveraged spread can produce substantial income and REIT distribution rules require at least 90% of taxable income to be distributed to shareholders. That income is not guaranteed. Higher borrowing costs, a flat yield curve, falling asset values, or credit deterioration can reduce income and book value. Dividends can then be cut. Treat a high yield as variable and risk bearing, not as a bond coupon.

### How does leverage affect mortgage REITs?

Leverage lets an mREIT borrow several dollars for every dollar of equity and buy more mortgage assets, multiplying the spread earned on equity. It also magnifies losses. Rising rates can reduce agency-asset values; credit losses can reduce commercial-asset values. Short-term repurchase-agreement funding can create margin calls if values fall, forcing sales at depressed prices. A narrower spread can further pressure earnings.

### How should I choose between mortgage REIT types?

Start with the risk you prefer to bear. Agency residential mREITs minimize credit risk but accept rate and prepayment volatility. Commercial mREITs emphasize commercial-property credit, often with floating-rate loans. Non-agency residential mREITs carry both risk sets. All are leveraged and higher risk, so the allocation should be sized to risk tolerance and an investor can consider diversification rather than concentrating on one type.

## Glossary

- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT that finances real estate and earns an interest-rate spread.
- **Residential Mortgage REIT:** An mREIT investing in residential mortgages and RMBS on homes and apartments.
- **Commercial Mortgage REIT:** An mREIT financing commercial property through mortgages, CMBS, and bridge loans.
- **Agency mREIT:** A residential mREIT holding government-guaranteed RMBS with minimal credit risk.
- **Non-Agency mREIT:** A residential mREIT holding unguaranteed mortgages and added credit risk.
- **RMBS:** Residential mortgage-backed securities, bonds backed by home loans.
- **CMBS:** Commercial mortgage-backed securities, bonds backed by commercial mortgages.
- **Net Interest Spread:** The gap between asset yield and borrowing cost that drives mREIT income.
- **Interest-Rate Risk:** Risk that a rate move compresses the spread or reduces asset values.
- **Prepayment Risk:** Risk that homeowners refinance early and force lower-yield reinvestment.
- **Credit Risk:** Risk that borrowers default and the mREIT bears the loss, especially in non-agency and commercial assets.
- **Bridge Loan:** A short-term commercial loan for a property being repositioned or stabilized.
- **Floating-Rate Loan:** A loan whose rate adjusts with benchmarks, reducing duration risk.
- **Leverage:** Borrowing that amplifies the portfolio, the spread, and losses.
- **Repurchase Agreement:** Short-term borrowing mREITs often use to fund portfolios.
- **Book Value:** An mREIT’s net asset value, which leverage can make volatile.

## Commercial vs. residential mortgage REITs at a glance

| Factor | Residential mREIT | Commercial mREIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Underlying loans | Home mortgages and MBS | Loans on commercial property |
| Agency versus non-agency | Often agency guaranteed with lower credit risk | Direct credit exposure to borrowers |
| Primary risk | Interest-rate and prepayment risk | Credit and property-value risk |
| Leverage | Typically high | Moderate to high |
| Income driver | Spread on borrowed funds versus MBS yield | Spread on commercial loan yields |
| Rate sensitivity | High; spread can compress quickly | Present, but more credit driven |
| Best for | Rate-spread, lower-credit-risk income | Commercial-credit yield with property exposure |

Agency-backed residential mREITs emphasize interest-rate and prepayment risk over credit risk, which can appeal to an investor seeking mortgage-spread income with lower default exposure. Commercial mREITs trade some of that rate sensitivity for direct exposure to commercial borrowers. Both still rely on the spread between borrowing costs and loan yields. Before choosing, understand which risk is being paid for.
