# 1031 Exchange Into Oil & Gas, Minerals & Royalties


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I find that the most interesting 1031 decisions start with a category error: assuming replacement property must be another building. It does not. Perpetual mineral and royalty interests are real property, which lets a real-estate investor exchange into oil and gas and defer gain while adding an asset class with different drivers.

First-order thinking sees another source of yield and the tax deferral. Second-order thinking asks what actually qualifies, whether the interest is perpetual, how production decline and commodity prices change the income stream, and whether a small allocation is enough. A royalty can be a useful diversifier. It is not a substitute for underwriting the risk that comes with it.

This guide covers why minerals and royalties can be like-kind property, why perpetual royalties are the cleanest fit, how royalty-pool DSTs package the opportunity, what diversification does and does not solve, and how to investigate the option inside the exchange timeline.

## Key Takeaways

- Perpetual mineral and royalty interests are like-kind real property, so real-estate investors can exchange into oil and gas.

- Perpetual royalties are passive and cost-free, have well-settled authority, and avoid a working interest’s equipment carve-out.

- Royalty-pool DSTs package diversified royalties as turnkey, qualifying replacement property and are the practical route for most investors.

- A royalty allocation can add energy exposure and depletion-sheltered income alongside real estate, but it can be a partial allocation rather than an all-or-nothing move.

## Minerals & royalties as like-kind

The starting point is the property’s character. Under the law of most producing states, minerals in the ground and rights in them are real property. The IRS generally follows that characterization. Because real-property like-kind analysis looks at investment real estate’s character rather than its particular asset type, a perpetual mineral or royalty interest is like-kind to an apartment building, office tower, land, or other investment real property.

That creates a two-way bridge. An owner selling a rental property or commercial building can reinvest in perpetual royalty interests, directly or through a vehicle holding them, and defer gain as with another building. A mineral owner can exchange into real estate for the same reason: both are real property held for investment.

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act limited Section 1031 to real property. It did not change the real-property character of perpetual minerals and royalties. What it removed was personal-property exchanges, including the equipment component attached to a working interest. The focus here is royalties because they tend to be the most accessible and clearest oil-and-gas replacement property for a real-estate investor.

## Why royalties qualify cleanly

Among mineral interests, a perpetual royalty is generally the cleanest 1031 fit. It is a cost-free right to a share of production: the owner receives a percentage off the top and does not bear drilling, operating, or environmental costs. There are no operating liabilities and no equipment to carve out, unlike a working interest whose equipment is non-qualifying personal property.

The word **perpetual** does the important work. A perpetual royalty continues for the productive life of the minerals rather than ending after a fixed term or cap. That open-ended duration is part of what the tax law associates with a real-property interest. Revenue Rulings 68-226 and 73-428 support the treatment of perpetual royalties as real property eligible for a like-kind exchange.

That clarity keeps a non-specialist from having to navigate the less clean alternatives. A working interest can involve equipment carve-outs. An override can depend on a lease’s duration. A production payment is treated as debt and is ineligible. Perpetual royalties, especially when held through a vetted vehicle, avoid those gray areas. They are the oil-and-gas asset with the clearest qualification, a passive cost-free profile, and no working-interest complications.

## Royalty DSTs as a turnkey option

For most investors, sourcing direct royalty interests is not a practical 45-day exercise. The mineral market is fragmented and opaque; there is no MLS for royalties. Finding, valuing, and vetting individual interests takes specialized work, particularly for a buyer without oil-and-gas expertise.

A royalty-pool Delaware Statutory Trust solves the sourcing problem by putting a diversified portfolio of perpetual royalties into a turnkey security. A sponsor assembles royalty interests across many wells, operators, and basins and sells fractional beneficial interests to an [accredited investor](insights.html). Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats the DST interest as direct ownership of underlying real property. Since the underlying royalties are real property, the royalty-pool DST can serve as like-kind 1031 replacement property.

The vehicle applies the familiar real-estate DST advantages to minerals. The sponsor handles sourcing and diligence. Pooling many royalty interests reduces single-well risk. The offering can close quickly enough to help with the deadline or as a backup replacement, and the investor has no management role. It also generally passes through depletion, preserving the tax-advantaged character of royalty income. For most real-estate-to-minerals exchanges, this is how the transaction is actually executed.

## Diversifying beyond real estate

The case for a mineral allocation is diversification, not a claim that energy is safer than real estate. A real-estate-heavy investor is exposed to property cycles, interest rates, and local economies. Royalties are driven by energy prices and production, which do not move in lockstep with real estate. Holding both can reduce the volatility of total income and net worth when a weak period in one asset class is offset by the other.

Royalties have other attributes distinct from rent. For qualifying owners, depletion often equals 15% of gross income and shelters part of that income, analogous to but different from depreciation. Royalties are also passive and cost-free. Those features make energy exposure a different source of income and risk, not simply another flavor of property ownership.

The allocation can be partial. An investor can reinvest one portion of proceeds in a royalty-pool DST and another in real estate or real-estate DSTs. The [identification rules, which allow multiple replacement properties](1031-exchange-into-multiple-replacement-properties.html), make that practical. Keeping a real-estate core while adding a measured royalty slice retains the portfolio’s existing exposure while adding energy, income, and tax features on a tax-deferred basis.

## Explore oil & gas replacement options

The first practical question is what is actually available. For most investors, the relevant choices are royalty-pool DSTs, not direct interests. Then the analysis is the same disciplined process used for any replacement property: What role would minerals serve—income, diversification, or both? How does the investment fit value and debt targets? Can it be identified inside the 45-day clock?

Because this is both a security and a specialist asset class, an advisor experienced in [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html)s and oil and gas can help compare offerings. The dimensions are sponsor track record, pool diversification, fees, distribution rate, depletion treatment, and the suitability review required for securities. Deeper resources offer [step-by-step structuring details for investors who want to go further](1031-exchange-into-oil-and-gas-step-by-step.html), including perpetual-interest rules, mineral-interest types, depletion mechanics, and royalty-pool DST structure.

The short version is straightforward. Perpetual royalties, usually through a royalty-pool DST, can be qualifying, turnkey, diversifying replacement property. An investor seeking a different dimension beyond real estate can explore whether that role fits the exchange rather than assuming the entire replacement amount belongs in minerals.

## Considerations before exchanging into minerals

Commodity-price exposure is the most obvious trade. Royalty income rises and falls with oil and gas prices, which are more volatile than stabilized real-estate rents. That difference provides the diversification benefit, but it also makes the income less predictable. Position sizing should reflect both sides of that fact.

Production decline is another mineral-specific issue. A producing royalty depletes as the well declines, and income falls over time absent new drilling. A royalty-pool DST can spread the exposure across wells at different stages, reducing single-well risk, but it cannot change the underlying physics. That differs from real estate, where income can be relatively steady or grow.

When accessed through a DST, the interest is also an illiquid security. Sponsor dependence, fees, lack of control, and the risk of the underlying minerals remain. The right question is not whether royalties are universally attractive. It is whether commodity volatility, decline, illiquidity, and the specialized nature of oil and gas fit the investor’s goals and risk tolerance after a suitability review. An advisor who understands both minerals and 1031s can help make that assessment deliberately.

## How royalty income compares to real estate income

Stabilized rent is comparatively steady and can grow with inflation. Royalty income varies with production and commodity prices and normally declines over time as wells deplete. A royalty-pool DST’s income can be higher in strong periods but is lumpier, less predictable, and likely to decline without new drilling. It follows a different trajectory from rent.

The tax shelter differs too. Real-estate income is sheltered by depreciation; royalty income is sheltered by depletion. Both reduce the taxable portion of income, but they attach to different assets. Percentage depletion can continue after basis is recovered, which is a distinctive feature. A DST holding royalties generally passes that treatment through to investors.

This comparison argues for a measured allocation rather than a wholesale move for most investors. A real-estate core supplies steadier, inflation-sensitive income. A royalty slice adds a different risk driver, higher yield potential, and depletion shelter. The mix can smooth the overall portfolio while respecting the fact that the mineral income stream is unfamiliar and less predictable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I 1031 exchange real estate into oil and gas?

Yes. Perpetual mineral and royalty interests are real property, so they are like-kind to investment real estate. A seller can reinvest property-sale proceeds in perpetual royalties, typically through a royalty-pool DST, defer gain, and add diversified energy exposure and depletion-sheltered income inside the 1031 framework.

### Why do minerals and royalties qualify as like-kind?

Under the law of most producing states, minerals and their associated rights are real property, and the IRS generally follows that treatment. Like-kind analysis asks whether the interest is investment real property, not whether it is the same asset type. A perpetual mineral or royalty interest, an investment building, land, and a DST can therefore be like-kind property.

### Why do perpetual royalties qualify so cleanly?

A perpetual royalty is passive, cost-free real property with no operating liabilities and no equipment to carve out. Its open-ended duration provides the real-property character the tax law recognizes. Revenue Rulings 68-226 and 73-428 provide longstanding authority. That avoids the complications of working interests and makes perpetual royalties the cleanest oil-and-gas asset for a 1031.

### What is a royalty-pool DST?

It is a Delaware Statutory Trust that holds a diversified portfolio of perpetual oil-and-gas royalty interests and sells fractional interests to accredited investors. It is turnkey, passive, diversified across wells and basins, and can close quickly. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, the DST interest is treated as direct ownership of underlying real property, making a royalty-pool DST qualifying 1031 replacement property when the underlying interests qualify.

### Why use a royalty DST instead of buying minerals directly?

Direct minerals are difficult to find, value, and vet in a fragmented, opaque market, especially inside the 45-day identification period and without specialist knowledge. A royalty-pool DST packages vetted, diversified interests into one turnkey investment. It addresses the sourcing problem and reduces single-well risk, which is why it is the common route for a real-estate-to-minerals exchange.

### How does exchanging into minerals diversify my portfolio?

It adds exposure to energy prices and production, drivers that do not move in lockstep with real estate. That can reduce volatility in total income and net worth for a real-estate-heavy investor. Royalty income is also partly sheltered by depletion, analogous to real-estate depreciation. A measured mineral allocation adds a genuinely different asset class on a tax-deferred basis.

### Do I have to move all my proceeds into minerals?

No. An investor can exchange a portion into a royalty-pool DST and reinvest the rest in real estate or real-estate DSTs. Multiple replacement-property identification rules make a partial allocation straightforward. Most investors use minerals as a measured diversifier while retaining a real-estate core.

### Is royalty income sheltered from tax?

Partly. Qualifying royalty owners can generally claim depletion, often percentage depletion of 15% of gross income. It shelters part of the income, much as depreciation shelters part of real-estate income. A royalty-pool DST generally passes this treatment through to investors, which is one appeal of a royalty allocation.

### What are the risks of exchanging into oil and gas?

The core risks are commodity-price volatility, production-decline risk, and the security risks of a DST: illiquidity, sponsor dependence, fees, and lack of control. Diversifying across many wells can reduce single-well risk, but it does not remove the nature of the asset class. Allocation size and fit should be assessed with an advisor experienced in both 1031 exchanges and minerals.

### Do working interests qualify like royalties?

Only partly, and less cleanly. The real-property component of a working interest can qualify, but tangible equipment is non-qualifying personal property that must be carved out and taxed. Working interests also carry operating costs and liabilities. Perpetual royalties, usually accessed through a royalty-pool DST, are a simpler and cleaner choice for a real-estate investor.

### How do I explore exchanging into minerals?

Work with an advisor who understands both 1031 exchanges and oil and gas. The advisor can present vetted royalty-pool DST offerings, compare sponsor, diversification, fees, distribution rate, and depletion treatment, and conduct the required suitability review. The result should be a decision about whether minerals fit the diversified replacement and, if so, how much to allocate.

### Is exchanging into oil and gas right for every investor?

No. It can fit an investor seeking diversification beyond real estate who can accept commodity-price volatility, depletion-driven decline, and the security structure of a royalty-pool DST. It may not fit an investor who wants only real estate or greater stability. The tax-deferred energy exposure and depletion shelter do not eliminate the need for a suitability assessment before committing.

### How does royalty income compare to rental income?

Rental income from a stabilized property tends to be steadier and can grow with inflation. Royalty income varies with production and commodity prices, and it normally declines as wells deplete. A royalty-pool DST can offer higher yield in good periods, but its income is lumpier, less predictable, and subject to decline absent new drilling.

### Is royalty income taxed like rental income?

Both streams can receive a shelter, but the mechanism differs. Real-estate income is sheltered by depreciation; royalty income is sheltered by depletion. Each reduces taxable income on a different asset, and depletion can continue after basis is recovered. An investor adding royalties through a DST generally gains depletion treatment on that portion alongside depreciation treatment for real estate.

### Why add only a measured allocation to minerals?

Maintaining a real-estate core while adding a royalty slice combines steadier, inflation-sensitive real-estate income with a higher-yield, depletion-sheltered stream exposed to different drivers. The mix can bring diversification without overexposing the portfolio to commodity volatility and production decline. It is a way to capture the benefits without assuming minerals should replace the real-estate core.

## Glossary

**Mineral Interest** — Ownership of minerals beneath a tract; generally real property eligible for 1031.

**Royalty Interest** — A cost-free right to a share of production; perpetual royalties qualify as real property.

**Perpetual Royalty** — A royalty continuing for the life of production; the cleanest oil and gas asset for 1031.

**Like-Kind** — The standard requiring exchanged property to share the character of real property held for investment.

**Royalty-Pool DST** — A DST holding diversified mineral royalty interests as turnkey 1031 replacement property.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86** — The IRS ruling treating a DST interest as direct ownership of the underlying real property.

**Revenue Ruling 68-226** — IRS authority treating a perpetual oil and gas royalty as real property.

**Depletion** — A deduction sheltering part of royalty income; often 15% of gross income for qualifying owners.

**Working Interest** — An operating interest with costs and taxable equipment; less clean for 1031 than a royalty.

**Commodity-Price Exposure** — Sensitivity of royalty income to oil and gas prices, a diversification benefit and a risk.

**Decline Curve** — The projected decline in a well's production, causing royalty income to fall over time.

**Diversification** — Spreading capital across asset classes (real estate and energy) to reduce concentration.

**Accredited Investor** — An investor meeting SEC thresholds, eligible for royalty-pool DST offerings.

**Suitability Review** — The assessment that a securities product like a royalty-pool DST fits an investor.

**Cross-Asset Exchange** — A 1031 between different real-property asset classes, such as real estate and minerals.

**Broker-Dealer** — The firm through which royalty-pool DSTs are offered; Baker 1031's is Aurora Securities.
