# DST Distribution Yield vs. Total Return

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing projected distribution yield treated as the answer before anyone has asked what the entire investment is expected to return. It is an easy number to grasp. It is not the whole investment.

Distribution yield measures current cash flow. Total return combines distributions, appreciation when the property sells, and debt paydown that builds equity over the hold. A high yield may reflect return of capital or a payout the property cannot sustain; a lower-yield DST may produce a stronger overall outcome through appreciation and amortization. Every yield and return figure is a projection, not a guarantee. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

## What distribution yield measures

Distribution yield is the annual distribution divided by the price, or equity, invested. If an investor puts in $100,000 and a DST distributes $5,000 over a year, the distribution yield is 5%. That is a useful current-cash-flow measure for someone who needs to understand the income expected while the DST is held.

It does not show property appreciation or decline, principal paid down on the debt, or what an investor will receive when the property sells. Nor does it answer whether the distribution is sustainable or what it is made of. Two DSTs can display the same yield and produce very different total returns. Yield is a current-income snapshot, not the eventual result.

## Total return explained

Total return has three components: distributions, appreciation, and debt paydown. Distributions are the current cash flow measured by yield. Appreciation is an increase in the property’s value, realized when the sponsor sells at the end of the hold. Debt paydown, or amortization, reduces loan principal over the hold and builds equity that can add to sale proceeds even if property value remains flat.

In shorthand: total return = distributions + appreciation + debt paydown. It is commonly expressed as an IRR or equity multiple. IRR also accounts for timing. A DST paying a 4% yield can deliver a better total return than its yield implies when appreciation and amortization are meaningful. A DST with a 6% yield can deliver less—or lose money—if the property declines or the sale requires a higher exit cap rate.

First-order thinking compares 5%, 6%, or the largest indicated current payment. Second-order thinking separates each projected dollar of return into income, value change, and principal reduction, then asks what has to occur for those projections to hold.

> Yield is visible early. Appreciation and debt paydown are usually clear only at the finish line, but both belong in the return.

## Why yield alone misleads

Yield can mislead in two specific ways. First, a distribution may include return of capital: the investor is receiving some of their own money, rather than property earnings. That can raise a headline yield while reducing the capital still working in the investment.

Second, a distribution needs to be sustainable. A DST paying more than property income comfortably supports may be using reserves or relying on optimistic assumptions. Distributions can be reduced or suspended when income falls short. A high-yield DST with a weak exit can underperform a lower-yield DST with stronger appreciation and debt paydown. A large payout is not, by itself, proof of a strong investment.

## Appreciation and debt paydown

Appreciation is value growth realized when the sponsor sells. A property bought well, in a growing market, and operated more effectively may sell for more and add to total return. Appreciation is not guaranteed. Values can fall, and the exit cap rate has a major effect on the sale result.

Debt paydown is more mechanical. With amortizing financing, loan payments reduce principal during a multi-year hold. At sale, a lower loan balance leaves more proceeds for investors as equity, even if the property’s value is unchanged. The property’s cash flow is, in effect, paying down the mortgage and creating a form of forced savings.

That contribution does not appear in distribution yield. A lower yield can reflect a conservative payout or income retained in the property while value growth and principal reduction build equity. Leverage also creates risk and can amplify losses when property value falls, so amortization is not a free benefit.

## Key Takeaways

- Distribution yield is annual distribution divided by price; it measures current cash flow, not the entire return.
- Total return combines distributions, appreciation, and debt paydown. Yield captures only the first component.
- A high yield can include return of capital or be unsustainable; a lower-yield DST can have the better total return.
- Judge projected performance through total return, its components, and the assumptions supporting each component—not the yield alone.

## Evaluating true performance

Start with projected total-return measures: IRR, which weights cash flows by timing, and sometimes equity multiple, the total dollars returned per dollar invested. Those measures incorporate distributions, appreciation, and debt paydown.

Then inspect the composition. How much depends on income, appreciation, or amortization? Income-heavy returns depend on leasing and property cash flow. Appreciation-heavy returns depend on exit conditions. Check whether a current distribution includes return of capital and whether projected income can sustain it. Examine exit assumptions, particularly the exit cap rate. Compare total return with total return using comparable assumptions, rather than comparing one DST’s yield against another’s.

Stress-test the assumptions. A model is only as dependable as its rents, occupancy, expenses, financing, and exit inputs. The useful question is not merely “What does this DST yield?” It is “What is the projected total return, where does it come from, and how well supported are the assumptions?”

## Matching the metric to your goal

An income-focused investor has a legitimate reason to care about sustainable distribution yield: it estimates cash flow available during the hold. But total return still tests whether that income is preserving or eroding capital. A high distribution that partly returns principal can feel like income while the capital base shrinks.

An investor aiming to build wealth over the hold will likely give total return more weight, because appreciation and debt paydown may matter more than current distributions. The disciplined approach is to use yield to confirm that current income fits the need and total return to check whether the payout is sustainable and the capital is being built, not consumed. Neither metric works well in isolation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a DST's distribution yield?

It is the annual distribution divided by invested price or equity, expressed as a percentage. An investment of $100,000 that receives $5,000 over one year has a 5% distribution yield. It measures current cash flow, not appreciation, debt paydown, or sale proceeds. Two DSTs with the same yield can therefore have different total returns. Use yield as the income slice and pair it with total-return measures.

### What is total return in a DST?

Total return is distributions plus appreciation plus debt paydown. Distributions provide current cash flow; appreciation is value growth realized at sale; amortizing debt reduces principal and adds equity to sale proceeds. IRR expresses this combined return as an annualized, timing-aware measure; an equity multiple shows dollars returned per dollar invested. Yield covers only distributions, the first component.

### Why can't I judge a DST by its yield alone?

Yield can include return of capital, which pays back an investor’s own money and can inflate the figure while reducing working equity. It can also be unsustainable if property income does not support the distribution. It ignores appreciation and debt paydown, so a high-yield DST with a weak exit may trail a lower-yield DST with stronger total-return drivers. Treat yield as one input, not the verdict.

### What is return of capital in a DST distribution?

It is the portion of a distribution that returns the investor’s own invested money rather than property earnings. It can make a yield appear more generous than the property’s income supports, reduces cost basis and working equity, and is not free income merely because it may not be currently taxed. The tax treatment is technical; confirm it with a CPA.

### How do appreciation and debt paydown affect DST returns?

Appreciation is an increase in property value realized at sale and depends heavily on the exit cap rate; it is not guaranteed. Debt paydown occurs when amortizing loan payments reduce principal using property cash flow, leaving more sale proceeds for investors even if value is flat. Both add to total return without appearing in yield, which is why a lower-yield DST can outperform a higher-yield alternative.

### Can a lower-yield DST have a higher total return?

Yes. A lower distribution can reflect a conservative payout, income retained in the property, or a value-building strategy. Strong appreciation and meaningful amortization can make its IRR and equity multiple higher than a high-yield DST whose property has limited value growth, a higher exit cap rate, return-of-capital support, or an unsustainable distribution. Compare projected total return and stress-test the inputs.

### What is IRR and how does it relate to total return?

IRR, internal rate of return, expresses total return as a single annualized rate while accounting for when cash flows occur. It combines distributions during the hold, debt paydown, and sale proceeds. A dollar received sooner counts more than one received later. IRR is more complete than current yield, but a projected IRR is still the output of assumptions, not a promise. Use it with yield and equity multiple.

### Is a high distribution yield a red flag?

Not automatically. It may be supported by strong, durable property income. But it warrants investigation: is some cash return of capital, is the payout supported by projected net income, is it using reserves, and are the underlying assumptions stretched? A high yield is a question to investigate rather than a conclusion. Read it alongside total return and the property assumptions.

### How do I find a DST's total return, not just its yield?

Read the offering’s projected IRR and, where provided, equity multiple. Then determine how much return comes from income, appreciation, and debt paydown; whether distributions include return of capital; whether the payout is sustainable; and what exit cap rate supports appreciation. Compare like with like and stress-test assumptions. A broker-dealer can help locate and interpret these figures.

### Does debt paydown really add to my return?

Yes, if the DST has amortizing financing. Each loan payment includes principal, so the outstanding balance falls over the hold through property rental cash flow. The lower balance means more sale proceeds can flow to investors as equity even without property appreciation. This forced-savings contribution is outside the distribution yield. It comes with leverage’s risks, including amplified losses if value declines.

### Should an income-focused investor still care about total return?

Yes. Current yield matters for income needs, but total return shows whether the yield is sustainable and whether capital is being preserved or eroded. It also shows whether value is rising or falling, which can affect future distributions and sale proceeds. Use total return as a sanity check on yield and make sure the projected income actually fits the investor’s needs.

### Are DST yields and returns guaranteed?

No. Distribution yields, IRRs, and equity multiples are projections based on occupancy, rents, expenses, financing, and sale assumptions. Distributions can be reduced or suspended, the property can sell for more or less than projected—or at a loss—and debt paydown depends on financing and debt service. Past sponsor or asset-class performance does not guarantee future results. Scrutinize and stress-test projections with suitability in mind.

### How should I compare two DSTs with different yields?

Compare projected IRR and equity multiple, return composition, distribution sustainability, and comparable rent-growth, occupancy, and exit-cap-rate assumptions. A higher projected return may reflect rosier assumptions rather than a stronger asset. Ideally, stress-test both with the same conservative inputs. The lower-yield DST may be the better choice if its total return is stronger and better supported. A broker-dealer can help put the offerings on consistent footing and confirm suitability.

### How is a DST's total return realized, and when?

Distributions of net rental income are received during the hold, often monthly. Appreciation and debt-paydown equity are realized when the sponsor sells the property and the investor receives a share of sale proceeds. The hold commonly lasts around five to seven years. A DST relying on appreciation leaves more of its return for an uncertain exit; an income-driven DST returns more along the way. The complete total return cannot be known until sale.

## Glossary

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding income-producing real estate as fractional interests.

**Distribution Yield:** Annual distribution divided by price — a current-cash-flow measure.

**Total Return:** Distributions plus appreciation plus debt paydown over the hold.

**Distributions:** Periodic payments of net rental income to DST investors.

**Appreciation:** Any increase in the property's value, realized at sale.

**Debt Paydown (Amortization):** Loan principal repaid over the hold, building equity.

**Return of Capital:** A distribution returning your own invested money, not earnings.

**Sustainability:** Whether a distribution can be maintained by the property's income.

**IRR (Internal Rate of Return):** Time-weighted total return expressed as an annualized rate.

**Equity Multiple:** Total dollars returned divided by dollars invested (ignores timing).

**Exit Cap Rate:** The capitalization rate assumed for the future sale price.

**Forced Savings:** Equity built as tenants' rent pays down the DST's loan principal.

**Cost Basis:** Your investment amount, reduced by any return of capital.

**Leverage:** The financing a DST uses, which adds debt paydown and amplifies risk.

**Net Operating Income (NOI):** Property income after operating expenses, before debt service.

**Hold Period:** A DST's multi-year term (often ~5-7 years) before the property sells.

## Distribution yield vs. total return at a glance

| Factor | Distribution Yield | Total Return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it measures | Annual distributions divided by investment | All sources of return combined |
| Includes appreciation? | No | Yes |
| Includes debt paydown? | No | Yes |
| Captures return of capital? | Can be obscured and may overstate income | Accounts for it |
| Time frame | Snapshot, period by period | Full hold, including exit |
| Risk if used alone | Mistakes a high payout for strong performance | Requires estimating the eventual sale |
| Best for judging | Current cash flow | True overall performance |

## Which should you choose?

Use distribution yield to gauge current income, but not as the sole measure: a high payout can include return of capital and excludes appreciation and debt paydown. Use total return to assess whether the investment built wealth through distributions, value change, and debt paydown over the full hold. Yield is especially relevant to spending needs; total return is the fuller measure of investment performance.
