# BDCs vs. REITs

I keep seeing income investments grouped together because their distribution yields look alike on a screen. That is a reasonable first look. It is not enough to make the investment decision.

A Business Development Company, or BDC, and a Real Estate Investment Trust, or REIT, can share a pass-through income structure and still own entirely different assets. A REIT owns or finances income-producing real estate. A BDC lends to, and sometimes owns stakes in, private operating companies. One collects rent or real-estate-backed interest; the other collects interest from business borrowers.

First-order thinking says both vehicles distribute income, so the higher yield wins. Second-order thinking starts with the contents of the wrapper: What creates the cash flow? What happens to that cash flow when rates move, property occupancy weakens, or a borrower cannot repay? What do leverage, price, and liquidity do to the outcome? Those questions matter more than a yield quoted in isolation.

## What a BDC is

A BDC is a closed-end investment company created by Congress in 1980 to provide capital to small and mid-sized private companies. These are often businesses that are too large for a typical bank loan, yet too small or too private to access public capital markets easily. A BDC raises investor capital and deploys it primarily as loans, with some equity stakes in certain cases. It is a way to gain private-credit and some private-equity exposure. It is not a real estate investment.

Structurally, a BDC is a regulated investment company under the Investment Company Act of 1940. Like a REIT, it can use pass-through tax treatment when it distributes at least 90% of its taxable income to shareholders, generally avoiding entity-level corporate tax and passing income through to investors.

BDCs can be publicly traded—listed on an exchange and liquid—or non-traded: unlisted, illiquid, and periodically priced at net asset value, or NAV. Non-traded BDCs are offered through broker-dealers to accredited or otherwise suitable investors after a suitability review.

The common structure can be useful shorthand, but it does not turn business lending into property ownership. A BDC is the private-credit counterpart to a REIT: a vehicle that finances private companies rather than owning buildings.

## BDC vs. REIT holdings

The simplest comparison is to inspect what each vehicle holds.

An equity REIT owns physical, income-producing property: apartments, warehouses, shopping centers, data centers, medical buildings, and other real estate. A mortgage REIT, or mREIT, holds real-estate debt, including mortgages and mortgage-backed securities. Either way, the underlying asset is real property or a loan secured by it. The income engine is rent or mortgage interest tied to that real estate.

A BDC holds private-company debt and, sometimes, equity. Its portfolio commonly includes floating-rate senior secured loans to small and mid-sized private businesses. Those loans often sit near the top of a borrower’s capital structure, alongside occasional equity stakes. A BDC investor therefore owns a share of a pool of private business loans, not a pool of buildings. Borrowers may be in software, healthcare services, manufacturing, or consumer businesses—sectors with no necessary connection to real estate.

The wrapper may look familiar. The assets inside it are not: a REIT contains buildings and mortgages; a BDC contains loans to private operating companies.

## Income and risk profiles

REIT income comes from rents for an equity REIT or mortgage interest for an mREIT. Equity-REIT income can be relatively stable when leases and occupancy are stable. Its main exposures are real estate risk—falling rents, occupancy, or property values—and interest-rate risk. Higher rates can pressure property valuations and, for mortgage REITs, the rate spread.

BDC income comes from interest on loans to private companies. Many of those loans are floating rate, so BDC income often rises as short-term rates rise and declines as they decline. That is one reason BDC yields can be high. It is not free income: the yield reflects the credit risk of lending to private, often leveraged companies.

The dominant BDC risks are credit and business risk. Borrowers can default, particularly in a recession. BDCs themselves often borrow to amplify returns, so leverage can magnify gains and losses. Private loans also require valuation estimates rather than exchange-traded prices. Rising rates can increase interest income, but they also increase borrowers’ interest expense, which can increase credit stress.

REIT fortunes are linked to property fundamentals and rates. BDC fortunes are linked to whether private-company borrowers can service and repay their debt. That difference is why the two may diversify one another, but neither should be treated as a substitute for the other.

## Tax treatment differences

Both BDCs and REITs are pass-through income vehicles. Each generally distributes roughly 90% or more of its income so it can avoid entity-level corporate tax, leaving income mainly taxable at the shareholder level. Their distributions are largely ordinary income, but the character and treatment of each distribution are not identical.

Qualified REIT dividends benefit from the 20% Section 199A deduction. BDC distributions mostly reflect loan interest and are also generally ordinary income; some BDCs may designate amounts eligible for the 199A deduction, but the treatment can vary by BDC and by year. Both vehicles can also pass through capital-gain or return-of-capital components.

REITs report distribution character on Form 1099-DIV. The applicable reporting and tax result are technical and fact-specific. Read the reporting for the particular vehicle and consult your CPA rather than assuming that a BDC and a REIT receive identical treatment.

## Key takeaways

- A BDC is a closed-end investment company that lends to and invests in small and mid-sized private companies. It holds private-company loans and equity, not real estate.
- REITs earn rent or mortgage interest and carry real-estate and interest-rate risk. BDCs earn loan interest, often at floating rates, and carry corporate-credit, business, and leverage risk.
- Both generally distribute roughly 90% or more of income, but Section 199A treatment and distribution character can differ. Confirm the details with your CPA.
- Choosing between them is an asset-allocation decision: real-estate exposure through a REIT or private-credit exposure through a BDC. Some portfolios use both for separate roles.

## Non-traded BDCs and non-traded REITs

The non-traded forms of BDCs and REITs have closely related structural trade-offs. Both are registered with the SEC but not listed on an exchange, so their shares do not trade daily at a market price. They are typically priced periodically at NAV and offer limited, redemption-based liquidity that is commonly capped and may be gated. They are sold through broker-dealers to accredited or otherwise suitable investors following a suitability review.

An investor may consider either structure for access to a private strategy without daily mark-to-market price swings: private credit in a BDC and private real estate in a REIT. The exchange is illiquidity and a longer holding period. Periodic appraisal-based pricing can lag real markets, fees matter, and a multi-year holding plan should be realistic before money is committed.

If you understand the non-traded REIT structure, you understand much of a non-traded BDC’s structure: NAV pricing, capped redemptions, suitability gating, and illiquidity. The decisive difference remains the asset class inside—private-company loans versus real estate.

## Choosing between them

There is no universal answer to which is “better.” The choice is about the exposure you want and the job it performs in a portfolio.

Choose a REIT when the objective is real estate exposure: income from rents or mortgages, participation in property values, and an allocation that has historically behaved somewhat differently from stocks and bonds. Choose a BDC when the objective is private-credit exposure: income from lending to private companies, often with floating-rate characteristics that can benefit as short-term rates rise.

Many investors hold both as separate sleeves: a real-estate allocation and a private-credit allocation. A BDC’s high yield should be read with its corporate-credit and leverage risk, just as a REIT’s income should be read with property and rate risk. Size each allocation to your risk tolerance. For non-traded vehicles, confirm that you can accept the limited liquidity before investing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a BDC?

A Business Development Company is a closed-end investment company created by Congress in 1980 to provide capital to small and mid-sized private companies. It raises money from investors and makes primarily loans, sometimes adding equity stakes. A BDC is regulated under the Investment Company Act of 1940 and can generally avoid entity-level corporate tax when it distributes at least 90% of its taxable income. BDCs are available in publicly traded, listed and liquid forms, and non-traded, unlisted, illiquid, NAV-priced forms. Their assets are private-company loans, not buildings.

### How is a BDC different from a REIT?

The difference is what each holds. A REIT owns or finances real estate: an equity REIT holds physical income-producing property and earns rent, while an mREIT holds mortgages and earns real-estate-linked interest. A BDC holds loans to and equity in small and mid-sized private operating companies, earning loan interest rather than rent. Both are generally pass-through vehicles distributing roughly 90% or more of income, but a REIT brings real-estate and rate exposure while a BDC brings corporate-credit and leverage exposure.

### What does a BDC actually hold?

A BDC holds private-company debt and equity. Its portfolio often consists of floating-rate senior secured loans near the top of the borrower’s capital structure, plus some equity interests. Its borrowers can include software, healthcare-services, manufacturing, business-services, and consumer companies. A BDC’s income and risk depend on those businesses paying interest and principal, unlike a REIT, whose portfolio consists of property or mortgages secured by property.

### Do BDCs have higher yields than REITs?

BDCs often have high yields, reflecting the credit risk of lending to private, often leveraged businesses and the floating-rate character of many loans. REIT yields can also be high because of the distribution requirement, but they are driven by rent or mortgage interest. Neither vehicle is universally higher yielding. A higher BDC yield can be compensation for default, recession, and leverage risk, not a reason to skip underwriting. Current yields and past performance do not guarantee future distributions.

### Are BDC distributions taxed like REIT dividends?

They are similar in structure but not identical in detail. Both generally distribute roughly 90% or more of income and are largely ordinary-income vehicles. Qualified REIT dividends receive the 20% Section 199A deduction. Some BDCs may designate amounts that qualify as well, but treatment varies by vehicle and year. Both can also pass through capital-gain or return-of-capital components. Review the vehicle’s tax reporting and consult your CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### What are the main risks of a BDC?

The main risks are corporate credit, business conditions, and leverage. Private-company borrowers can default, particularly in a recession, reducing income and NAV. BDC leverage magnifies gains and losses. Floating-rate loans can earn more as rates rise, but higher borrowing costs can also pressure the borrowers. Private-loan valuations are estimated, and a non-traded BDC adds illiquidity. High yield is partly compensation for these risks, so the allocation should fit your risk tolerance.

### Are there non-traded BDCs like non-traded REITs?

Yes. A non-traded BDC is SEC-registered but unlisted, so its shares are periodically priced at NAV rather than traded every day. It has limited redemption-based liquidity that is usually capped and may be gated, and it is sold through a broker-dealer to accredited or otherwise suitable investors following a suitability review. Like a non-traded REIT, it offers a private strategy without daily price swings in exchange for illiquidity, appraisal pricing that may lag, fees, and a longer holding period. The structural form is similar; the BDC owns private credit rather than real estate.

### Should I choose a BDC or a REIT?

Choose based on asset class and role, not yield alone. A REIT is for real-estate exposure, rent or mortgage income, and property-value participation. A BDC is for private-credit exposure and interest from private-company loans, often with floating-rate characteristics. Many investors hold both as separate sleeves. The relevant question is whether you can accept the particular real-estate, rate, credit, leverage, and liquidity risks and size the allocation accordingly.

### Are BDCs considered real estate investments?

No. BDCs lend to and invest in private operating companies across sectors such as software, healthcare services, manufacturing, and consumer businesses. They earn loan interest, not rent. A REIT, by contrast, owns income-producing property or mortgages secured by it and is a real-estate vehicle. If the aim is real-estate exposure, a REIT, a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), or direct property may be more relevant than a BDC.

### Can a BDC be used in a 1031 exchange?

No. A 1031 exchange requires like-kind real property held for investment or business use. BDC shares are securities representing interests in private-company loans, not real property, and do not qualify. REIT shares are also securities and are not directly 1031-eligible. A replacement property or a DST may be used for real-estate exchange planning. There is an indirect REIT path through a 1031 into a DST followed by a 721/UPREIT exchange into a REIT, but there is no equivalent route for BDCs. Confirm any exchange strategy with your tax adviser.

### Why do BDCs and REITs get compared so often?

They are both income-oriented pass-through vehicles that generally distribute roughly 90% or more of their income and thereby avoid entity-level corporate tax. Both may have higher yields than the broad stock market and both exist in publicly traded and non-traded forms, with NAV pricing and limited redemptions in the non-traded versions. The comparison is useful only if it leads back to the assets: a REIT holds real estate, while a BDC holds private-company loans.

### Do BDC yields rise with interest rates?

Often on the income side, because many BDC loans are floating rate and the interest paid by borrowers adjusts with short-term benchmarks. That differs from many fixed-rate bonds, whose prices tend to fall as rates rise. But rising rates also increase a private borrower’s interest cost, which can increase default risk. REIT property valuations can face rate pressure, and mREITs can face spread pressure. The net result depends on the rate setting and borrower health, so verify the details with your adviser.

### Are BDCs riskier than REITs?

Neither is universally riskier. A BDC’s principal risks are corporate credit, business weakness, borrower default, and leverage. A REIT’s principal risks are falling rents, occupancy, property values, and interest-rate pressure, including mREIT rate-spread pressure. The economic setting, the specific vehicle, leverage, and whether the investment is traded or non-traded all matter. A BDC’s higher yield should be viewed as a signal of risk as well as return. Holding both can spread exposures but does not eliminate risk.

### Can I hold both a BDC and a REIT in my portfolio?

Yes. Many income-focused investors use a REIT for real-estate exposure and a BDC for private-credit exposure. Their different economic drivers can provide diversification. That does not promise stability: distributions can be cut, values can decline, and non-traded forms are illiquid. Treat each allocation as a separate decision, avoid overconcentration, confirm suitability for non-traded investments, and keep the combined exposure appropriate for your goals.

## Glossary

- **BDC (Business Development Company):** A closed-end investment company that lends to and invests in private companies.
- **REIT:** A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
- **Private Credit:** Loans made to private companies outside public bond markets—the assets a BDC commonly holds.
- **Senior Secured Loan:** A loan near the top of a borrower’s capital structure, common in BDC portfolios.
- **Floating-Rate Loan:** A loan whose interest adjusts with short-term benchmark rates.
- **Closed-End Fund:** An investment company with a fixed share count; the BDC’s structural form.
- **Pass-Through Entity:** A vehicle that distributes income to avoid entity-level tax, including BDCs and REITs.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to distribute most income to keep pass-through status.
- **Credit Risk:** The risk that a BDC borrower defaults on its loan.
- **Leverage Risk:** The risk that a BDC’s own borrowing magnifies gains and losses.
- **Equity REIT:** A REIT that owns property and earns rent.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT that finances real estate and earns mortgage interest.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The periodic per-share value used to price a non-traded BDC or REIT.
- **Non-Traded BDC:** An SEC-registered BDC not listed on an exchange; it is illiquid and NAV-priced.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends and some designated BDC amounts.
- **Suitability Review:** The assessment of whether an illiquid, non-traded vehicle fits an investor.

## Which should you choose?

Choose a REIT when you want real-estate exposure, property or mortgage income, and the diversification potential of a property portfolio. Choose a BDC when you specifically want private-credit yield from lending to middle-market companies and understand that the risk is corporate credit rather than property risk.

Both distribute mostly ordinary income, so compare the after-tax result with your CPA. Before using a non-traded version of either vehicle, weigh its limited liquidity against a public counterpart.
