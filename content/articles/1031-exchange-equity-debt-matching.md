# 1031 Exchange Equity & Debt Matching Explained

I keep seeing 1031 exchange plans that stop at the cash proceeds. The visible number gets all the attention. But the amount of debt paid off at the sale can be just as important, and missing it is one of the fastest ways to create taxable boot by accident.

Full deferral is a two-part matching exercise. The replacement property generally must have equal or greater value than the relinquished property’s net sale price. That means the investor has to reinvest all equity and replace the paid-off debt. An owner who reinvests every dollar of cash but borrows less than the old loan balance can still recognize gain through mortgage boot.

First-order thinking is, “I rolled all my cash into the next property, so I am done.” Second-order thinking is, “What was the full value I relinquished, how much debt relief did I receive, what is the total capitalization of the replacement, and does the lower-leverage choice still meet the matching requirement?” The exchange math needs to be right before the replacement is chosen, not after the closing statement is produced.

## The equal-or-greater-value rule

For full deferral, the replacement property generally must be acquired for at least the net sale price of the relinquished property. Net sale price means the gross sale price less selling costs. If the replacement is worth less, the investor has bought down. The shortfall can be [boot](insights.html), taxable up to the amount of gain.

The important word is **total**. A property’s total value is made up of the owner’s equity and the debt financing. If the relinquished property’s value includes both, matching it requires that the replacement’s equity-plus-debt total equals or exceeds the old property’s net sale price.

This is the umbrella rule behind equity and debt matching. Reinvest all the equity and replace all the debt, and the replacement value generally matches. Reinvest all the equity but fail to replace debt, and total value falls short unless added cash covers the difference. Full deferral depends on the combination, not on the cash number alone.

## Reinvest all of your equity

Equity is the net proceeds from the sale: the property’s value minus the debt paid off and selling costs. For full deferral, all of that equity must be reinvested in the replacement property. Any proceeds the investor keeps are cash boot, taxable up to the amount of gain.

The [qualified intermediary](insights.html) holds the equity proceeds and disburses them toward the replacement purchase. The investor directs the use of funds but does not receive them. When the replacement includes new financing, the QI’s equity plus the loan proceeds fund the purchase. When there is no new loan, the equity funds the purchase by itself. Either way, all equity has to go back in.

Cash boot can occur through a deliberate distribution or an undersized replacement that does not absorb the available equity. The useful discipline is simple: keep none of the exchange proceeds if full deferral is the goal. The replacement must be large enough to take the equity as well as the financing needed to match value.

## Replace the debt you paid off

The debt side is less intuitive, but it is no less important. When a mortgage is paid off at the sale of the relinquished property, the investor is relieved of an obligation. That debt relief is treated like cash received for exchange-matching purposes. To avoid [mortgage boot](1031-exchange-debt-replacement-mortgage-boot.html), the paid-off debt must be replaced with at least as much new debt or with equivalent additional cash.

Consider a property with a **$400,000** mortgage. If the investor acquires a replacement using only a **$250,000** loan, the difference is **$150,000**. Without more cash, that **$150,000** of unreplaced debt is mortgage boot and is taxable up to the gain. The investor can avoid the shortfall either by taking on a replacement loan of at least **$400,000** or by contributing **$150,000** of additional cash.

Debt relief must be treated with the same care as sale proceeds. Reinvesting equity addresses the cash side; replacing debt with new debt or cash addresses the financing side. Focusing on one and ignoring the other is how an exchange can be structurally sound in appearance but incomplete in value.

## Use additional cash if you want less leverage

Cash can substitute for debt. That gives an investor who wants to deleverage a workable path to full deferral.

Using the same numbers, an investor who pays off **$400,000** of debt but wants only a **$250,000** replacement loan can add **$150,000** cash from outside the exchange. The added cash fills the debt gap. The replacement still reaches the required total value, and the debt relief is offset, so there is no mortgage boot.

This lets an investor reduce leverage and its associated risk without giving up full deferral. The capital stack can consist of reinvested equity, new cash, and a smaller loan. What matters is that the total replacement value reaches the required level and that the paid-off debt has been replaced.

The substitution works in one direction only. More borrowing cannot cure cash boot if an investor keeps exchange proceeds. Cash retained from the exchange is taxable regardless of the amount of new debt. The rule is: reinvest all equity, then replace the debt with new debt, added cash, or a combination.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Match both equity and debt

Assume an investor sells a property for **$1,000,000** net. A **$400,000** mortgage is paid off, leaving **$600,000** of equity.

The investor acquires a replacement for **$1,000,000**, uses a new **$400,000** mortgage, and reinvests the full **$600,000** equity. The value matches at **$1,000,000**. The equity is fully reinvested. The **$400,000** debt is replaced with **$400,000** of new debt. That is full deferral under the matching framework.

### Example 2: Under-replace debt and create mortgage boot

Use the same **$1,000,000** sale, **$400,000** debt payoff, and **$600,000** equity. This time, the investor acquires an **$850,000** replacement, reinvests the full **$600,000** equity, and borrows only **$250,000**.

The investor has reinvested all equity, but the replacement value is only **$850,000**, less than **$1,000,000**, and the debt is **$150,000** short. The **$150,000** of unreplaced debt is mortgage boot, taxable up to gain. One way to correct the structure would be to add **$150,000** cash and acquire a **$1,000,000** replacement.

### Example 3: Deleverage with added cash

Again start with the same sale. The investor wants lower leverage and buys a **$1,000,000** replacement with only a **$250,000** mortgage. The full **$600,000** equity is reinvested, and the investor adds **$150,000** of new cash.

The capitalization is **$600,000** of exchange equity plus **$150,000** of added cash plus **$250,000** of debt, for **$1,000,000** of replacement value. The debt shortfall is covered with cash. Full deferral can be achieved with lower leverage.

These figures are illustrative. A CPA should perform the precise calculation for the investor’s facts. The mechanics, however, are clear: full deferral requires matching value by reinvesting all equity and replacing all debt with new debt or cash.

## Key takeaways

- Full deferral requires replacement property of equal or greater value, which means matching both equity and debt.
- Reinvest all net proceeds. Retained proceeds are taxable cash boot.
- Replace paid-off debt with at least equal new debt, added cash, or a combination. Under-replacement creates mortgage boot.
- Additional cash can replace debt and allow deleveraging without boot.
- Debt is the side investors most often overlook, so size the replacement and financing with your CPA before the exchange.

## Avoid common matching mistakes

The most common error is reinvesting visible cash proceeds while overlooking the debt paid off at closing. Debt relief is less visible, but it is treated like cash and must be replaced. Matching equity without matching debt can leave the total replacement value short.

Buying down in value is another error. A replacement that costs less than the net sale price can create boot even when the investor has reinvested all equity. The replacement needs enough value to absorb both the equity and the replaced debt.

The third error is misunderstanding cash substitution. Cash can replace debt that the investor chooses not to re-borrow. Debt cannot replace cash that the investor chooses to keep. Plan the value, equity, and debt targets with your CPA before the exchange; the final calculation should not be based on rough estimates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it take to fully defer a 1031 exchange?

Acquire replacement property of equal or greater value, reinvest all equity, and replace all debt paid off with new debt or added cash. Reinvesting cash alone is not enough if the debt side falls short.

### What is the equal-or-greater-value rule?

It requires the replacement’s total value to be at least the relinquished property’s net sale price. Since total value includes equity and debt, both have to be matched. Buying down can create boot equal to the shortfall.

### What does replacing my equity mean?

It means reinvesting all net proceeds—the value minus debt paid off and selling costs—into the replacement. The QI holds and disburses those funds. Any cash retained is taxable cash boot up to gain.

### What does replacing my debt mean?

It means offsetting the debt paid off at sale with at least equal new debt or equivalent added cash. Debt relief is treated like cash received, and an unfilled shortfall is mortgage boot.

### What is mortgage boot?

It is taxable gain created when less debt is replaced than was paid off and no cash fills the gap. If **$400,000** is paid off and the new loan is **$250,000**, the **$150,000** difference is mortgage boot unless **$150,000** cash is added.

### Can I add cash to avoid boot?

Yes. Added cash can replace debt you choose not to re-borrow. That allows deleveraging while retaining full deferral if all other requirements are met.

### Can I take cash out and replace it with debt?

No. Extra debt does not cure retained exchange proceeds. Cash boot is taxable. The substitution rule only allows cash to replace debt.

### Why is the debt side so often overlooked?

Investors naturally focus on the cash they can see. The paid-off mortgage is less visible, but debt relief is treated like cash and must be offset. Matching both parts prevents the common error.

### Do I have to take on the exact same debt amount?

You can take on at least the old debt amount, more than the old amount, or less if cash covers the difference. The constraint is under-replacing debt without added cash.

### How do leveraged DSTs help with debt matching?

Leveraged DSTs typically have pre-arranged, non-recourse financing at a known loan-to-value ratio. A DST with debt that matches the old loan can satisfy debt replacement without a new loan application or personal guarantee, subject to suitability and diligence.

### What is the most common matching mistake?

Forgetting the debt side: reinvesting all equity but taking on less debt than was paid off. Buying down in value and mishandling cash substitution are other common mistakes. CPA planning before the exchange helps prevent them.

### How do I make sure my matching is right?

Have your CPA confirm that the replacement value equals or exceeds the relinquished value, that all equity is reinvested, and that debt is replaced with new debt or cash. Use a precise calculation, not a rough estimate.

### Does buying a more expensive replacement satisfy the matching?

It satisfies the value component, but all equity must still be reinvested and the debt must still be replaced. Trading up may create fresh depreciation on excess basis, but it does not eliminate either matching requirement.

### Can I increase debt instead of adding cash?

Yes. More replacement debt than was paid off is generally acceptable and can help meet value and debt matching. The problem is under-replacing debt without cash, not over-replacing it.

### What if I do not want any debt on the replacement?

You can buy debt-free, but must add cash equal to all paid-off debt. If **$400,000** was paid off, add **$400,000** beyond the exchange equity to offset that debt relief and reach the required value.

### Does matching apply to multiple replacement properties?

Yes. Match the aggregate replacement value, equity, and debt across all the replacement properties. A DST can be used to fill an aggregate gap, subject to the exchange rules and the offering’s terms.

## Glossary

- **Equity and Debt Matching:** The two-part requirement to reinvest all equity and replace all debt for full deferral.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** The requirement to acquire replacement value at least equal to the relinquished net sale price.
- **Equity:** Net proceeds, or value minus debt and selling costs; all must be reinvested.
- **Debt:** Property financing; paid-off debt must be replaced with new debt or cash.
- **Debt Replacement:** Offsetting paid-off debt with new debt or added cash to avoid mortgage boot.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Taxable gain from replacing less debt than was paid off unless cash offsets the difference.
- **Cash Boot:** Taxable gain from keeping some proceeds instead of reinvesting all equity.
- **Boot:** Cash or other value not reinvested, taxable up to gain, from equity or debt shortfalls.
- **Cash Substitution:** Adding cash to replace debt you do not re-borrow, allowing deleveraging without boot.
- **Deleveraging:** Reducing debt on the replacement while adding cash to avoid mortgage boot.
- **Net Sale Price:** Relinquished value, or gross price minus selling costs, that must be matched.
- **Buying Down:** Acquiring a lower-value replacement and creating value boot.
- **Trading Up:** Acquiring a higher-value replacement, which meets the value rule.
- **Leveraged DST:** A DST with pre-arranged debt that can help replace investor debt.
- **Full Deferral:** Deferring the entire gain by matching both equity and debt.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party holding equity and disbursing it to acquire the replacement.
