# International & Global REITs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing international real estate presented as a simple escape hatch from the United States. It is not that simple. But it can be useful.

REIT regimes now exist in more than 40 countries. That gives an investor access to income-producing real estate in Asia, Europe, North America, and beyond, through shares rather than a local property purchase. The appeal is exposure to property cycles and economies that do not move in lockstep with the US. The cost is a new set of variables: currencies, politics, regulation, taxes, and unfamiliar markets.

First-order thinking says that adding foreign REITs automatically means more diversification. Second-order thinking asks what countries, sectors, currencies, fund costs, and rules you are actually adding—and whether those risks are being sized sensibly. For most investors, global REIT funds and ETFs are the practical route. This is general educational material, not a recommendation; international investing carries added risks, and past performance does not guarantee future results. Verify current rules with your advisor.

Going global is an extension of a real-estate allocation, not a substitute for understanding the domestic base. Start with our [complete guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## Why Look Beyond US REITs

The US has the largest and most developed REIT market, with the deepest pool of public REITs. It is sensible for a US investor to begin there. Still, real-estate markets and economies in different countries do not move together perfectly. When US property markets are weak, another market may be stronger, and the reverse can also be true. Spreading exposure across countries can reduce reliance on the US cycle, much as an investor might use [recession-resistant REIT sectors](recession-resistant-reit-sectors.html) within a domestic allocation.

International exposure also opens property markets that are not available through US REITs: fast-growing Asian cities, European logistics hubs, and [growth-oriented property types where appreciation comes from](growth-reit-sectors-where-appreciation-comes-from.html) that may be more prominent abroad. It broadens the opportunity set beyond domestic valuations and yields.

That is the benefit. The trade-off is complexity: currency changes, political and regulatory differences, and the work of understanding markets from a distance. International REITs generally belong as one diversified portion of a real-estate sleeve, not the whole sleeve.

## Major Global REIT Markets

The REIT model has spread to more than 40 countries, but the investable market is concentrated in a smaller group. The United States is the largest and most developed market. Outside the US, Asia-Pacific is a major center: Japan’s J-REITs are one of the largest non-US markets; Australia’s A-REITs, historically called LPTs, are long-established; and Singapore’s S-REITs are a prominent regional hub with diversified and cross-border portfolios.

Europe is another important region. The United Kingdom has a well-developed REIT regime. Continental Europe includes significant REIT and listed-property markets in France, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and elsewhere. Canada rounds out the major developed markets.

Together, the US, Japan, Australia, Singapore, the UK, continental Europe, and Canada make up the bulk of the global REIT universe. Each has its own dominant sectors, regulation, and economic drivers. A global allocation is not an abstract idea; it is a set of exposures to these markets.

> The REIT idea has gone global: from Japan’s J-REITs to Singapore’s cross-border S-REITs and Europe’s listed-property companies, investors can own real estate through shares in more than 40 countries.

## Currency & Political Risk

Foreign real estate adds risks a domestic REIT does not carry in the same way. Currency risk begins with the fact that a foreign REIT’s income, value, and distributions are denominated in a local currency. A Japanese REIT can have a strong year in yen terms, while a weaker yen reduces the dollar return; a stronger yen can amplify it. Currency can add or subtract meaningfully from results and introduces volatility that is separate from the real estate itself.

Political and regulatory risk is the other layer. Countries have different legal systems, property-rights protections, tax regimes, REIT rules, and levels of political and economic stability. A change in tax treatment, a restriction on foreign ownership, a rule change, or instability can affect an outcome in ways that are harder to monitor from abroad.

Some markets are established and relatively stable; others involve more uncertainty. None of that makes international exposure inappropriate. It argues for diversification across countries and appropriate sizing rather than a concentrated bet on one foreign market.

## Global REIT Funds & ETFs

For most investors, buying individual foreign REITs is not the easiest way to go abroad. A global or international REIT fund or ETF holds a diversified basket across countries and property sectors. One US-listed security can provide exposure to Japan, Australia, Singapore, the UK, Europe, Canada, and often the US, without requiring research and trading in each foreign market.

Funds and ETFs offer broad country and sector diversification, professional management of foreign-market complexity, and ordinary brokerage-account access. A **global** fund includes the US. An **international** or **ex-US** fund excludes it and can complement a domestic REIT allocation. Before choosing, examine the expense ratio, country and sector mix, and whether—and how—the fund hedges currency. Those details affect both risk and return.

## Key Takeaways

- More than 40 countries have REIT regimes. The US is the largest market, with Japan, Australia, Singapore, the UK, Europe, and Canada as major centers.
- The main case for going global is diversification across property cycles and economies that do not move in lockstep with the US.
- International REITs add currency risk and political/regulatory risk to ordinary real-estate risk.
- Global REIT funds and ETFs are generally the easiest diversified way to access international real estate.

## Currency Hedging and Costs

Currency is a defining choice in international investing. An unhedged fund leaves the investor exposed to foreign-currency movements in both directions. A currency-hedged fund uses financial instruments to neutralize much of that exchange-rate effect, so results track the underlying real estate more closely. Hedging can reduce currency volatility, but it has a cost and gives up any currency tailwind. Neither choice is automatically superior.

Costs deserve the same attention. International and global REIT funds typically have higher expense ratios than plain domestic REIT funds because investing across markets is more complex. Hedging adds its own cost. Foreign withholding taxes on dividends can also reduce net income, although treaties and foreign-tax-credit mechanisms may offset some of the tax. That treatment is technical and should be confirmed with a tax advisor.

In other words, look beyond the fund label. The expense ratio, hedge policy, country mix, and tax treatment all influence what remains after the headline return.

> Going global means choosing whether to accept currency swings in both directions or to pay for a hedge that keeps results closer to the underlying real estate.

## Allocating Internationally

The practical question is not whether global real estate exists. It is how much of it belongs in a portfolio. For most investors, international REITs are a measured part of the real-estate sleeve: a complement to domestic REITs rather than a replacement. The appropriate amount depends on goals, time horizon, risk tolerance, and comfort with currency and political risk.

One approach is to keep a domestic core and add an ex-US fund, avoiding overlap. Another is to use a single global fund that bundles US and non-US exposure for simplicity. Either way, keep the foreign allocation broad across countries and sectors, rather than concentrated in one market, and revisit it as conditions and circumstances change.

The aim is modest but important: capture the diversification benefit of global real estate while containing currency, politics, and cost inside a sensible allocation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are international and global REITs?

International and global REITs are REITs—or funds holding them—that own real estate outside the United States or across the US and other countries. “International” commonly means ex-US; “global” usually includes the US alongside foreign markets. They can own apartments, offices, logistics properties, retail, and other real estate under their home-country REIT-style rules. For most US investors, a global or international fund or ETF is the practical access point. The benefit is broader real-estate exposure; the added considerations are currency and politics.

### Why should I invest beyond US REITs?

The main reason is diversification. Foreign property cycles and economies do not move perfectly with the US, and international exposure can add markets, property types, valuations, and income opportunities unavailable domestically. The price of that breadth is currency movement, unfamiliarity, and political or regulatory risk. It is general educational information, not a recommendation of a particular market.

### How many countries have REITs?

More than 40 countries have REIT regimes. The structure originated in the United States and has spread across Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania, and beyond as governments adopted REIT frameworks to broaden access to real-estate investment and develop property markets. The US is by far the largest and most developed; Japan, Australia, Singapore, and the UK are large and established, while other regimes vary widely in age and scale. The exact count and the rules evolve over time.

### What are the major global REIT markets?

The core markets are the US, Japan, Australia, Singapore, the UK, continental Europe, and Canada. Japan has J-REITs; Australia has A-REITs; Singapore has S-REITs. France, Germany, the Netherlands, and Belgium are important continental-European markets. Their sectors, regulations, and economic drivers differ, which is why country mix matters in a global fund.

### What is currency risk in international REITs?

Currency risk is the chance that exchange-rate movement changes a foreign investment’s return when it is translated back into US dollars. A gain in yen, euros, or Singapore dollars may be reduced or increased by the local currency’s movement against the dollar. The currency effect is separate from the property result. Some funds hedge it at a cost; others remain unhedged.

### What political and regulatory risks do global REITs face?

Countries differ in property rights, legal protections, tax regimes, REIT rules, foreign-ownership restrictions, and political and economic stability. A policy or tax change can affect a foreign investment and may be harder to anticipate from abroad. Some major developed markets are well-established and relatively stable, while others carry more uncertainty. Investors seeking to limit political and regulatory risk should favor diversified exposure across developed markets and size it carefully; those steps help contain, but cannot erase, the risk.

### How do I invest in global REITs?

For most investors, global or international REIT funds and ETFs are the simplest route. They combine many countries and sectors in one US-listed security, offer professional management, and trade through an ordinary brokerage account. Global funds include the US; international or ex-US funds do not. Review expense ratio, country and sector mix, and currency policy before investing.

### Are global REIT funds diversified?

Usually, yes. A fund can spread exposure across countries and sectors such as residential, industrial, office, retail, and healthcare. That reduces dependence on a single country, REIT, or sector. It does not eliminate real-estate, currency, political, or broad-market risk, so the actual country and sector mix still needs review.

### Should I hedge currency in international REIT investments?

Neither choice is automatically right. A hedged fund tries to reduce exchange-rate volatility and track underlying foreign real estate more closely, but hedging adds cost and removes a potential currency tailwind. An unhedged fund accepts currency movement in both directions. Over shorter periods the effect can be significant; over longer periods it may partly wash out. The appropriate choice depends on horizon, views, and tolerance for currency volatility.

### How much of my portfolio should be in international REITs?

There is no single right percentage. International REITs are generally a diversified complement to a domestic real-estate core, sized so foreign-market and currency swings cannot dominate the result. Keep the international portion broad across countries and sectors, and revisit it as conditions and personal circumstances change.

### Are international REITs riskier than US REITs?

They add currency risk, political and regulatory risk, and in some markets less liquidity or transparency, on top of the usual REIT risks of rents, occupancy, property values, and interest-rate sensitivity. At the portfolio level, holding multiple countries can improve diversification because they do not move in lockstep with the US. Assess specific funds and personal circumstances rather than labeling international exposure simply safer or riskier.

### Do international REITs follow the same rules as US REITs?

Not exactly. They follow the REIT-style rules of their own countries. Most regimes share the broad idea of owning income-producing real estate, distributing a high share of income, and receiving tax treatment in return. Distribution thresholds, asset and income tests, ownership rules, leverage limits, and taxes vary. The US 90% distribution rule has foreign analogues with different percentages or conditions, and those rules can change.

### How are international REIT dividends taxed?

Foreign REIT dividends can face foreign withholding tax as well as US tax. Treaties may lower withholding, and a foreign tax credit may offset some foreign tax in a taxable account. Income character, potential Section 199A treatment available to US REIT dividends, and account type matter; foreign tax credits generally do not help in retirement accounts. This is technical. Confirm current treatment with a tax advisor.

### Can I hold both US and international REITs?

Yes. A domestic REIT allocation can provide a US core, while an ex-US fund adds foreign property cycles, economies, and currencies without overlap. Alternatively, a single global fund combines US and non-US exposure. In either case, keep the international portion diversified and sized to your comfort with added risk.

## Glossary

- **International REIT:** A REIT or fund owning real estate outside the US, or ex-US.
- **Global REIT:** A REIT or fund spanning US and foreign real estate.
- **J-REIT:** A Japanese REIT and one of the largest non-US markets.
- **A-REIT:** An Australian REIT and a long-established global market.
- **S-REIT:** A Singapore REIT and prominent cross-border regional hub.
- **Currency Risk:** Exchange-rate movement affecting returns converted to dollars.
- **Currency Hedging:** Neutralizing exchange-rate effects at added cost.
- **Political Risk:** Risk arising from different laws, policy, and stability abroad.
- **Regulatory Risk:** Risk arising from country-by-country tax regimes and REIT rules.
- **Global REIT Fund:** A diversified basket of REITs across many countries.
- **Ex-US Fund:** An international fund that excludes the US and can pair with domestic exposure.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across countries and cycles to reduce risk.
- **Expense Ratio:** The annual cost of a REIT fund, often higher for global funds.
- **Foreign Withholding Tax:** Tax a country withholds from dividends paid to foreign investors.
- **Foreign Tax Credit:** A potential US credit that may offset some foreign tax paid.
- **Real Estate Sleeve:** The portion of a portfolio allocated to real estate.
