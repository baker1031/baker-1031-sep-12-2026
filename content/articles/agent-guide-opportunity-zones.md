# The Agent & Broker's 2026 Guide to Opportunity Zone Funds

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › The Agent & Broker's 2026 Guide to Opportunity Zone Funds

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

For Agents & Brokers · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read

I would frame the first client conversation around recognition, not a recommendation: does this person have a capital gain and a clock worth recognizing? Whether an Opportunity Zone fund is right belongs with the licensed firm and the CPA. The first-order view is a referral. The second-order view is a relationship, real-estate deal flow inside the tracts, and a hard compliance line around a security.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">Every other deferral tool you know only works for a client who is selling property. A 1031 needs real estate going out and real estate coming in. An Opportunity Zone fund breaks that rule. It accepts <em>any</em> capital gain, so the client who sold a company, dumped a concentrated stock position, or cashed out of crypto is suddenly someone you can help. That single fact makes the OZ conversation reach people who never appear on your listing pipeline. The fund itself is a securities transaction you refer out. The awareness, the introduction, and the real estate inside the zones are all yours.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways for Agents &amp; Brokers</div>
<ul>
<li>An Opportunity Zone fund lets a client defer tax on <strong>any</strong> capital gain, not just real estate, and can wipe out tax on the fund's growth after a ten-year hold.</li>
<li>OZ funds are securities sold by a licensed broker-dealer to accredited investors. You educate and refer. You do not sell, recommend a specific fund, or split a securities commission unless you are properly licensed.</li>
<li>The "any gain" angle reaches business sellers, stock holders, and crypto investors. These are people a 1031 conversation never touches.</li>
<li>The real estate inside OZ tracts is ordinary real estate. You can list it, sell it, source development sites, and represent buyers there.</li>
<li>The program is permanent starting January 1, 2027, with a new zone map. New tracts may land in your market.</li>
<li>Knowing OZ turns you into the person who solved a tax problem. That is how referral networks compound.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 id="works">What an Opportunity Zone fund actually is</h2>
<p>An <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">Opportunity Zone fund</a>, properly a Qualified Opportunity Fund or QOF, is an investment vehicle that puts capital into real estate or operating businesses inside federally designated low-income census tracts. Congress created the program in 2017 to pull private money into those areas. The incentive is the tax treatment. An investor who reinvests a capital gain into a QOF gets to defer the tax on that gain, and if they hold the fund long enough, the appreciation on the QOF investment itself can come out tax-free.</p>
<p>Here is the mechanic that matters for your conversations. The investor does not reinvest the whole sale proceeds. They reinvest the <strong>gain</strong>. Someone who sells a building for $3 million with a $1 million gain only needs to place that $1 million into a QOF to defer it. The rest is theirs to keep. They have <strong>180 days</strong> from the date of the sale to make the investment. Miss the window and the chance is gone, so timing is part of why the referral has to happen early.</p>
<p>The other half is the ten-year payoff. Hold the QOF interest for at least ten years and the basis steps up to fair market value at the time of sale. In plain terms, the growth inside the fund is excluded from capital gains tax. The deferred gain still gets recognized along the way, but the new appreciation can escape tax entirely. The <a href="opportunity-zone-10-year-rule.html">ten-year rule</a> is the reason these funds attract patient money. You do not need to master the regulations, the <a href="cpa-guide-opportunity-zones.html">CPA version of this guide</a> goes deep on that. You need to recognize the situations where an OZ fund fits.</p>
<h2 id="securities">The securities line every agent has to hold</h2>
<p>This is the part to get exactly right. A QOF is a <strong>security</strong>. It is placed under Regulation D by a licensed broker-dealer and its registered representatives, sold only to <a href="insights.html">accredited investors</a> through a private placement memorandum. A real estate license does not authorize you to sell a security, recommend a specific fund, or collect a securities commission. That line is bright, and crossing it is how agents get into trouble with both their state regulator and the SEC.</p>
<p>So what is your role? You <strong>recognize the opportunity and refer it</strong>. You spot the client with a big gain, you explain that an OZ fund might defer the tax, and you introduce them to a licensed firm and to their CPA. You do not pick the fund. You do not opine on whether it is a good investment. You do not take a cut of the securities transaction unless you hold the appropriate license or are affiliated with the broker-dealer. Frame the referral as relationship and value, not as a paid securities commission, unless you are licensed to receive one.</p>
<p>There is one nuance worth carrying around, because it works in your favor. Buying, selling, and developing the <em>underlying real estate</em> inside an OZ tract is ordinary real estate. That is squarely within your license. The QOF acquiring a development site still needs a broker to source and close that dirt. A fund building apartments in a zone still lists and sells units. Only the <strong>fund interest</strong> is a security. The bricks are yours.</p>
<h2 id="any-gain">The "any gain" angle that reaches clients no other tool does</h2>
<p>A 1031 exchange is a closed loop. Real property out, like-kind real property in, and only a real estate seller can use it. If your client sold a business, a 1031 does nothing for them. If they sold Nvidia stock at a 600% gain, a 1031 does nothing. If they made a fortune on Bitcoin, a 1031 does nothing. The OZ fund does something in every one of those cases, because it accepts a capital gain from <strong>any</strong> source.</p>
<p>Sit with what that opens up. Your client base is not just sellers anymore. It is anyone in your sphere who triggered a capital gain this year. The founder who exited her company. The early employee whose options finally vested and sold. The retiree sitting on decades of appreciated index funds. The art collector who sold a painting. The neighbor who flipped a coin into real money. None of these people would ever call a real estate agent about taxes, and that is precisely the point. You become the one who knew the strategy existed and made the introduction.</p>
<p>This is also the difference between OZ and the <a href="opportunity-zones-vs-1031.html">1031 you already use</a>. They are not competitors so much as tools for different problems. The 1031 keeps a property investor in real estate with full deferral and no ten-year requirement. The OZ takes a gain from anywhere and offers the tax-free-growth payoff in exchange for a long, illiquid hold. Knowing both means you have an answer for a far wider set of clients.</p>

<h2 id="talk">How to talk to a client about it</h2>
<p>Keep the script short and stay on your side of the line. The goal is to plant the idea and hand off, not to advise. Something like this works in a listing appointment or a casual catch-up: <em>"If you've got a big capital gain this year, from this property or from selling a business or stock, there's a strategy called an Opportunity Zone fund. It can defer the tax now, and if you hold about ten years, the growth on the new investment can come out tax-free. The thing people don't realize is it works on any kind of gain, not just real estate. I'm not licensed to sell or advise on the funds, but I can put you in front of a firm that specializes in them and loop in your CPA."</em></p>
<p>That last sentence does the compliance work. You named the strategy, you flagged the deadline pressure implicitly by saying "this year," and you drew the line on your own role out loud. If the client owns or wants real estate inside a zone, you add the part that is yours to keep: <em>"And on the real estate side in those zones, the buying, selling, and development, that's something I can handle for you directly."</em></p>
<p>One discipline to build in. The 180-day window means speed matters. If a client mentions a sale that already closed, ask when, and if it was recent, make the introduction the same week. A great OZ referral that arrives on day 181 is worth nothing. Treat the calendar like you would a financing contingency.</p>
<h2 id="clients">How clients actually use OZ funds</h2>
<p>Once the client is with the licensed firm, here is roughly what happens, so you understand the product you are pointing them toward. They take the gain amount and place it into a QOF within the 180-day window. The QOF deploys that capital into Opportunity Zone real estate or businesses, very often <strong>ground-up development or major redevelopment</strong>, because the rules reward substantial improvement of the property. The investor defers the original gain and settles in for a long hold.</p>
<p>The hold is the trade. These are illiquid, speculative investments. There is usually no secondary market and no easy exit before the ten-year mark, which is the whole point of the structure. In exchange, the investor is reaching for that basis step-up so the appreciation comes out tax-free. The profiles using this are the founder reinvesting an exit, the executive unwinding a concentrated position, the property seller who chose growth over a replacement building. They are accepting time and illiquidity to chase the after-tax result.</p>
<p>For you, there is a second-order benefit hiding in that "development and redevelopment" detail. OZ capital has to be spent on real projects in real places. That is deal flow. The fund needs land, entitlements, contractors, and eventually buyers or tenants for what it builds. If you know which tracts in your market are attracting OZ money, you know where the next development sites and listings are going to come from.</p>
<h2 id="changed">What changed for 2026 and 2027</h2>
<p>The program went through a major revision, and the dates matter for how you talk about it. Under the original 2017 program, deferred gains were scheduled to be recognized on <strong>December 31, 2026</strong>. That deadline still stands for those older investments. If you have a client who went into a QOF years ago, their deferred gain comes due at the end of this year, and that is a CPA conversation to push them toward now.</p>
<p>Starting <strong>January 1, 2027, Opportunity Zones become a permanent program</strong> rather than a temporary one. The new version uses a rolling five-year deferral on reinvested gains and a single 10% basis step-up. The old structure of a 7-year step-up worth an extra 5% is gone. The ten-year exclusion on the fund's own appreciation, the headline benefit, remains. So the basic pitch you make to clients does not change, but the deferral mechanics did.</p>
<p>The piece most relevant to your business is the map. A <strong>new zone map takes effect January 1, 2027</strong>. Governors nominate the new tracts beginning July 1, 2026, and the designations are set to refresh every ten years going forward. New maps mean new eligible tracts, and some of them may be in your market when the old ones were not. The agent who reads the new designations the week they drop is the one who knows where development capital is about to land. Track it the way you track zoning changes. For the deferral details and forms, the <a href="opportunity-zones-2-0.html">updated program guide</a> and the <a href="opportunity-zone-tax-forms.html">tax forms guide</a> cover the specifics.</p>
<h2 id="grow">How this grows your business</h2>
<p>None of this matters if it stays theoretical, so here is the practical payoff. The first win is a wider referable network. Knowing OZ lets you be useful to anyone with a gain, which means business sellers, stock holders, and crypto investors enter your orbit. People remember who solved a tax problem for them, and they send their friends.</p>
<ul class="bul">
<li><strong>Expand who you can help.</strong> The "sold something big this year?" question reaches gains you would never see through listings alone, and every introduction is a reason for that person to keep your name.</li>
<li><strong>Work the tracts directly.</strong> The underlying real estate is yours. Source development sites for OZ capital, list completed projects, and represent funds acquiring property in the zones.</li>
<li><strong>Build a referral loop with a licensed firm and CPAs.</strong> Send them the fund work, and they send you the real estate and the clients who need an agent. Reciprocity beats a one-way handoff.</li>
<li><strong>Get ahead of the 2027 map.</strong> New designations create new opportunity in places that were not eligible before. Reading the map first is a genuine edge.</li>
<li><strong>Become the educated agent.</strong> The understanding of <a href="opportunity-zone-investing-risks.html">the risks</a> and the benefits is what separates a referral source from a name on a card. Clients trust the agent who can explain why a strategy is not for everyone.</li>
</ul>
<p>The throughline is positioning. You are not trying to become a securities salesperson. You are becoming the real estate professional whose knowledge extends past the closing table, which is exactly the kind of agent high-net-worth clients want representing them.</p>

<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF)</dt><dd>The investment vehicle that holds Opportunity Zone real estate or businesses. It accepts a reinvested capital gain, defers the tax, and can deliver tax-free growth after a ten-year hold. It is a security.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Opportunity Zone Tract</dt><dd>A federally designated census tract where the underlying real estate sits. Buying, selling, and developing that property is ordinary real estate an agent can transact.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Capital Gain</dt><dd>The taxable profit from selling an appreciated asset. For OZ purposes the gain can come from any source, including a business, stock, crypto, real estate, or collectibles.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>180-Day Window</dt><dd>The period after a sale during which the investor must place the gain into a QOF to qualify for deferral. Miss it and the opportunity is lost.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Basis Step-Up</dt><dd>The adjustment to fair market value after a ten-year hold that allows the QOF's appreciation to be excluded from capital gains tax.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Accredited Investor</dt><dd>An investor who meets SEC income or net-worth thresholds and is therefore eligible to buy private offerings such as QOFs.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Private Placement Memorandum (PPM)</dt><dd>The legal offering document for a private securities placement like a QOF. It is delivered by the licensed broker-dealer, not the agent.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Referral</dt><dd>The agent's compliant role with the fund. You educate the client and introduce them to a licensed firm and their CPA, without selling or advising on the security.</dd></div>
</dl>
