# Public vs. Non-Traded REITs: A Liquidity Trade-Off

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REITs · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 11 min read**

I have been thinking about how often a smooth statement is mistaken for an easy investment. A public REIT and a non-traded REIT can own the same types of buildings, operate similar leases, and distribute the same share of income. They can still feel completely different to the owner because the route back to cash is different.

A public REIT trades on an exchange all day. An owner can sell at 10 a.m., with cash typically settling in two business days, but the market price moves with stocks even when not one tenant has changed. A non-traded REIT is priced periodically at net asset value, or NAV. Its statement may barely move, but an owner asks the sponsor to redeem the shares through a program that can be limited, queued, or shut off.

The first-order view calls the steadier line safer. The second-order view asks whether the exit will be open when cash is actually needed. That is the trade: market liquidity and visible price volatility on one side, steadier reported value and conditional redemption on the other. This article stays with that two-way liquidity-and-pricing question. For the third category, see our [private vs. public vs. non-traded REIT comparison](private-reit-vs-public-reit.html).

## Key Takeaways
- Public REITs trade on an exchange with daily liquidity and a market price shaped by the broader market, sector sentiment, and interest rates—not only by the buildings.
- Non-traded REITs use scheduled NAV pricing, but their redemption program can be capped, queued, or suspended.
- A steady statement price is not low risk. Redemptions are often limited to a small share of net assets each quarter and can be gated when many investors seek an exit.
- Neither public nor non-traded REIT shares are directly 1031 eligible. Shares are securities, not like-kind real property.
- Non-traded REITs matter most to 1031 investors as the usual destination of a 721 UPREIT roll-up, where DST property is contributed for operating-partnership units. Public REITs rarely fill that role.
- The decision is daily access and a market quote versus a steadier NAV and an income focus that can be held without watching a ticker.

## What both REITs share

The common ground is real. A REIT is a company that owns income-producing real estate and, in exchange for special tax treatment, must distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders each year. The REIT pays no corporate tax on the income it passes through. That rule applies to public and non-traded REITs alike.

Both pool investor money, buy and manage a portfolio of properties, collect rent, and pass most resulting income through as distributions. Both provide fractional ownership of a diversified real-estate portfolio without putting the investor’s name on a deed or creating a midnight tenant call.

Both also report dividends on Form 1099-DIV rather than a partnership K-1, which can simplify tax filing relative to many private real-estate structures. Under the 2025 tax law, the 20% qualified-business-income deduction on ordinary REIT dividends—the Section 199A deduction—was made permanent. That can lower the effective rate on a large share of ordinary income from either structure. The essential difference is not what they own or how they are taxed. It is how the shares are priced and how fast they can become cash. For broader context, see our [guide to REITs](reits-guide.html).

## How a public REIT works

A publicly traded REIT lists its shares on a stock exchange. Investors buy and sell through a brokerage account during market hours, in any quantity, with no minimum beyond the price of 1 share, no accreditation requirement, and no subscription paperwork. Buyers and sellers set the price continuously. An owner knows the market value to the penny and can sell at that price on a trading day, with settlement shortly after.

Liquidity is the benefit and its cost comes with it. A public REIT price moves every day, not only when the underlying property changes. It moves with the stock market’s mood, sector sentiment, and particularly interest rates. When rates rise, share prices often fall even if rents and occupancy are steady, because investors reprice the yield against safer alternatives. A public REIT can fall 20% in a quarter while the apartment or warehouse portfolio is performing as planned.

Daily access and daily volatility are inseparable. The market offers an exit every day, and it also marks the investment to that day’s mood.

Public REITs are the most transparent of the three broad types. They file audited financials and quarterly reports with the SEC, receive analyst coverage, and have a valuation visible in real time. That openness helps the investor who values a known price and the ability to act. It also means every bad market day is visible.

## How a non-traded REIT works

A non-traded REIT, sometimes called a NAV REIT, is SEC-registered but not listed on an exchange. There is no ticker to pull up and sell. The sponsor calculates a net asset value—the per-share worth of underlying real estate net of debt—on a regular schedule, often monthly. Investors buy and sell at that NAV through financial advisors and broker-dealers rather than the open market. Programs usually have a stated minimum investment, commonly in the low thousands of dollars, although terms vary.

Appraisal-based NAV moves slowly and in small steps. That steady statement is the appeal for many investors. The hard constraint is the exit. To get cash, an investor does not sell to another market participant; the investor requests that the REIT redeem the shares through its repurchase program.

Those programs are deliberately limited. A program may cap redemptions at roughly 2% of NAV per month or 5% per quarter, with exact terms in the offering documents. When requests exceed the cap, redemptions are prorated. In stress, a sponsor can slow or suspend the program. A stable price is not a promise of access. For a closer look at actual gates, read [whether private and non-traded REITs are safe](are-private-reits-safe.html).

This is where reported stability can be misleading. The value matters only if the redemption window is open when it is needed. A downturn or wave of withdrawals may be exactly when the gate is down. The price may be steady; liquidity remains conditional.

## The liquidity trade-off, in detail

The choice is clean. With a public REIT, an investor accepts a price that can swing sharply for reasons unrelated to the properties in return for the certainty of selling any share on any trading day at a known market price. With a non-traded REIT, an investor accepts slow, capped, revocable liquidity in return for a price that does not jump around daily.

There is no free version. The investor is choosing the risk of an ugly mark on the day cash is needed or the risk of a closed gate on that day.

| Feature | Public REIT | Non-Traded REIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it trades | Stock exchange, continuous | Not listed; sold via advisors |
| How it is priced | Live market price, all day | Periodic net asset value (NAV) |
| Liquidity | Daily; sell any share, any day | Limited redemption program; can be gated |
| Price volatility | High; moves with the market | Low reported volatility; steadier NAV |
| Minimum | Price of 1 share | Stated minimum, often a few thousand |
| Who can invest | Anyone with a brokerage account | Investor suitability standards apply |
| Transparency | Real-time price and full SEC reporting | SEC-registered, but no live quote |
| Typical fees | Low expense ratio and brokerage costs | Higher selling commissions and management fees |

The fee row deserves practical weight. A non-traded REIT usually has higher upfront selling commissions and ongoing management fees than a comparable public REIT. A diversified public REIT index can often be purchased for a fraction of a percent. Those costs pay for the access model and advice channel, but they are a real drag that a steady NAV can mask. Compare the all-in fee load with the value placed on a stable reported price.

## Why "stable" and "safe" are not the same word

The most common error is to read a non-traded REIT’s smooth price line as low risk. It is low *reported* volatility. That is different. The underlying real estate still faces vacancy, rising interest expense, falling market rents, and a weak sale environment. Those risks remain whether or not the statement has a daily swing.

A public REIT prices stress immediately and visibly. A non-traded REIT absorbs it into a slower NAV adjustment, so bad news arrives more quietly and with delay. The risk has not disappeared. It is being repriced on a different clock.

There is a behavioral angle as well. Daily prices can tempt an owner to trade noise or sell a sound portfolio in a panic. A non-traded REIT’s steadiness can help the right investor remain patient. But that is an advantage only when patience is a choice. A gated redemption can turn the same steadiness into a trap when cash is actually required. Treat the stable price as a long-holding-period feature, never as a safety guarantee.

## How each is taxed

Tax treatment is largely the same for both structures. Distributions from either a public or non-traded REIT are reported on [Form 1099-DIV](private-reit-dividends-tax.html), and one distribution can have more than one character. The ordinary-income portion is taxed at ordinary rates but generally qualifies for the permanent 20% Section 199A deduction. A portion may be capital-gain distribution, and a portion may be return of capital, which is not taxed in the year received but reduces cost basis and defers tax until sale.

Ordinary REIT dividends do not receive the lower qualified-dividend rate that applies to many common-stock dividends. The Section 199A deduction is the offsetting benefit. Because the mechanics are the same, tax is rarely the deciding factor between a public and a non-traded REIT. Liquidity, volatility tolerance, fees, and access usually decide. For more detail, see [how REIT dividends are taxed](private-reit-dividends-tax.html).

## REITs and the 1031 exchange

This point surprises many 1031 investors: neither type of REIT is direct replacement property. A 1031 exchange requires like-kind real property. REIT shares are securities rather than real estate, so using exchange proceeds to buy either public or non-traded REIT shares would be a taxable sale, not an exchange.

An investor selling a rental cannot defer the gain simply by buying a basket of public REIT shares. For a passive replacement structure intended to qualify, the direct route is a Delaware Statutory Trust, or DST. It is structured as 1031 replacement property. See the [DST vs. REIT comparison](dst-vs-reit.html) for that boundary.

REITs can enter a 1031 plan through the side door of a [721 exchange](1031-to-721-exchange.html), also called an UPREIT. After holding a DST for a period, a sponsor may acquire the DST property into a REIT operating partnership in exchange for OP units. That contribution is tax-deferred under Section 721. The roll-up almost always leads to a non-traded REIT because that program is designed to absorb contributed property and issue units. It is rarely a publicly traded REIT.

For a 1031 or DST investor, then, a non-traded REIT can be the relevant endpoint: a place where a DST chain may eventually consolidate for broader diversification and a path to eventual liquidity. Daily liquidity is not the public REIT’s role in that sequence.

## Who each one fits

A public REIT may fit an investor who values daily access, transparent pricing, low fees, and the ability to increase or reduce a position at will. It belongs naturally in a broader stock-and-bond portfolio for an investor who treats real estate as one liquid sleeve and can tolerate a market-driven share price even when the buildings are fine.

A non-traded REIT may fit an investor seeking real-estate income who will not need the principal for years and prefers a steady reported value to a daily quote. It can also fit the 1031 and DST investor for whom a non-traded REIT is the natural home of a 721 roll-up. The structure rewards a long holding period and punishes a sudden need for cash.

If there is a realistic chance the money will be needed on short notice, daily public-REIT liquidity—or simply holding that portion in cash—is the safer call. Neither form substitutes for an emergency fund.

The honest framing is not “riskier” versus “safer.” It is which inconvenience an investor can carry: a visible price drop that can be acted on or a steady price that may not be actionable. Read the redemption terms and fee load, then let the liquidity trade-off, not the calm-looking statement, determine the decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between a public and a non-traded REIT?

Liquidity and pricing. A public REIT trades on an exchange with daily liquidity and a live market price. A non-traded REIT is priced periodically at NAV and sold through advisors. Its reported value may be steadier, but exit depends on a limited redemption program.

### Are non-traded REITs safer because their price is more stable?

No. A steady reported price means lower reported volatility, not lower risk. The same vacancy, interest-rate, and valuation risks affect the underlying real estate. A non-traded REIT records the changes in slower NAV adjustments rather than daily price moves. The risk is repriced on a different clock, not removed.

### Can I sell a non-traded REIT whenever I want?

Not freely. The sponsor’s share-repurchase program normally caps redemptions at a small share of net assets each month or quarter. If requests exceed the cap, they are prorated. In stress, the program can be slowed or suspended.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange into a REIT?

Not directly. A 1031 exchange needs like-kind real property, and REIT shares are securities. Buying shares with exchange proceeds is a taxable sale. A deferred route into a REIT runs through a 1031-eligible DST and then a Section 721 roll-up of DST property into a REIT operating partnership.

### Why do non-traded REITs matter to 1031 and DST investors?

They are the usual destination of a 721 UPREIT exchange. After holding a DST, an investor may contribute the property into a REIT operating partnership for OP units in a tax-deferred Section 721 transaction. That usually leads to a non-traded REIT, not a public REIT.

### Are the taxes different between the two?

Largely no. Both use Form 1099-DIV, and a distribution may include ordinary income, capital gain, and return of capital. The ordinary portion generally qualifies for the permanent 20% Section 199A deduction but not the lower qualified-dividend rate. Tax rarely decides the structure choice.

### Which has higher fees?

Non-traded REITs typically cost more through upfront selling commissions and higher ongoing management fees tied to the advice channel and access model. A diversified public REIT can often be purchased at a small expense ratio. Compare the all-in fee load with the value placed on a steady price.

### Which one should I choose?

It depends on what the money needs to do. A public REIT fits daily access, transparent price discovery, and low fees if the investor can tolerate market swings. A non-traded REIT fits a years-long income allocation and a preference for periodic NAV, provided limited redemptions are acceptable. Neither is risk-free.

## Glossary

**REIT:** A real estate investment trust: a company that owns income-producing real estate and must distribute at least 90% of taxable income to shareholders, avoiding corporate-level tax.

**Public (Traded) REIT:** A REIT whose shares list on a stock exchange, offering daily liquidity at a live market price set continuously by buyers and sellers.

**Non-Traded REIT:** An SEC-registered REIT that is not exchange-listed; shares are bought and sold at periodic net asset value through advisors, with exits through a limited redemption program.

**Net Asset Value (NAV):** The per-share value of a REIT’s real estate net of debt, calculated on a schedule and used as the buy and redemption price for a non-traded REIT.

**Redemption Program:** The mechanism by which a non-traded REIT buys back investor shares, typically capped per period and subject to proration or suspension under stress.

**Liquidity:** How quickly and reliably an investment can be turned into cash. Public REITs offer daily liquidity; non-traded REITs offer conditional, gated liquidity.

**721 Exchange (UPREIT):** A tax-deferred contribution of property, often a DST interest, into a REIT’s operating partnership in exchange for OP units; the usual route from a DST into a non-traded REIT.

**Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends, made permanent under the 2025 tax law, that lowers the effective rate on the ordinary-income portion of REIT distributions.
