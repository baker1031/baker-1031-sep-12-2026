# 1033 Exchange: Deferring Tax After Condemnation or Casualty

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 14 min read**

I keep seeing the same understandable reaction after a fire, flood, condemnation notice, or eminent-domain taking: the owner reaches immediately for the familiar 1031 playbook. That can be the wrong tool. Where the property was lost rather than sold by choice, Section 1033—not Section 1031—is often the relevant deferral provision.

The first-order thought is that compensation has arrived and tax must follow. The second-order question is whether the owner simply replaced a property lost through no choice of their own. Section 1033 can defer gain on that compensation when it is reinvested in qualifying replacement property. It is a time-sensitive tax matter, though, and its rules are different enough from 1031 that a CPA should be involved from the beginning.

Lost a property to eminent domain, flood, or fire? This guide explains involuntary conversions, the differences from 1031, the longer replacement periods, and how to qualify.

## What Is a 1033 Exchange?

Section 1033 defers gain from an involuntary conversion: property that is taken, destroyed, stolen, or condemned. If the owner reinvests the compensation—often an insurance payment or condemnation award—in similar replacement property, the gain that otherwise would be recognized can be deferred.

“1033 exchange” is shorthand. Technically, it is an involuntary-conversion gain deferral, not an actual swap. There is no requirement to use a [qualified intermediary](insights.html): the owner may receive the compensation and reinvest it within the applicable period.

That distinction follows a simple fairness logic. Replacing property that was lost without the owner’s choice is not the same as voluntarily cashing out of an investment. Reinvestment can therefore defer the tax.

## Involuntary Conversions Defined

An involuntary conversion includes property destroyed, stolen, seized, or condemned, as well as property sold or exchanged under the threat or imminence of condemnation, when the owner receives money or other property as compensation. The relevant gain is the gain realized on that compensation.

Common triggers include eminent-domain takings for a road, utility, or other public project; disasters such as fire, flood, or hurricane; and a sale made under an imminent condemnation threat.

Control is the dividing line. A sale the owner chose is a 1031 question. A taking or destruction is a 1033 question.

## 1033 vs. 1031: The Key Differences

Both provisions defer gain through reinvestment, but the starting facts and operating rules are different. A 1033 requires an involuntary event; a 1031 concerns a voluntary transaction. Under 1033 the owner can receive the proceeds; under 1031 a qualified intermediary must hold them. The usual 1033 replacement test is “similar or related in service or use,” often narrower than 1031’s broad like-kind standard.

Timing is the practical difference most people notice. A 1033 period generally runs two years, or three years for condemned real property held for business or investment. A 1031 generally allows 180 days. The longer 1033 window recognizes that a casualty or taking is not planned.

There must also be gain to defer. Compensation below basis creates a loss, not a 1033 deferral, although casualty-loss rules may be relevant. Establishing which framework applies comes first.

## The Longer Replacement Timelines

The general period ends at the close of the second tax year after the year the gain is realized—often roughly two years. For condemned business or investment real property, it ends at the close of the third tax year.

Federally declared disasters can bring further extensions, and in some circumstances additional time may be requested. Those provisions reflect the reality that involuntary losses disrupt both the owner and the replacement-property search.

The clock generally starts when gain is realized, typically when compensation is received, rather than when the property was lost. The exact deadline depends on the conversion and the property, so confirm it with a CPA.

## Replacement Property Under 1033

The basic standard—“similar or related in service or use”—is usually narrower than 1031’s like-kind test. An owner-user generally needs a replacement that performs a similar function. A special rule is more flexible for condemned investment or rental real estate.

Specifically, condemned real property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment can be replaced with like-kind real property. That can open the broader 1031 like-kind universe, potentially including DSTs and other investment real estate.

The appropriate standard and its breadth depend on the property and conversion. That is not a detail to infer from a headline; it determines what can be acquired while preserving the deferral.

## Key Takeaways

- Section 1033 defers gain from involuntary conversions, including condemnation and casualty, not voluntary sales.
- A qualified intermediary is not required; the owner may receive and reinvest the proceeds.
- Replacement periods are generally 2–3 years, substantially longer than a 1031 period.

## How to Qualify for 1033 Deferral

Qualification requires an involuntary conversion that produces gain, reinvestment in qualifying replacement property within the replacement period, and a proper election on the tax return. The election tells the IRS that gain is being deferred under Section 1033.

For full deferral, the owner generally reinvests at least the amount of the conversion proceeds. Reinvesting less generally produces recognized gain to the extent of the shortfall, much like [boot](insights.html) in a 1031. The replacement property’s basis is reduced by the deferred gain.

Because insurance and condemnation proceedings can run alongside tax reporting, coordination with a CPA should begin as soon as the conversion occurs.

## Condemnation and Eminent Domain

Condemnation is a government taking of private property for public use through eminent domain, with compensation. It is one of the most common 1033 triggers. Gain on the award can be deferred, as can gain from a sale made under the imminent threat of condemnation.

The threat-or-imminence rule matters. The owner need not wait for the actual taking; a sale—even to a third party—can qualify where condemnation is imminent.

Condemned business or investment real property receives the most generous combination: the three-year replacement period and the broader like-kind standard. Early planning helps determine how to reinvest.

## Casualty and Disaster Losses

Fire, flood, hurricane, and other sudden destruction can also trigger Section 1033. When insurance proceeds exceed basis, the owner has gain that can be deferred by reinvesting in replacement property.

That can happen after a natural disaster where an insurance payout exceeds a heavily depreciated basis. The property loss is painful; the payout can still create taxable gain. Section 1033 provides a way to replace the property and defer that gain.

Declared-disaster areas often receive extended replacement periods and other relief. Check the applicable IRS relief and work with a CPA on timing and reinvestment.

## Basis in the Replacement Property

The mechanism is basis. Deferred gain reduces the basis in the replacement property, which generally equals its cost less the deferred gain. The gain is preserved for later recognition.

So 1033 defers rather than eliminates tax. If the replacement is later sold without another deferral, the gain generally comes due.

As with a 1031, holding the replacement property until death can create a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) that eliminates the deferred gain for heirs. The basis mechanics resemble 1031 even though the conversion rules do not.

## Making the 1033 Election

The owner generally elects deferral by reporting the involuntary conversion on the tax return and stating that the gain is being deferred. If the owner intends to reinvest within the period, gain generally is not recognized in the conversion year.

If reinvestment occurs as planned, the deferral stands. If the owner does not reinvest enough within the period, the return for the gain year generally must be amended to recognize the gain. A CPA handles the election and any amended return.

The conversion, compensation receipt, and reinvestment can extend across several tax years. That is one reason the reporting deserves professional guidance.

## Common 1033 Mistakes

The common mistake is treating an involuntary conversion as a 1031: engaging a qualified intermediary unnecessarily or assuming a 180-day deadline. The 1033 framework is different and usually more generous on timing.

Other mistakes are reinvesting too little, failing to make the election, and missing the longer replacement period. Each can produce current gain or forfeit the deferral.

Casualties and condemnations are stressful and may involve insurers or legal proceedings. Bringing the CPA in early is the practical protection.

## Can You Combine 1033 With a Later 1031?

Yes. A Section 1033 replacement property takes a reduced basis that carries the deferred gain. If that replacement is investment real estate, it can later be exchanged under Section 1031, continuing the deferral of the originally involuntary gain.

Over time, the provisions can work in sequence: Section 1033 handles the initial loss; later [1031 exchanges](1031-exchange-guide.html) can support a trade-up, diversification, or a shift toward passive ownership. The deferred gain follows the basis through both transactions.

If the final replacement is held until death, a step-up in basis can eliminate the accumulated deferred gain. That is a powerful result from an unplanned event, but the basis record has to be maintained.

Chaining 1033 and 1031 deferrals across years is a CPA’s job. The rules differ even though the basis consequence travels through both.

## Documentation and Working With Your CPA

Keep condemnation awards and correspondence, insurance claims and payouts, evidence of the property’s basis, and records of the replacement property and its cost. Those records support the election and any later examination.

The CPA’s role is broader than filing a form: applying the right replacement period and standard, determining gain, calculating the amount that must be reinvested, maintaining the election, and coordinating casualty-loss rules or disaster relief.

The practical stance today is simple: at the first condemnation notice, casualty, or eminent-domain threat, preserve the documents and call the CPA. The rules are forgiving on timing, but the benefit depends on a correct election and records. How would you organize the replacement decision when the loss was not your choice?

## Is a 1033 Deferral Right for You?

If an involuntary condemnation, eminent-domain taking, or casualty produced gain, Section 1033 is generally the appropriate tool. It is more flexible than 1031 on timing and intermediary requirements, and it can defer tax on compensation the owner did not choose to receive.

The questions are whether compensation exceeds basis, whether the owner will acquire qualifying replacement property, and whether the replacement can occur during the applicable period. If so, the election preserves capital while deferring tax.

Work with a CPA from the outset. The current priority is not to force a familiar 1031 template onto an unfamiliar event; it is to document the conversion, select the correct replacement rule, and make the election correctly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 1033 exchange?

It is a Section 1033 deferral of gain from an involuntary conversion—property destroyed, condemned, stolen, or taken—when compensation is reinvested in qualifying replacement property. Unlike 1031, no qualified intermediary is required, proceeds may be received directly, and the replacement periods are longer.

### How is a 1033 different from a 1031 exchange?

A 1033 requires an involuntary event, permits direct receipt of proceeds, uses the similar-or-related-in-service-or-use standard, and generally gives two to three years. A 1031 is voluntary, requires a qualified intermediary, uses the broad like-kind standard, and has 45/180-day deadlines.

### What is an involuntary conversion?

It is property lost through destruction, theft, seizure, condemnation, or sale under a condemnation threat, with money or other property received as compensation. Reinvestment in qualifying replacement property can defer gain on that compensation.

### How long do I have to replace property in a 1033?

Generally through the end of the second tax year after the gain year—often roughly two years. Condemned business or investment real property generally receives through the end of the third tax year, and a federally declared disaster can extend the period further.

### Do I need a qualified intermediary for a 1033?

No. The owner may receive the condemnation award or insurance proceeds and reinvest them during the replacement period; a qualified intermediary need not hold the funds.

### What does “similar or related in service or use” mean?

It is the 1033 replacement test and is generally narrower than 1031 like-kind. The replacement must serve a similar function. Condemned business or investment real property can instead receive the broader like-kind standard.

### Does a casualty insurance payout trigger a 1033?

It can. If destruction produces insurance proceeds above basis, the resulting gain can be deferred through qualifying replacement property. That is common when a disaster payout exceeds a depreciated basis.

### Can I defer gain from eminent domain?

Yes. Eminent-domain compensation is a classic 1033 trigger. Condemned business or investment real property gets the three-year period and broader like-kind standard.

### How much do I have to reinvest under 1033?

Generally, at least the conversion proceeds for full deferral. A shortfall generally creates recognized gain, like boot, and the replacement property’s basis is reduced by the gain deferred.

### Does a 1033 eliminate the tax?

No. It defers tax through a reduced replacement basis. The gain is generally recognized on a later sale without another deferral, although holding property until death can result in a step-up in basis for heirs.

### What if condemnation is only threatened, not final?

The threat-or-imminence rule may apply. A sale under an imminent condemnation threat, including a sale to a third party, can qualify before the actual taking.

### Do I make an election for a 1033 deferral?

Yes. The owner generally reports the conversion and intended deferral on the tax return. If reinvestment is insufficient, the gain-year return generally is amended. A CPA handles the mechanics.

### Can I use a 1033 for a rental property that burned down?

Yes, if the insurance proceeds exceeded basis and created gain; reinvestment can defer it. The special broader standard and longer period apply to condemned—not casualty—business or investment real property.

### What’s the most common 1033 mistake?

Applying the 1031 process, including unnecessary QI custody or a 180-day assumption, to an involuntary conversion. Engage the CPA as soon as it occurs.

### Should I work with a professional on a 1033?

Yes. The rules are time-sensitive, differ from 1031, and often overlap insurance or condemnation proceedings across multiple tax years. CPA involvement from the conversion helps get deferral, election, and reporting right.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange after a 1033 deferral?

Yes. Investment real estate received in a 1033 carries a reduced basis and can later be exchanged under Section 1031, continuing the deferred involuntary gain with CPA basis tracking.

### Does the deferred 1033 gain ever disappear?

It can. A later sale without deferral generally triggers gain, but holding the replacement—or a later 1031 chain—until death can allow a step-up in basis that eliminates accumulated deferred gain for heirs.

### What records should I keep for a 1033 deferral?

Keep condemnation awards and correspondence, insurance claims and payouts, basis evidence, and replacement-property and cost records. They support the election, an examination, and a correct deferred-gain and basis calculation.

### Is a 1033 deferral mandatory or optional?

It is optional. An owner may recognize gain—for example, to use a loss or because they will not reinvest—but most owners with gain from an involuntary loss who plan to reinvest use the election to avoid tax on compensation they did not choose to receive.

### What if my insurance payout is less than my basis?

That is a loss rather than a 1033 gain. Casualty-loss rules may instead allow a deduction in some circumstances. A CPA determines whether there is gain to defer or a loss to claim.

### Can I use a 1033 for business equipment that was destroyed?

Potentially. Section 1033 can apply to involuntary conversions of property beyond real estate when insurance proceeds create gain and similar property is acquired. That differs from Section 1031, which since 2017 covers only real property. Confirm with a CPA.

### How does federally declared disaster relief affect a 1033?

It can extend the replacement period and provide other IRS relief to affected taxpayers. Check the applicable notice; a CPA can track the relief that applies.

## Glossary

- **1033 Exchange:** Deferral of gain from an involuntary conversion under Section 1033.
- **Involuntary Conversion:** Property lost by destruction, theft, seizure, condemnation, or a condemnation threat, with compensation received.
- **Condemnation:** A government taking through eminent domain with compensation.
- **Eminent Domain:** Government authority to take private property for public use with just compensation.
- **Similar or Related in Service or Use:** The general, often narrower, 1033 replacement-property standard.
- **Replacement Period:** The generally 2–3-year window to reinvest proceeds and defer gain.
- **Casualty:** Sudden property destruction, such as fire, flood, or storm, that can trigger 1033 when gain results.
- **Threat or Imminence:** The rule under which a sale made under imminent condemnation can qualify.
- **1033 Election:** The tax-return election to defer gain from an involuntary conversion.
- **Carryover/Reduced Basis:** Replacement-property basis reduced by deferred gain, preserving it for later recognition.
- **Federally Declared Disaster:** A disaster designation that may extend the 1033 period and provide other relief.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** A basis reset at death that can eliminate deferred 1033 gain for heirs.
