# Industrial & Logistics REITs Explained

I have been thinking about the buildings that do the least to attract attention and the most to keep commerce moving: the warehouse, distribution center, fulfillment facility, and last-mile site. They are plain assets. Their economics are not always plain.

## What Industrial REITs Own

Industrial REITs own the places where goods are stored, handled, and distributed. Their core holdings are warehouses and distribution centers, where inventory is held and routed; fulfillment centers, where individual online orders are picked, packed, and shipped; logistics facilities near transportation hubs; light-industrial and flex space; and increasingly last-mile facilities close to population centers for fast final delivery.

These buildings are designed to move goods rather than display them. Large floor plates, high clear height for stacking inventory, loading docks, truck courts, and access to highways, ports, rail, and airports all matter. Location is central: a warehouse’s value depends greatly on how efficiently it connects inventory to customers and transportation networks.

Industrial REITs assemble portfolios of these properties and lease them to retailers, e-commerce companies, manufacturers, distributors, and third-party logistics providers. They own the physical nodes of the supply chain.

## E-Commerce & Supply-Chain Demand

E-commerce is the sector’s central demand driver. Online shopping requires multiples more warehouse and distribution space per dollar of sales than traditional brick-and-mortar retail, by various estimates. The reason is structural. Products in a store sit on shelves until a customer carries them away; an online order must be stored, picked from inventory, packed, and shipped to an individual address.

Supply-chain reconfiguration reinforces that demand. Disruptions exposed the fragility of lean, just-in-time systems, leading many companies to hold more inventory through just-in-case stocking, diversify suppliers, or reshore production. Each move can require additional space. Last-mile logistics adds another demand layer as companies position smaller facilities near population centers to provide rapid final delivery.

Those forces explain the attention industrial real estate has received. They do not eliminate the cycle. Demand still needs to meet the supply being built in each market.

## Lease Structures & Tenants

Industrial leases tend to last longer than residential leases, often several years to a decade or more. That can give an industrial REIT relatively predictable, durable income compared with apartments, where rent resets more frequently. Many industrial leases include contractual annual rent escalators, often a fixed percentage, and many are [net leases where the tenant covers property expenses](net-lease-reits-explained.html), including taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Long terms, escalators, and net leases can make income comparatively steady and contractually growing.

The tenants are the companies that need to store and move goods: retailers, e-commerce companies, third-party logistics providers (3PLs), manufacturers, and distributors. A creditworthy long-term tenant supports stable rent. A REIT relying too heavily on one or a few large tenants has concentration risk if a major occupier downsizes, relocates, renegotiates, or struggles.

As leases expire, a REIT in a strong market may re-lease space at higher market rents than the expiring lease. That can add income growth, but it depends on conditions. For a comparison of [relatively predictable, durable income](income-reits-explained-investing-for-cash-flow.html), the operating detail is always as important as the lease label.

## Sector Risks

Industrial REITs have real tailwinds and real risks. The prominent sector-specific risk is oversupply. Strong demand and high rents can bring extensive warehouse construction. If developers deliver more space than tenants need, especially as demand cools, vacancies can rise and rent growth can slow. A sector that has been a standout can soften quickly when supply outpaces absorption.

An e-commerce slowdown would weaken a central demand engine. Tenant concentration can expose a REIT if one large occupier cuts its footprint, builds its own facility, or renegotiates. Location obsolescence is another risk: a property with weak access to transportation networks or population centers can become less competitive as logistics needs change. General REIT risks still apply, including interest-rate sensitivity and leverage.

The useful question is not whether industrial has had a good run. It is whether a specific portfolio has enough location quality, tenant depth, lease protection, and balance-sheet capacity to handle a less friendly period.

## Outlook Considerations

The support case is clear but not a promise. E-commerce growth, supply-chain reconfiguration toward more inventory, and the need for last-mile delivery near population centers can support demand for well-located industrial space. Long leases with escalators and the chance to re-lease expiring space at higher market rents can support income growth in favorable markets.

The caution is equally clear. Capital follows strong results. Construction can catch up with, or move beyond, demand in a market and pressure occupancy and rents. The sector is tied to online retail, and higher interest rates can pressure valuations even when operations are sound. Historical outperformance describes the past; it is not a forecast. Treat an outlook as a set of considerations, not a prediction.

## How Investors Evaluate Industrial REITs

Investors need operating, lease, and financial evidence. Occupancy and same-store net operating income (NOI) growth show how the existing portfolio is performing. Leasing spreads—the difference between rent on new leases and rent on expiring leases—show pricing power as leases roll. A strongly positive leasing spread indicates that the REIT is re-leasing space at higher market rents. The new-supply pipeline in core markets can flag future pressure on rents and occupancy; the [REIT glossary](reit-glossary-of-terms.html) explains leasing spreads in more detail.

On the lease side, review weighted-average lease term, rent-escalator provisions, tenant credit, and tenant concentration. On the financial side, review funds from operations (FFO) and adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) for cash earnings, the payout ratio for distribution sustainability, leverage for rate sensitivity, and net asset value (NAV) as a value reference. Track broader e-commerce and supply-chain trends as well. No one metric carries the entire answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do industrial REITs own?

Industrial REITs own property where goods are stored, handled, and distributed. That includes warehouses and distribution centers, fulfillment centers that pick, pack, and ship online orders, logistics facilities near transportation hubs, light-industrial and flex space, and last-mile facilities close to population centers. These buildings typically have large floor plates, high clear height, loading docks, truck courts, and access to highways, ports, rail, or airports. Industrial REITs lease these properties to companies that need to move inventory through the supply chain. Location and transportation access are central to their value.

### Why is e-commerce good for industrial REITs?

E-commerce needs multiples more warehouse and distribution space per dollar of sales than brick-and-mortar retail, by various estimates. Online orders require products to be stored, picked, packed, and shipped to individual customers, while a store lets customers take displayed goods from the shelf. As online sales have become a larger share of retail, warehouse, distribution, and fulfillment demand has grown. Fast delivery also increases need for last-mile space near customers. The relationship cuts both ways: a slowdown in e-commerce growth or a pullback by major online occupiers can reduce industrial demand.

### How long are industrial REIT leases?

Industrial leases often run several years to a decade or more, longer than many residential leases. They commonly include annual rent escalators, often a fixed percentage, and net-lease terms that put taxes, insurance, and maintenance on the tenant. That structure can provide relatively durable, contractually growing income. The trade-off is that a long lease can lag the market if rents rise rapidly; the REIT may not capture the full new rent until the lease expires. Re-leasing at higher market rent, a positive leasing spread, is the usual way it captures that increase.

### Who are the tenants of industrial REITs?

Tenants include large retailers and e-commerce firms, third-party logistics providers that warehouse and ship for other companies, manufacturers, and distributors. Creditworthiness and tenant concentration matter. A strong, long-term tenant can support stable rent. A portfolio heavily dependent on one or a few large occupiers faces more risk if they downsize, relocate, renegotiate, or develop their own facilities. Investors should assess both tenant credit quality and how diversified the tenant base is.

### Have industrial REITs outperformed other sectors?

Industrial REITs have historically outperformed many other REIT sectors over parts of the past decade. E-commerce, supply-chain demand, rising rents, and re-leasing at higher market rent supported results and drew attention to the sector. That record is not a forecast. It also attracted extensive new construction, creating oversupply risk in some markets. A slowdown in online retail or higher rates can pressure the sector even when previous operations were strong. Treat historical outperformance as context and evaluate the current risks beside the tailwinds.

### What is last-mile logistics?

Last-mile logistics is the final leg of moving a product from a local distribution point to the customer’s door. Demand for fast, sometimes same-day or next-day delivery has led companies to seek smaller distribution and fulfillment facilities near dense population centers. Those properties can be scarce and valuable because they reduce delivery time and cost. Last-mile facilities complement larger regional distribution centers by placing inventory closer to the end customer. The concept also explains why location matters so much in industrial real estate.

### What are the main risks of industrial REITs?

The leading sector-specific risk is oversupply: developers may build more warehouses and distribution centers than tenants need, increasing vacancy and pressuring rent growth. Other risks include an e-commerce slowdown, tenant concentration in a few large occupiers, and location obsolescence for poorly sited property. General REIT risks, including interest-rate sensitivity and leverage, also apply. Diversification can help manage these exposures but does not remove them.

### What is a rent escalator in an industrial lease?

A rent escalator is a contractual provision that raises rent by a set amount or percentage at defined intervals, usually each year, over a lease term. It gives the REIT a scheduled source of income growth without a new lease negotiation. That is one reason long industrial and net leases can produce relatively steady, growing income. A fixed escalator can lag market rents in a strong market, however. The REIT captures the full market move only when it re-leases space at a new rate, reflected in the leasing spread.

### How do industrial REIT dividends get taxed?

Industrial REIT dividends follow the general REIT tax framework. Most ordinary dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than lower qualified-dividend rates because the REIT itself paid no corporate tax. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the 20% Section 199A deduction, which was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. A distribution can also include return of capital, which reduces cost basis instead of being taxed currently, or capital-gain distributions taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the mix on Form 1099-DIV. Some investors hold REITs in tax-advantaged accounts. Exact treatment is individual; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice, so verify it with your tax advisor.

### How does oversupply affect industrial REITs?

When demand and rents are strong, developers build. Construction takes time, so a large wave of new space can arrive as demand cools, leaving more supply than tenants need. Vacancy can rise, rent growth can slow or fall, and landlords can lose pricing power as they compete for occupants. This can soften a formerly strong sector quickly in affected markets. Investors watch the new-supply pipeline in a REIT’s core markets for this reason. Oversupply varies by submarket, so geographic analysis matters; one location can be tight while another is overbuilt.

### What's the outlook for industrial REITs?

The outlook has both support and risk. E-commerce, supply-chain reconfiguration toward more inventory, and last-mile delivery can sustain demand for well-located property. Long leases, escalators, and re-leasing potential can support income growth in favorable markets. Construction can also catch up with or outrun demand, reducing occupancy and rent growth. The sector’s dependence on online retail and its interest-rate sensitivity add uncertainty. Historical outperformance is not a prediction. Treat the outlook as a set of factors to assess, not a promise about future results.

### What metrics should I look at for an industrial REIT?

Use operating, lease, and financial measures together. Occupancy, same-store NOI growth, leasing spreads, and the new-supply pipeline show property-market conditions and pricing power. Weighted-average lease term, rent escalators, tenant credit, and tenant concentration show income durability. FFO and AFFO show cash earnings; payout ratio helps assess distribution sustainability; leverage indicates rate exposure; and NAV is a value reference. Also follow e-commerce and supply-chain trends because demand is connected to them. No single metric tells the whole story, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How can I invest in industrial REITs?

Publicly traded industrial and logistics REITs can be bought and sold like stocks through an ordinary brokerage account. An investor can buy individual shares or use REIT mutual funds and ETFs holding baskets of industrial and other REITs for diversification. Non-traded and private industrial REITs are not bought on an exchange; they are offered through a broker-dealer, typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors, and require a suitability review. Public REITs and funds generally provide more liquid, lower-minimum access; non-traded options are a longer-term, advisor-assisted commitment. Before investing, review the property focus, markets, tenants and concentration, lease terms, occupancy, leasing spreads, fee structure, and liquidity terms.

### Are industrial REITs interest-rate sensitive?

Yes. Rising rates can make other income options more competitive with REIT dividends, pressure REIT share prices, increase debt costs, and complicate refinancing. A rate increase can hurt valuation and cash flow even when a warehouse is full and rent is growing. Industrial REITs also have operating supports—e-commerce and supply-chain demand, long leases, and rent escalators—that can work through rate cycles. The outcome depends on leverage, fixed versus floating debt, and local demand. Rate sensitivity is one factor among several; do not assume one rate relationship will hold in every cycle.

## Glossary

- **Industrial REIT:** A REIT owning warehouses, distribution, and logistics property.
- **Logistics REIT:** An industrial REIT focused on supply-chain and distribution space.
- **Warehouse:** A facility for storing inventory and routing shipments.
- **Distribution Center:** A hub for storing and distributing goods to stores or customers.
- **Fulfillment Center:** A facility that picks, packs, and ships online orders.
- **Last-Mile Facility:** A site near customers for rapid final delivery.
- **Clear Height:** Interior ceiling height for stacking warehouse inventory.
- **Net Lease:** A lease where the tenant covers most property expenses.
- **Rent Escalator:** A contractual periodic rent increase built into a lease.
- **Leasing Spread:** The gap between new-lease and expiring-lease rents.
- **Third-Party Logistics (3PL):** A company handling warehousing and shipping for others.
- **Tenant Concentration:** Reliance on one or a few large tenants for income.
- **Same-Store NOI:** Net operating income growth at consistently owned properties.
- **FFO / AFFO:** REIT cash-earnings measures used to assess income.
- **E-Commerce:** Online retail, the central demand driver for the sector.
- **Oversupply:** New construction exceeding demand, pressuring rents.
