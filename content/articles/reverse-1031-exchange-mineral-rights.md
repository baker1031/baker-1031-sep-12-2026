# Reverse 1031 Exchange for Mineral Rights: When You Buy First

*Mineral & Royalty · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about the moment a scarce mineral package shows up before the seller of the old interest is ready. The first-order reaction is to buy the prize and solve the exchange later. The second-order reality is that buying first changes both title and financing mechanics, then puts the sale of the relinquished property under a hard 180-day clock.

When replacement minerals or property appear before you have sold, a reverse 1031 exchange lets you buy first. This guide explains the structure, the exchange accommodation titleholder, the Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor, tighter timing and financing, and whether the cost is justified.

The normal 1031 is a forward exchange: sell first, then buy. A reverse exchange addresses the less common case where a rare mineral package or ideal property arrives first and will not wait. It permits the replacement acquisition before the relinquished sale while preserving deferral, but it costs more and is more involved because the taxpayer cannot own both properties simultaneously and call it an exchange. A third party must temporarily park one property.

If you are evaluating this structure for minerals, begin with [the 2026 mineral rights & royalties 1031 guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html).

## What is a reverse 1031 exchange?

A [reverse 1031 exchange](reverse-1031-exchange.html) flips the ordinary order: the replacement property is acquired before the relinquished property is sold, while seeking deferral under Section 1031. The same-taxpayer and exchange rules do not allow an owner simply to buy the replacement, sell the old property later, and retroactively label the two transactions an exchange. A bridge is necessary.

That bridge is a parking arrangement. The exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) temporarily takes legal title to one property, usually the replacement, while you sell the relinquished property. Once the sale is complete, the EAT transfers the parked property to you and the proceeds satisfy the exchange. Parking makes a buy-first sequence work within the exchange rules.

Revenue Procedure 2000-37 supplies the IRS safe harbor. Under it, the EAT holds the parked property under a qualified exchange accommodation arrangement and the exchange must finish within specified time limits. The structure is recognized and IRS-sanctioned, but it is more involved than a forward exchange.

## When mineral owners need one

Mineral owners use a reverse exchange when a replacement opportunity arrives before the old interest can be sold. Qualifying minerals are hard to source. A rare, attractive package or ideal property may not wait, and losing it can mean settling for a poorer replacement. The reverse structure allows the owner to secure it and arrange the sale afterward.

It also applies to a competitive or time-sensitive purchase where the replacement seller will not accept a sale contingency. If a prompt closing is needed to win the asset, there may be no time to sell the relinquished property within a forward exchange structure.

It can also help when relinquished interests are slow to sell, as can occur with [minerals being sold to defer the gain](how-to-sell-mineral-rights-defer-capital-gains-1031.html) in a fragmented market. Instead of losing a desirable replacement, the owner buys through a reverse exchange and then sells the mineral interest. The common issue is timing: the replacement arrives before the sale can be completed.

> Qualifying minerals can be hard to source. A rare replacement may show up before you sell, and a reverse exchange lets you secure it now and sell afterward.

## The role of the exchange accommodation titleholder

The EAT is the linchpin. It is a special-purpose entity, commonly formed by the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) or a related accommodation company, that holds legal title to the parked property under the Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor. In the usual exchange-last structure, it holds the replacement while you sell the relinquished property, then transfers the replacement to you.

The EAT holds title under a qualified exchange accommodation arrangement: a written agreement confirming the purpose. During the parking period, the parties make arrangements for operations, income, expenses, and financing. You generally lease or manage the parked property and fund its acquisition, often through a loan to the EAT, because the EAT is not intended to be its economic owner. It holds title rather than bearing the economics.

By keeping title to one property with the EAT, the arrangement avoids simultaneous ownership that would break the exchange. Its temporary ownership bridges the replacement acquisition and relinquished sale, then it conveys title to finish the exchange.

## Timing and financing challenges

The clocks are demanding. Under the safe harbor, the relinquished property generally must be identified within 45 days after the EAT acquires the parked property. The full exchange—including sale of the relinquished property and conveyance of the parked property—must be completed within 180 days. The familiar 45- and 180-day periods apply, but they now bear on your sale instead of your search for a replacement. That is especially challenging for slow-selling minerals.

Financing is the other hurdle. You must fund the replacement before receiving proceeds from the relinquished sale. That usually requires capital up front, often a bridge loan or other financing that lets the EAT acquire the property. Financing an EAT-held property can be more complicated than a normal purchase because the lender has to accept the arrangement.

The structure asks for careful planning, adequate capital or financing, and a realistic view of whether the old property can sell in time. It is powerful, but it requires greater timing discipline and financial capacity than a forward exchange.

## Costs vs. benefits

Reverse exchanges cost more. The EAT, qualified exchange accommodation arrangement, added legal and accommodation fees, bridge financing, and carrying costs all add expense and complexity beyond a forward exchange. The owner generally bears holding costs during parking and should expect a meaningfully higher cost.

The benefit is keeping a replacement that would otherwise be lost. For an exceptional mineral package or property, the value may be the [capital gains tax deferred](capital-gains-tax-selling-mineral-rights-defer.html) on a transaction that could not otherwise be completed as an exchange. The question is whether the value of acquiring this specific replacement now exceeds the added cost and complexity.

For routine exchanges, a forward exchange with a fast-closing DST backup is generally simpler and cheaper. The reverse exchange is a specialized choice for a replacement that cannot wait. Weigh its additional cost and risk with your advisor and CPA.

## Key Takeaways

- A reverse 1031 exchange acquires the replacement before the relinquished property is sold while deferring gain.
- An exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) parks one property under the Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor.
- You fund the replacement up front, identify the relinquished property within 45 days, and sell it within 180 days.
- The structure costs more and is justified when a specific exceptional replacement will not wait.

## Executing a reverse exchange well

Bring in the right professionals early. You need a QI or accommodation company experienced with reverse exchanges and the EAT structure, a CPA to model tax and eligibility, an attorney for conveyances and the qualified exchange accommodation arrangement, and, if needed, a lender comfortable with parking arrangements. There is little room for improvisation.

Plan the relinquished sale honestly. The 180-day pressure is on that sale, and minerals can be slow to move. If there is real doubt that the property can sell within the window, the central risk is loss of deferral. Pricing to sell and lining up likely buyers in advance can help.

Confirm eligibility on both sides before starting. Replacement minerals or property must be qualifying real property, the relinquished interest must qualify, and value and debt math must support full deferral. The reverse structure adds complexity but does not change those fundamentals. An experienced team, realistic sale plan, and confirmed eligibility can preserve an opportunity a forward exchange could not, but the structure rewards planning and punishes improvisation.

## Reverse vs. forward: choosing the structure

Most owners should begin with a forward exchange. Selling first, then buying, is simpler and cheaper when you can market the relinquished property and identify replacements within the deadline. A fast-closing DST backup often handles the fear of missing the identification period. If a certain-to-close DST can be the replacement, a forward exchange usually works.

The reverse structure earns its added cost when buying first is truly necessary: a particular exceptional replacement appears, will not wait, and no backup is an adequate substitute. A rare mineral package or uniquely attractive property can meet that test. The issue is whether the replacement is special enough to justify the cost and the risk of selling the old property under the 180-day clock.

There is a middle path. Sometimes a longer replacement closing or a contingency tied to your sale turns the situation back into a manageable forward exchange. Explore those simpler alternatives before committing to a costlier structure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a reverse 1031 exchange?

It lets you acquire replacement property before selling relinquished property while still deferring gain. Because you cannot hold title to both without breaking the exchange, an EAT parks one property until sale. It is governed by the Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor.

### When would a mineral owner need a reverse exchange?

When an exceptional mineral package or ideal property appears before the relinquished property can sell and would be lost if the owner waited. It can also address a time-sensitive acquisition or slow-selling minerals. The replacement comes before the sale.

### What is an exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT)?

An EAT is a special-purpose entity, often formed by the QI or an accommodation company, that holds legal title to parked property under the Rev. Proc. 2000-37 safe harbor. Typically it holds the replacement until the relinquished property sells, then conveys it to the taxpayer.

### What is Revenue Procedure 2000-37?

It is the IRS safe harbor for reverse exchanges. It approves a parking structure where an EAT holds title under a qualified exchange accommodation arrangement if requirements, including time limits, are met. It makes reverse exchanges a recognized IRS-sanctioned structure.

### How does the timing work in a reverse exchange?

The same 45- and 180-day clocks apply, but they pressure the sale. Identify the relinquished property within 45 days after the EAT acquires the parked property and complete the exchange, including the sale, within 180 days. Slow-selling minerals make that deadline difficult.

### How is a reverse exchange financed?

You must acquire the replacement before receiving sale proceeds, often using a bridge loan or other financing that allows the EAT to acquire the parked property. This up-front funding requirement is significant, and lenders must be comfortable financing a parking arrangement.

### Why is a reverse exchange more expensive?

The EAT, qualified exchange accommodation arrangement, legal and accommodation fees, frequent bridge financing, and carrying costs all add expense beyond a forward exchange. It is therefore reserved for a specific replacement whose value justifies the cost.

### Is a reverse exchange worth the cost?

It can be when an exceptional replacement would be lost by selling first; the added cost preserves a deferral on a deal that otherwise could not be completed as an exchange. For routine situations, a forward exchange with a fast-closing DST backup is simpler, cheaper, and sufficient. Compare the specific replacement's value with the extra cost with your advisor.

### What's the main risk of a reverse exchange?

The relinquished property may not sell within 180 days, unwinding deferral. Since minerals can be slow to sell, realistically test marketability, price to sell, and line up likely buyers in advance.

### Do I hold title to both properties in a reverse exchange?

No. The EAT holds legal title to one property, generally the replacement, while you sell the other. You fund and usually manage the parked property, but the EAT holds title until it transfers the property after the sale.

### Can I do a reverse exchange into minerals or a DST?

Yes. The replacement can be qualifying minerals, conventional real estate, or a DST if it is like-kind real property. Reverse governs the order of operations; eligibility follows the rules for any exchange and should be confirmed with a tax adviser.

### Who do I need on my team for a reverse exchange?

Use a QI or accommodation company experienced with reverse exchanges and EATs, a CPA for tax and eligibility, an attorney for conveyances and the qualified exchange accommodation arrangement, and a lender comfortable with the structure if financing is needed. Assemble them early.

### Should I use a reverse exchange or just a forward exchange with a DST backup?

Default to forward with a DST backup: it is simpler and cheaper, and the backup handles the fear of not identifying a replacement. Reserve reverse for a specific exceptional replacement that will not wait and cannot be replaced by a backup. A certain-to-close DST usually makes forward sufficient.

### Can I negotiate around needing a reverse exchange?

Often. A longer replacement closing or a contingency tied to your sale may turn the purchase back into a forward exchange. Explore those options before choosing the more costly reverse structure.

### What is an 'exchange last' versus 'exchange first' reverse structure?

They are parking variants. In exchange last, the EAT holds the replacement until the relinquished property sells. In exchange first, it can hold the relinquished property. Exchange last is more common; the accommodation company and attorney choose the fitting form.

### How long can the EAT hold the parked property?

Under the Rev. Proc. 2000-37 safe harbor, generally up to 180 days. The property must transfer to you, and the exchange must complete, inside that period. Beyond it, safe-harbor protection is at risk.

### Can a reverse exchange be combined with an improvement exchange?

Yes. A reverse-improvement exchange parks the replacement with the EAT while improvements are completed and paid within the window, then transfers the improved property. It combines parking and construction timing, is more complex than standard reverse, and requires experienced counsel.

## Glossary

**Reverse 1031 Exchange.** An exchange in which replacement property is acquired before relinquished property is sold.

**Forward Exchange.** The standard 1031 structure: sell relinquished property first, then acquire replacement property.

**Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT).** The entity holding title to parked property in a reverse exchange under the safe harbor.

**Parking Arrangement.** The structure in which an EAT temporarily holds title to enable a reverse exchange.

**Parked Property.** The property, usually the replacement, held by the EAT until the relinquished property is sold.

**Revenue Procedure 2000-37.** The IRS safe harbor governing reverse exchanges and the EAT parking structure.

**Qualified Exchange Accommodation Arrangement (QEAA).** The written agreement under which an EAT holds parked property within the safe harbor.

**Bridge Loan.** Short-term financing used to fund the replacement acquisition before the relinquished property sells.

**45-Day Identification Period.** In a reverse exchange, the window to identify the relinquished property to be sold.

**180-Day Exchange Period.** The window to complete the full reverse exchange, including sale of relinquished property.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI).** The party that, with an accommodation company, often forms and operates the EAT.

**Same-Taxpayer Rule.** The taxpayer who sells relinquished property must also acquire the replacement.

**Carrying Costs.** Expenses of holding parked property during the exchange period, generally borne by the taxpayer.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST).** A securitized real-property interest that can serve as replacement or backup in a reverse exchange.

**Relinquished Property.** The property sold in the exchange; in a reverse exchange, it is sold after acquisition of the replacement.

**Boot.** Cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange; taxable up to the gain amount.
