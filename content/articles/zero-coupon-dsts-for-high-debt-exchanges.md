# Zero-Coupon DSTs for High-Debt Exchanges

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › Zero-Coupon DSTs for High-Debt Exchanges

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about a familiar exchange constraint: a seller has ample equity, but an even larger debt-replacement number. The easy reaction is to ask where the income is. The more useful question is what job the replacement needs to do.

First-order thinking sees a DST with little or no current distribution and dismisses it. Second-order thinking recognizes that a zero-coupon DST can be a specific debt-replacement tool. It uses property cash flow to amortize a high-leverage mortgage, rather than paying that cash flow out. That may solve an exchange need, but it also exchanges income today for an uncertain, back-loaded result later.

Most Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs) are selected for current income. A zero-coupon DST is the opposite: essentially all net cash flow pays down its mortgage instead of being distributed. It may help a highly leveraged 1031 seller replace debt while equity builds through amortization and any sale appreciation. DST interests are securities; distributions and returns are never guaranteed. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice, so confirm current rules with your advisors.

## What Is a Zero-Coupon DST?

A zero-coupon DST is a high-leverage [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) that directs substantially all net operating cash flow to mortgage principal and interest. Investors receive little or no current income. The comparison is to a zero-coupon bond: no periodic interest, with the return realized at the end rather than along the way. Here, the potential return comes from principal paydown and any appreciation when the property is sold.

These structures often use roughly **50%** to **65%** loan-to-value, or more, and the loan is designed to amortize aggressively. Rent, after operating expenses, pays the debt rather than distributions. Falling principal increases trust equity even if the market value does not change. Like other DSTs, the trust holds real property; investors own fractional beneficial interests treated as like-kind real property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, so those interests can serve as 1031 replacement property. What distinguishes this version is the use of cash flow: retire debt and build equity, rather than produce current income.

## When High Debt Replacement Is Needed

A fully deferred 1031 exchange requires reinvesting net equity and replacing at least as much debt as was on the relinquished property. If the seller does not replace debt, the gap is [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html)—taxable value received in the exchange—and deferral is lost on that portion.

That can be as demanding as the equity test. Income-oriented DSTs often have moderate leverage, which may not supply enough debt for a seller of a highly mortgaged property, especially when that seller wants to place only a modest slice of equity into the debt-replacement sleeve. A zero-coupon DST can supply more non-recourse replacement debt per dollar of equity without the investor bringing additional cash or personally borrowing. This is why it exists: high relinquished-property debt can require a high-leverage replacement.

## How the Structure Works

The DST acquires property with a high-loan-to-value, non-recourse mortgage that is structured to fully amortize—to pay down to zero, or near zero, over the hold through regular principal-and-interest payments. Rent, after operating expenses and reserves, goes to those payments. With much of the cash flow committed to debt service and principal reduction, little or nothing remains for investor distributions.

Equity is the payoff. Each dollar of principal reduction increases net equity, even if the property’s value is unchanged; rent is effectively being converted into forced savings. At sale, commonly after **five-to-seven years**, although some programs run longer to allow further amortization, investors receive their pro-rata share of net sale proceeds: the equity built through debt paydown plus any appreciation. The return is back-loaded and realized at sale. Projections are not guarantees, and high leverage introduces interest-rate and refinancing considerations.

## Trade-Offs: Low Income, Debt Paydown

The trade-off is direct. The investor gives up current income in exchange for high debt replacement and potential equity buildup. For someone who needs replacement-property cash flow for living expenses, obligations, or portfolio yield, a zero-coupon DST is a poor fit. For someone who does not need income and is focused on completing an exchange, deferring tax, estate planning, or building equity through loan paydown, the same feature can be useful.

Leverage amplifies both directions. If the property performs and the loan amortizes as projected, equity can build. If operations weaken or a sale occurs into a soft market, the thinner equity cushion can impair return. Interest-rate and refinancing risk matter, particularly where debt must be refinanced before payoff. Minimal distributions mean there is little ongoing cash flow to offset these risks. As with all DSTs, interests are illiquid through the hold, fees apply, and results are projections rather than promises.

## Key takeaways

- A zero-coupon DST uses cash flow to amortize its mortgage instead of paying current income.
- It can help a 1031 investor with high relinquished-property debt replace that debt and seek full capital-gains tax deferral.
- Potential return is back-loaded: loan paydown and any appreciation are realized at sale, not distributed during the hold.
- It suits an investor who does not need current income. High leverage adds interest-rate and refinancing considerations, and no outcome is guaranteed.

## Suitability Considerations

The central suitability question is simple: do you need current income? If yes, this is the wrong tool. If no, its debt-replacement capacity may meet an exchange requirement.

The clearest candidate is a 1031 exchanger with substantial relinquished-property debt who needs to replace it to fully defer and has other income sources, an estate-planning focus, or a preference for forced equity buildup. A retiree relying on DST distributions for living expenses, by contrast, would be poorly served by minimal or absent cash flow. The investor must also accept leverage and a back-loaded, non-guaranteed return.

Like all DST interests, a zero-coupon DST is a security offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors under Regulation D following a suitability review. That review considers goals, finances, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. It is especially important for a low-income, high-leverage structure. See the [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html) and [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) resources for related definitions.

## Fitting a Zero-Coupon DST Into Your Exchange

A zero-coupon DST usually belongs in a broader plan rather than standing alone. Begin with the relinquished-property numbers: net equity to reinvest and debt to replace. The structure is most useful where the debt requirement is large relative to the equity requirement, because a modest equity allocation can carry substantial non-recourse replacement debt.

Many investors pair it with income-oriented DSTs: those investments absorb most equity and provide current cash flow, while the zero-coupon DST supplies debt that might otherwise leave taxable boot. The goal is to satisfy both equity and debt tests while reserving income production for the part of the exchange that needs it. Each replacement must be identified within the **45-day** window under the three-property or **200%** rule. This is a targeted debt-replacement component, not a whole-exchange answer.

The prudent capital-management stance is to match the instrument to the exchange problem, then test liquidity, leverage, and the possibility that projected equity does not materialize. Do you have a genuine debt-replacement gap, and can you accept no current income while it is being addressed?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a zero-coupon DST?

It is a high-leverage DST that uses substantially all net cash flow to pay down a mortgage rather than distribute income. Investors receive little or no current income. Potential return comes from equity built by loan paydown and any appreciation at sale. The fractional beneficial interests can serve as 1031 replacement property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86.

### Why would a 1031 investor use a zero-coupon DST?

Primarily to replace a large amount of debt. Full deferral requires reinvesting net equity and replacing relinquished-property debt; a shortfall is taxable boot. High loan-to-value can supply more non-recourse debt per dollar of equity than a moderate-leverage income DST, without a personal mortgage, for an investor who does not need current income.

### How does a zero-coupon DST generate a return if it pays no income?

Rent after operating expenses pays a fully amortizing mortgage. Principal reduction increases equity, even if market value is flat. The investor’s pro-rata net sale proceeds, commonly after **five-to-seven years** but sometimes longer, reflect debt paydown plus any appreciation. Property performance, rates, leverage, and sale price affect the result; it is realized only at sale and is not guaranteed.

### What loan-to-value do zero-coupon DSTs typically carry?

They often use roughly **50%** to **65%** loan-to-value or more. Exact terms vary by offering, sponsor, property type, and lending conditions. Review the stated loan terms, amortization schedule, and refinancing requirements. The debt is generally non-recourse, but higher leverage amplifies gains and losses and increases rate and refinancing sensitivity.

### Is a zero-coupon DST riskier than a regular DST?

It has a different and sometimes heightened risk profile because of its high leverage and lack of current distributions. A weaker property or sale at a weak point can harm the thinner equity cushion. It also has standard DST risks: illiquidity, fees, sponsor and property risk, and projections rather than guarantees. It is not simply a higher- or lower-yield version of a regular DST.

### Who is a zero-coupon DST suitable for?

It may suit an accredited 1031 investor with high debt to replace who does not need current cash flow, has other income or an estate-planning focus, and accepts high leverage and a back-loaded result. It is generally unsuitable for someone who relies on real-estate income for living expenses. A Regulation D suitability review confirms the fit.

### Does a zero-coupon DST help me defer all of my capital-gains tax?

It can help, but it is not an automatic guarantee. It addresses the debt side of the exchange by supplying high replacement debt. Full deferral still requires reinvesting all net equity, sometimes across this DST and other replacement property. Work with the QI and CPA to confirm the specific equity and debt amounts.

### What does “non-recourse debt” mean in a zero-coupon DST?

The loan is secured by the property and generally does not create personal liability for individual investors beyond the DST’s assets. The lender’s recourse is ordinarily limited to the property and trust assets, although misconduct-related “bad-boy” carve-outs can apply. The trust-level debt can count as the investor’s share of replacement debt; confirm the loan terms in the offering documents.

### How long is the hold period for a zero-coupon DST?

It is generally a multi-year, illiquid commitment, commonly **five-to-seven years**, though some programs run longer so more debt can amortize. The sponsor controls the sale timing, which depends on market conditions. Investors typically stay until sale because there is little or no secondary market. Review the anticipated hold and amortization schedule in the specific offering.

### Can I receive any distributions from a zero-coupon DST?

Most have minimal or no current distributions because cash flow pays down debt. Some may make a very small distribution, but that is not the purpose of the structure and no distribution is guaranteed. An investor requiring current cash flow should consider an income-oriented DST instead and review the offering’s distribution policy.

### What happens to a zero-coupon DST at the end of the hold?

The sponsor sells the property and investors receive pro-rata net sale proceeds reflecting amortization-driven equity plus any appreciation. An investor can take proceeds and recognize deferred gain, or may pursue a new 1031 exchange to continue deferring, including into another DST or replacement property. Some investors chain exchanges and some hold until death, when heirs may receive a step-up in basis that can eliminate deferred tax. Sale price and tax results are not guaranteed; ask your advisors about your facts.

### Does high leverage make a zero-coupon DST sensitive to interest rates?

Yes. The loan rate affects the split of cash flow between interest and principal, and refinancing can slow amortization or strain debt service. Even fixed-rate, fully amortizing debt can be affected by a broader rate environment through property values and eventual sale price. Many programs use fixed-rate, fully amortizing debt to manage this risk, but review the rate, amortization schedule, and refinancing provisions.

### Is a zero-coupon DST a guaranteed way to build equity?

No. Debt paydown is designed to increase equity, but property performance and sale price determine the outcome. Weak operations, declining occupancy, a weak market sale, interest rates, and refinancing can reduce return. High leverage amplifies downside. Any equity projection is an estimate, not a promise.

### Are zero-coupon DSTs only for accredited investors?

Yes. They are Regulation D securities generally available only to accredited investors who meet applicable income or net-worth thresholds. Accreditation alone is not enough: suitability review considers finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. The interests are offered through a broker-dealer through a subscription process, not bought on an exchange.

## Glossary

### Zero-Coupon DST

A high-leverage DST that amortizes debt instead of paying current income.

### Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)

A trust holding real estate in which investors own fractional interests.

### Debt Replacement

Matching relinquished-property debt to fully defer in a 1031 exchange.

### Loan-to-Value (LTV)

The loan amount as a percentage of property value.

### Amortization

Paying down loan principal over time through regular payments.

### Non-Recourse Debt

A loan with no personal liability beyond the property and trust.

### Boot

Taxable value received in an exchange, including unreplaced debt.

### Current Income

Distributions paid during the hold; these are minimal in this structure.

### Equity Buildup

The increase in trust equity as the loan balance is paid down.

### Back-Loaded Return

A return realized primarily at sale rather than along the way.

### Beneficial Interest

An investor’s fractional ownership in a DST, treated as real property.

### Revenue Ruling 2004-86

The IRS ruling that makes DST interests 1031-eligible like-kind property.

### Full Cycle

The point at which a DST sells its property and returns proceeds to investors.

### Accredited Investor

An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Regulation D offerings.

### Suitability Review

The process of confirming that an offering fits an investor before investing.

### Qualified Intermediary (QI)

The party that holds 1031 proceeds and facilitates the exchange.
