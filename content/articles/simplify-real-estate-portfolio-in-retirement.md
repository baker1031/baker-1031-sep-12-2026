# Simplify Your Real Estate Portfolio in Retirement

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 UPREIT Exchanges · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read

I keep seeing the same retirement question behind a very practical set of properties: how do you keep the equity and the income, while giving up the work?

For owners who have accumulated several rentals, the answer often has to solve three things at once: less management, more reliable passive income, and an estate that heirs can handle without being forced into co-ownership or a sale. A tax-aware path can be a 1031 exchange from active properties into passive, professionally managed holdings—principally Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs). Those interests can consolidate scattered rentals or diversify a concentrated position across markets and sectors while deferring gain. Later, a 721 (UPREIT) exchange can roll DST or property interests into operating-partnership units of a REIT, turning multiple buildings into a single diversified, income-producing holding that can be simpler to value, manage, and pass on. If held until death, the portfolio may receive a step-up in basis that erases deferred gain for heirs. These are securities for accredited investors, sold after a suitability review; this is educational information, not tax advice, and a CPA should confirm the tax treatment.

First-order thinking says that selling every rental is the cleanest way to simplify. Second-order thinking asks what that sale costs in capital-gains and depreciation-recapture taxes, what replaces the income, and what choices remain after the tax bill is paid. The point is not to chase an elegant structure. It is to decide whether less control and less complexity are worth the trade-offs.

## The Problem a Multi-Property Portfolio Creates in Retirement

Directly owned rentals can be a wealth-building machine during working years and an administrative burden in retirement. Several properties in different locations can mean ongoing management, uneven performance, concentration in particular markets or property types, and a lumpy, illiquid estate. When active management is no longer appealing—or no longer feasible—and predictable income matters more, the portfolio's complexity becomes the problem. The estate issue is just as real: dividing physical buildings fairly among multiple heirs can force co-ownership, co-management, or a sale.

Selling everything would end the management work, but it could also trigger a large capital-gains and depreciation-recapture bill across all appreciated properties at once. The objective, then, is to simplify and become passive without a forced taxable liquidation. That is what the 1031, the DST, and the 721 can be used to address.

## Consolidate and Diversify With DSTs

A 1031 exchange into DSTs is a central tool for portfolio simplification. Because a DST interest is like-kind real property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, one or more active properties can be exchanged into fractional DST interests while gain is deferred. The structure works in both directions. Several scattered rentals can be consolidated into a cleaner group of passive holdings managed by professionals. One concentrated property can be diversified across several DSTs in different markets and asset classes; see [building a diversified DST portfolio by asset class](building-a-diversified-dst-portfolio-by-asset-class.html).

The daily work ends with the owner. A sponsor handles acquisition, leasing, maintenance, and the eventual sale, while the investor receives passive income over a defined hold, commonly around five to seven years. For income-oriented retirement planning, see [REIT vs. DST for retirement income](reit-vs-dst-for-retirement-income.html). The trade-offs are not cosmetic: DSTs are illiquid, they carry fees, and distributions are projections rather than guarantees. Position size and diversification still require care.

The portfolio that built wealth does not have to be the one managed in retirement. A 1031 into DSTs can preserve equity and income while transferring the work.

## The 721/UPREIT Step: One Diversified Holding

The 721 exchange, named for IRC §721, can be the consolidation endgame for estate simplicity. In a [721 or UPREIT exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), real property—often DST interests at the end of their hold—is contributed to a REIT's operating partnership in exchange for operating-partnership (OP) units, without triggering tax on the contribution. Multiple buildings or DST interests can become units in one large, diversified, professionally managed REIT. A common sequence is to 1031 into a DST first, then make the 721 contribution when the DST goes full-cycle; see [from DST to REIT: the 721 roll-up](from-dst-to-reit-721-rollup.html).

For a retiree who values simplicity, OP units can be easier to hold, value, and eventually divide than scattered physical properties. They are a single line item with broad diversification and regular income. The counterweight, explored in [trading control for liquidity](721-upreit-trading-control-for-liquidity.html), is that a 721 is generally a one-way move. Once assets are in OP units, they cannot be exchanged out through a 1031; converting OP units into REIT shares later is a taxable event. A 721 therefore fits an owner who intends to remain passive for the long run and values consolidation and eventual liquidity more than the ability to keep exchanging.

## Estate Simplicity and the Step-Up

Passive holdings can pay their largest dividend in estate planning. Fractional DST interests and REIT OP units can be divided among heirs more readily than physical buildings: specified shares or units can be bequeathed without requiring children to co-own and co-manage real estate. The deferral may also become permanent. If DST interests or OP units are held until death, heirs may receive a [step-up in basis](721-exchange-estate-planning.html) under IRC §1014 that can erase gain deferred across the original properties. The same choices that simplify life now can simplify an estate and remove carried-forward tax for heirs.

### Key points to keep in view

- A multi-property portfolio can create management burden, concentration, and a hard-to-divide estate in retirement, while a full sale can trigger a large tax bill.
- A 1031 into DSTs can consolidate scattered rentals or diversify a concentrated property into passive, professionally managed holdings while deferring gain.
- A 721 (UPREIT) exchange can roll DST or property interests into REIT OP units—one diversified, income-producing holding—but it is generally a one-way move.
- DST interests or OP units held until death may receive a step-up in basis under IRC §1014 that erases deferred gain and makes division among heirs easier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I simplify my real estate portfolio in retirement without a big tax bill?

The tax-aware path is to use a 1031 exchange to move active properties into passive holdings rather than sell outright. Selling appreciated rentals all at once can trigger a large capital-gains and depreciation-recapture bill; a 1031 permits reinvestment into like-kind real property—principally DSTs—while deferring that gain. With DSTs, scattered rentals can be consolidated into professionally managed holdings, or a concentrated property can be diversified across several markets and sectors, and a sponsor handles management so the income is passive. A 721 (UPREIT) exchange can then roll DST or property interests into REIT OP units, turning multiple buildings into a single diversified, income-producing holding that is easier to value and pass on. If the interests are held until death, heirs may receive a step-up in basis that erases deferred gain. Illiquidity, fees, and 721 irreversibility are material trade-offs, so confirm the plan and tax treatment with a CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### What is a 721 (UPREIT) exchange and how does it help simplify?

A 721 exchange, named for IRC §721 and often called an UPREIT transaction, permits a contribution of real property—frequently DST interests at the end of their hold—to a REIT operating partnership in exchange for OP units, without triggering tax on the contribution. For a retiree focused on simplicity, multiple buildings or DST interests become units in one large, diversified, professionally managed REIT. OP units are a single line item with broad diversification and regular income, and can be easier to hold, value, and divide among heirs than physical properties. A common sequence is a 1031 into a DST followed by a 721 into the REIT when the DST goes full-cycle. The key caveat is that a 721 is generally a one-way move: OP units cannot later be used in a 1031 exchange, and a later conversion into REIT shares is taxable. It is a long-term passive decision that favors consolidation and eventual liquidity over continued exchanging. Confirm eligibility and timing with a CPA.

### Should I consolidate into one holding or diversify across several DSTs?

The answer depends on the starting point and goals, and the choices are not mutually exclusive. Owners of several scattered rentals may consolidate their equity into a cleaner set of passive DST holdings—and potentially later one REIT position through a 721—to reduce the number of moving parts. Owners of one large concentrated property may instead prioritize diversification through several DSTs across markets and asset classes, reducing dependence on a single building or local economy. Many retirees do both: consolidate administrative complexity while diversifying the underlying exposure, ending with a small number of diversified, professionally managed positions rather than numerous concentrated, self-managed properties. Diversification reduces concentration risk but does not eliminate investment risk. Each DST still carries fees, illiquidity, and sponsor risk. The right structure depends on income needs, risk tolerance, estate plan, and the value placed on simplicity versus control; map it with an advisor and CPA.

### How does consolidating into passive holdings help my heirs?

It helps through divisibility and basis. Fractional DST interests and REIT OP units can be split more fairly among multiple heirs than physical buildings. Instead of asking children to co-own and co-manage real estate—or sell properties to divide proceeds—an owner can leave specified shares or units, each a clean, valuable, income-producing line item. If DST interests or OP units are held until death, heirs may receive a step-up in basis under IRC §1014 to fair market value as of the date of death, which can erase capital gain deferred across the original properties. Consolidation can therefore simplify retirement and the estate while eliminating carried-forward tax for the next generation. Estate, basis, and timing rules are individual, and a 721 is generally irreversible, so coordinate with a CPA and estate attorney before acting.

### What are the risks and trade-offs of moving from direct ownership to DSTs and a REIT?

The trade-offs are substantial. Direct control is given up: a DST sponsor or the REIT manages assets and the investor cannot make property-level decisions. DSTs are illiquid, with a multi-year hold and little or no secondary market. OP units are also illiquid; liquidity typically comes only through conversion to REIT shares, which is a taxable event. Both structures have fees that reduce net returns, and distributions are projections rather than guarantees dependent on underlying real estate performance. A 721 into a REIT is generally a one-way move, so it should reflect a long-term decision to remain passive. There are sponsor, management, and underlying-property market risks, and strict 1031 and 721 rules mean timing or procedural missteps can disqualify deferral. Less management, diversification, income, estate simplicity, and a possible step-up at death must be worth the costs for the individual situation, which a suitability review and CPA help assess. Returns are never guaranteed, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible fractional real estate.

**721 / UPREIT Exchange:** Contributing property for REIT operating-partnership units (IRC §721).

**OP Units:** Operating-partnership units received in a 721 exchange.

**REIT:** A real estate investment trust holding a diversified property portfolio.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling treating a DST interest as real property for 1031.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset at death (IRC §1014) that can erase deferred gain.

**Diversification:** Spreading exposure across markets and asset classes to reduce concentration.

**Full-Cycle:** When a DST's sponsor sells the property and the investment ends.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds under SEC Rule 501.
