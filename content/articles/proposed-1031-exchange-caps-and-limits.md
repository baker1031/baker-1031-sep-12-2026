# Proposed 1031 Exchange Caps & Limits

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I have been seeing the same policy question return whenever an investor has a large embedded gain: “Should I change the plan because someone proposed changing 1031?” The question is understandable. It is also where a point-in-time proposal can be mistaken for the rule that exists today.

The first-order response is to hear “$500,000 cap” and assume the exchange has been capped. The second-order response is to separate a proposed direction from enacted law, then ask what the rule is now, who would actually be exposed if a proposal passed, and what an effective date would say. As of **2026**, none of the proposed 1031 caps or frequency limits discussed here has become law.

The leading proposal would limit deferrable gain to **$500,000** per taxpayer, or **$1,000,000** for married couples filing jointly. Other ideas have included limits on how often a person can use the exchange, including a one-exchange-per-lifetime concept. They are worth understanding because policy risk can matter, especially for a large gain or a long serial-exchange strategy. They are not current rules. Investors should use current authoritative sources and their advisors to verify any present status before acting. For related policy context, see [this discussion of whether the 1031 exchange is going away](is-the-1031-exchange-going-away-policy-outlook.html).

## The Proposals on the Table

Several proposals to limit the [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) have appeared in budget documents and policy discussions. The clearest has been a cap on deferrable gain. Other concepts would limit exchange frequency or attach restrictions to a broader tax-reform package.

Their common purpose is to reduce the [tax deferral the exchange provides](capital-gains-tax-deferral-1031-exchange.html), generally to raise revenue or limit what policymakers view as a tax advantage. Their mechanics differ. A deferral cap limits the amount of gain that can be deferred in an exchange or by a taxpayer. A frequency limit constrains the number of times a taxpayer can use the exchange. The two approaches would affect different investors and transactions.

That distinction is not academic. Current rules still allow full deferral of qualifying gain and do not impose a cap or a frequency limit. The proposals identify possibilities policymakers have considered. They do not describe the law an exchanger uses today.

## The $500,000 Deferral Cap

The most concrete proposal was advanced in budget proposals during the Biden administration. It would limit gain deferred through a like-kind exchange to **$500,000** per taxpayer per year, or **$1,000,000** for married couples filing jointly (MFJ). Gain above the threshold would be recognized and taxed in the year of the exchange rather than deferred.

For example, a single taxpayer with **$1,500,000** of gain could defer **$500,000** and recognize the remaining **$1,000,000**. Put in the shorter form used to describe the proposal, a **$1.5 million** gain would leave **$1 million** subject to tax after the **$500,000** deferral. An exchange with gain below the proposed threshold would remain fully deferred. In that sense, the proposal was designed to target large gains while preserving the exchange for smaller transactions.

That description is mechanical, not a statement of current law. The proposed cap was never enacted. No **$500,000** cap applies to a qualifying 1031 exchange as of **2026**.

## One-Exchange-Per-Lifetime and Frequency Ideas

A frequency limit would take a different approach. Instead of limiting how much gain can be deferred, it would limit how often the exchange can be used. The most discussed version is a one-exchange-per-lifetime idea.

Such a limit would affect the [serial-exchange strategy](1031-exchange-exit-strategies-swap-till-you-drop.html): exchanging property again and again to continue deferring gain, often until a death-time step-up in basis. A cap could leave unlimited below-threshold exchanges available; a frequency restriction could allow a large deferral but only a limited number of times. Once the allowed number had been used, later gain could become taxable.

These ideas are less developed than the proposed **$500,000** cap. They have also not been enacted. The important point is that frequency and amount are different policy levers, and neither is a current 1031 restriction.

## Who Would Be Affected

If enacted, a **$500,000** deferral cap would primarily affect gains above **$500,000** per taxpayer or **$1,000,000** for a couple. Investors below those amounts would generally continue to defer their full qualifying gain. The proposal therefore focuses on larger gains and larger transactions.

A frequency limit would instead focus on investors using repeated exchanges over a long period. Long-term real-estate owners pursuing serial exchanges could face tax after their allowed exchanges, whereas they can exchange indefinitely under current rules.

Many smaller investors, farmers, middle-market investors, and people who exchange infrequently would be less exposed to those particular proposals. Even that is hypothetical, however. Since the proposals are not law, no exchanger is currently subject to these limits.

## Planning Around Potential Limits

The starting point is to plan under the rules that actually exist. The 1031 exchange remains fully available under current rules as of **2026**. Rushing an exchange or avoiding a sound exchange solely because of an unenacted proposal is generally unwarranted.

That does not require ignoring policy. Investors with very large gains or a long serial-exchange plan may reasonably monitor proposals with their advisors. If a law were enacted, it would typically specify an effective date, allowing investors to assess pending transactions, complete exchanges where appropriate, and adjust future plans. The actual legislation—not an early discussion—would control that process.

The balanced approach is neither to overreact nor to assume the law can never change. Use current rules for present needs; monitor meaningful developments; consult current sources, a CPA, and other advisors; and adjust only when there is an enacted rule to analyze.

## Key Takeaways

- A proposed **$500,000** deferral cap (**$1,000,000** MFJ) and frequency ideas, including one-exchange-per-lifetime, have been floated but were not enacted as of **2026**.
- Under the cap proposal, gain above the threshold would be taxed while smaller exchanges could retain full deferral.
- A cap would matter most to large-gain investors; a frequency limit would matter most to long-term serial exchangers.
- Plan under current rules, stay informed through current authoritative sources, and be ready to analyze any enacted change and its effective date.

## The Broader Tax-Policy Context

1031 limits usually appear as part of larger revenue-raising or tax-reform packages. They may sit alongside proposals affecting capital gains, investment income, or the treatment of real estate more generally. That context matters because the fate of the exchange provision often depends on the fate of the whole package.

The **$500,000** proposal, for example, appeared in budgets containing many other tax provisions. As those wider packages stalled or changed, the 1031 provision was not enacted either. A 1031 item in a budget can show a contemplated policy direction, but it does not signal that a limit is imminent or certain.

Legislation depends on broader political and fiscal conditions. The exchange has survived repeated proposals over more than a century, supported by an economic case and a broad constituency of users, but that history is not a prediction. The right conclusion is modest: policy outcomes remain uncertain, so current law should lead current decisions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is there a cap on 1031 exchanges?

Not currently. As of **2026**, no enacted cap limits the gain deferrable through a qualifying 1031 exchange. A **$500,000** proposal, or **$1,000,000** for married couples, appeared in budget proposals but did not become law. Current rules allow full deferral of qualifying gain without a cap. Verify current status with up-to-date sources because policy can change.

### What is the proposed $500,000 cap?

It was a Biden-budget proposal to limit deferred like-kind-exchange gain to **$500,000** per taxpayer and **$1,000,000** for married couples filing jointly. Gain above the threshold would be recognized and taxed. With **$1,500,000** of gain, a single taxpayer would defer **$500,000** and pay tax on **$1,000,000**. It was aimed at large exchanges while leaving smaller gains unaffected, but it was never enacted.

### How would a deferral cap work?

Under the proposal, gain up to **$500,000** per taxpayer or **$1,000,000** MFJ could be deferred. Gain above the limit would be recognized in the exchange year and taxed. Exchanges below the threshold would continue to receive full deferral. This is a description of the proposed mechanism; no cap currently applies.

### What is the one-exchange-per-lifetime idea?

It is a floated concept to limit a taxpayer’s use of the 1031 exchange—such as once during a lifetime—rather than limit the gain deferred in each transaction. It would constrain repeated exchanges used to continue deferral. Unlike a cap, which limits amount, a frequency restriction limits the number of uses. The idea is less developed than the cap and has not become law.

### Who would a $500,000 cap affect?

It would mainly affect investors whose gain exceeds **$500,000** per taxpayer or **$1,000,000** for a couple. Smaller gains would generally remain fully deferrable. Thus, the proposed design targets larger gains and transactions, while smaller exchanges would be largely untouched. Because there is no enacted cap, no one is currently affected.

### Are these caps and limits in effect now?

No. As of **2026**, neither the **$500,000** cap nor frequency concepts are in effect. The exchange currently operates with full qualifying-gain deferral and no cap or frequency limit. A proposal is an indication of a possible direction, not a current rule; verify present status with authoritative sources.

### Should I change my plans because of these proposals?

Generally, no. Current and near-term planning should use the exchange as it actually exists. Rushing or avoiding a transaction because of an unenacted proposal is usually unwarranted. Investors with large gains or long-term serial strategies may want to stay informed, but should not act as if an unpassed proposal is law. A real change would normally provide an effective date and time to adjust. Consult an advisor.

### How should I plan around potential future limits?

Use current rules, monitor credible policy developments, and be ready to adjust only if legislation is enacted. Large-gain and serial-exchange investors may have more reason to follow the issue. Avoid both complacency and overreaction. An enacted law would generally have an effective date, and current sources and advisors should guide any response.

### Why do these proposals appear in budget documents?

They are usually parts of broader revenue-raising or tax-reform packages, alongside other proposed tax changes. A 1031 limit in a budget is one provision in a large fiscal document, many provisions of which never become law. Its fate can be tied to the larger package, as the **$500,000** proposal was when the packages containing it stalled or changed.

### Would a cap apply to exchanges I’ve already done?

Tax changes of this kind typically apply prospectively, after an effective date, rather than retroactively to completed exchanges. If a cap were enacted, the legislation would specify its effective date and application. The point remains hypothetical because no cap has been enacted; consult current sources for any actual law.

### How likely are these proposals to become law?

That is uncertain and not something Baker 1031 predicts. The proposals have been floated repeatedly and have not been enacted as of **2026**. Their prospects depend on political feasibility and the broader tax-policy package around them. The exchange’s long survival, economic case, and broad user base offer context but not certainty. Consult current authoritative sources and advisors for the latest status.

### Where can I track the status of these proposals?

Use current authoritative sources, including Treasury revenue proposals and congressional materials; industry groups such as the Federation of Exchange Accommodators; qualified intermediaries that monitor developments; and your CPA or tax advisor. This subject can change, so verify the current status rather than relying on a fixed-date summary. As of **2026**, no caps or limits are enacted.

### How would a cap interact with a partial exchange or boot?

Under a proposed cap, gain above the threshold would be recognized in the exchange year. That is functionally similar to receiving boot in the sense that an amount of gain becomes taxable, although the detailed interaction with existing boot rules would have to be set by enacting legislation. Today, this is hypothetical: current recognition is caused by actual boot and other applicable rules, not by a cap.

### Could a cap make DSTs more or less attractive?

Hypothetically, a cap could affect how investors with large gains use an exchange, but it would not change DSTs’ basic role as a replacement option for the deferrable portion. Under current rules, DSTs remain fully available as uncapped replacement property. If a cap became law, investors and advisors would assess its effect on strategy and replacement choices at that time.

## Glossary

**Deferral Cap:** A proposed, non-enacted limit on gain deferrable through an exchange, such as **$500,000**.

**$500,000 Cap:** The leading proposal to limit deferral to **$500,000** per taxpayer or **$1,000,000** MFJ and tax the excess.

**One-Exchange-Per-Lifetime:** A floated concept limiting how often a person could use the exchange.

**Frequency Limit:** A limit on the number of exchanges rather than the amount of gain deferred.

**Serial-Exchange Strategy:** Repeated exchanges intended to keep deferring gain over time.

**Recognized Gain:** Gain above a proposed cap that would be taxed in the exchange year.

**Married Filing Jointly (MFJ):** The filing status associated with the proposed **$1,000,000** cap.

**Effective Date:** The date on which an enacted change would begin to apply.

**Prospective Application:** Applying a change to future exchanges rather than completed prior exchanges.

**Budget Proposal:** An administration’s proposed budget, where 1031 limits have appeared.

**Revenue-Raising Package:** A broader fiscal proposal that can include a 1031 limit.

**Tax-Reform Package:** Wider tax legislation in which 1031 proposals may appear.

**Unenacted Proposal:** A proposal that has been floated but not passed into law; all discussed 1031 limits remain in this category as of **2026**.

**Capital Gains Tax:** The tax a 1031 exchange can defer and a source of the proposals’ revenue rationale.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The reset in basis at death relevant to serial-exchange planning.

**Current Rules:** The actual 1031 rules as of **2026**: full qualifying-gain deferral, no cap, and no frequency limit.
