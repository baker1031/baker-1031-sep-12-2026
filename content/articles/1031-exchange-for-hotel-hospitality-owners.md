# 1031 Exchange for Hotel & Hospitality Owners

I keep seeing hotel owners talk about selling a property as though they are selling one asset. They are often selling at least three things at once: land and building, furniture and equipment, and an operating business built around daily rooms and services. The 1031 exchange is available, but it has to be applied to the right part of the sale.

Hotel real estate can qualify for a 1031 exchange. The operating business and personal-property elements make a hotel sale more complicated than a simple apartment or net-leased building sale. Since the **2017** Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA), like-kind treatment is limited to real property. That means the land and building may be exchanged while the furniture, fixtures, equipment (FF&E), and business or goodwill value are treated separately.

First-order thinking is, “The hotel has appreciated, so I can exchange the whole sale.” Second-order thinking is, “How much of the sale price is actual real estate, how much is rapidly depreciated FF&E, what business value is being sold, and does the next property improve the owner’s work, risk, and income position?” The allocation is not a footnote. It is the center of the tax and investment decision.

## Hospitality as investment real estate

A hotel is real estate, but it is a distinctive form of real estate. The land and building are clear real-property assets. On top of them sits an active operation: daily room rentals, food and beverage, services, staff, management, marketing, and guest services. A hotel also carries meaningful FF&E—beds, furnishings, kitchen equipment, and more.

That combination differs from an apartment building or a net-lease asset. A net-lease property may be largely passive, with a tenant operating the business. A hotel is an operating business on real estate. The business value and the FF&E are not real property, even though they are economically important to the hotel.

The hotel’s real-property portion can be [1031-exchange property](1031-exchange-guide.html) when held for investment or productive use. The task is to separate the exchangeable land-and-building value from the non-exchangeable business and personal-property value. The dual nature of a hotel is why experienced professional handling is especially important.

## Why hotel owners exchange

The operational burden is a common reason. Hotel ownership is a **24/7** business: staffing, guests, marketing, services, changing travel demand, and events all require attention. An owner who wants to step back can exchange the qualifying real-property portion into more passive real estate while keeping invested capital in real estate on a tax-deferred basis.

Capturing appreciation is another reason. A hotel owner may have benefited from a strong hospitality market or successful operation and want to realize the property’s appreciated value through repositioning instead of a fully taxable sale. Hospitality is cyclical, so an owner may also want to act at a favorable point in the cycle and move toward a steadier asset.

Risk management matters as well. Hotels are sensitive to economic conditions, travel trends, and events. An exchange can allow an owner to reduce concentrated hospitality exposure and trade active hotel work for a more passive or stable form of real estate.

The exchange is often a hotel owner’s path out of a demanding, cyclical hospitality business and into passive, more stable real estate, tax-deferred.

## The operating-business wrinkle

The issue is allocation. A hotel sale price can include three broad components:

- **Real property:** the land and building, which may qualify for the exchange.
- **Personal property:** FF&E, which does not qualify for like-kind treatment under the post-**2017** TCJA rule.
- **Business or goodwill value:** the going-concern value of the hotel operation, which also does not qualify as real property.

Only the real-property portion can be deferred through the exchange. The CPA and advisors must allocate the sale price among those components. A larger allocation to FF&E or business value leaves less value that can be exchanged and may leave more current taxable value.

There is also a held-for-investment or productive-use question. A 1031 exchange requires that standard, and an owner-operated hotel can raise a question about investment property versus the owner’s active business. Hotels held and operated as investment or productive-use real property generally can qualify for the real-property portion, but the facts require review.

## Trade out of hospitality

Many owners use an exchange to move into a more passive or stable type of real estate. The hotel’s qualifying real-property value can be exchanged into net-lease assets, [multifamily apartments](1031-exchange-for-multifamily-apartment-owners.html), [industrial buildings](1031-exchange-for-industrial-property-owners.html), or passive DST interests.

That can solve two problems at once. Operationally, it moves the owner away from a hotel’s intensive management. From a portfolio standpoint, it can reduce exposure to a cyclical hospitality sector and place capital in a different kind of real estate.

For an owner ready to leave hotel operations entirely, a DST can be the endpoint: passive real-estate ownership, no day-to-day hotel management, and continued tax-deferred investment of the qualifying exchange proceeds. The exchange does not defer the non-real portions, but it can preserve the qualifying real-estate capital for the next phase.

## Hospitality and DSTs

DSTs are often the passive destination for owners trading out of hotels. A hotel owner can exchange the real-property portion into DSTs holding net-lease, multifamily, or industrial properties. That changes active hospitality operation into passive, professionally managed real-estate exposure and may offer a more stable income orientation.

Some DSTs hold hotels, so a hotel owner can retain hospitality exposure on a passive basis. Given the sector’s volatility, however, many owners who are trading out prefer DSTs in other sectors. The question is whether the owner wants passive exposure to hospitality or diversification away from it.

DSTs can address several common hotel-owner objectives: no active management, potential diversification, and tax-deferred reinvestment. They are also illiquid securities, sold only to accredited investors and subject to suitability review. They offer no operational control, so the PPM and the individual offering deserve the same attention as any other investment decision.

## Key takeaways

- A hotel is real estate blended with an operating business and substantial FF&E, which makes its exchange more complex.
- Only the real-property portion qualifies for exchange under the post-**2017** rule; FF&E and business value must be allocated out.
- Owners exchange to leave a demanding operating business, capture appreciation, and reduce cyclical hospitality exposure.
- Moving into passive real estate, often through DSTs, is a common trade-out strategy.
- Hotel exchanges call for experienced CPA and advisor involvement, especially on allocation, recapture, and held-for-investment questions.

## Tax considerations

The real-versus-non-real allocation drives the tax result. The portion assigned to FF&E and business value is not deferred through the exchange and may be currently taxable. Personal property can also carry depreciation recapture.

[Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) is particularly important for hotels because FF&E often has shorter recovery periods and may have been depreciated rapidly. The hotel’s real property uses a **39-year** recovery period, and recapture for the qualifying real-property portion is deferred through a properly structured real-property exchange. FF&E recapture generally is not deferred because FF&E does not qualify.

The four-layer tax stack applies to the taxable portions. The exchange can defer the qualifying real-property tax, but it does not eliminate the need to calculate business value, personal-property value, and related recapture. An experienced CPA should model those pieces before the owner commits to the sale and replacement plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a hotel qualify for a 1031 exchange?

The hotel’s real-property portion can qualify when it is held for investment or productive use. FF&E and business or goodwill value do not qualify under the post-**2017** rule, so they must be allocated out. Hotels can be exchanged; the issue is careful separation of qualifying and non-qualifying components.

### Why are hotel exchanges more complex?

A hotel combines land and building, an active operating business, and FF&E. Only the real property qualifies, which requires allocation. Owner-operated hotels can also raise held-for-investment questions that are not common with simpler net-lease or apartment property.

### What is the operating-business wrinkle?

It is the fact that the sale price includes non-real-property value: the hotel business or goodwill and the FF&E. Only the real-property portion can receive 1031 treatment, while the other portions may be taxable and must be handled separately.

### Why do hotel owners exchange?

They may want to leave **24/7** operations, capture appreciated value while deferring the qualifying real-property tax, or reduce exposure to hospitality’s cycles and volatility by moving to passive or more stable real estate.

### What happens to the FF&E in a hotel exchange?

FF&E is personal property and does not qualify for a 1031 exchange under the **2017** TCJA limitation. It is allocated out and can be taxable, including potential depreciation recapture. The CPA handles it separately from the real-property exchange.

### How is a hotel’s value allocated for an exchange?

The CPA allocates value among the real property (land and building), FF&E, and business or goodwill value. Only the real-property allocation can be deferred. A larger real-property allocation means more deferral, which makes the work important and fact-specific.

### Can I trade my hotel into passive real estate?

Yes. A common strategy is to exchange the hotel’s real-property portion into net-lease property, multifamily, industrial, or DST interests. That can reduce management work and diversify away from hotel-specific risk.

### Is the held-for-investment requirement an issue for hotels?

It can be. An owner-operated hotel raises the question of whether the property is held for investment or productive use versus as an active business. Hotels held and operated as investment or productive-use real property generally can qualify for the real-property portion, but advisors should review the facts.

### How does depreciation recapture work for hotels?

The real property is depreciated over **39 years** and its recapture is deferred through the qualifying real-property exchange. FF&E is often depreciated more rapidly, and its recapture generally is not deferred because FF&E is not exchangeable. This can create meaningful current taxable recapture.

### Are there hospitality DSTs?

Yes. Some DSTs hold hotel assets and can offer passive hospitality exposure. Many hotel owners trading out prefer non-hospitality DSTs—such as net-lease, multifamily, or industrial—for a more diversified, stable-sector position.

### Do I need special help for a hotel exchange?

Yes. The allocation, FF&E recapture, business-value treatment, and held-for-investment analysis make experienced CPA and advisor assistance especially valuable.

### Should I exchange or sell my hotel?

It depends on the owner’s goals. An exchange can preserve more qualifying real-property capital for reinvestment when the owner wants to remain in real estate. A taxable sale can fit an owner who wants to cash out. In either case, the FF&E and business-value portions need separate tax analysis.

### Does it matter whether my hotel is franchised or independent?

It can affect the business-value allocation. A franchised hotel may have franchise and brand value; an independent hotel may have a different mix of going-concern value. In either case, only the real-property portion qualifies and the CPA must analyze the allocation.

### Can a ground lease or leasehold hotel be exchanged?

A leasehold interest in real property with a sufficient remaining term—generally **30+ years** including options—can qualify as like-kind real property. A ground-lease hotel can therefore have an exchangeable leasehold interest, but its qualification and allocation require experienced review.

## Glossary

- **Hospitality Property:** Hotels and similar real estate blended with an operating business.
- **Operating Business:** The active hotel business of rooms and services; it is non-qualifying for exchange.
- **FF&E:** Furniture, fixtures, and equipment—personal property not eligible for exchange.
- **Personal Property:** Non-real-estate assets, including FF&E, excluded from 1031 treatment since the TCJA.
- **Real Property Allocation:** The exchangeable land-and-building portion of the hotel’s value.
- **Business Value (Goodwill):** The hotel’s going-concern value, which is not exchangeable.
- **Value Allocation:** Dividing the hotel price among real property, FF&E, and business value.
- **Held-for-Investment:** The qualification requirement that can raise questions for owner-operated hotels.
- **Trading Out of Hospitality:** Exchanging hotel real property into more stable, passive real estate.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation; FF&E recapture generally is not deferred.
- **39-Year Depreciation:** The recovery period for hotel real property.
- **Cyclical Sector:** Hospitality’s volatility, which can motivate a de-risking exchange.
- **Going Concern:** The operating-business value of a hotel, separate from real property.
- **Hospitality DST:** A DST holding hotel assets; many owners instead diversify to other sectors.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive destination for owners trading out of hotels.
- **Replacement Property:** The property acquired with the hotel’s qualifying real-property proceeds.
