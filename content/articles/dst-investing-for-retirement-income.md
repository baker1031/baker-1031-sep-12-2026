# DST Investing for Retirement Income

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep coming back to the same retirement distinction: retiring from active property management is not the same as making capital liquid. A DST may remove landlord duties and provide income, but it locks the capital into a multi-year real-estate hold.

For an owner approaching retirement, rental property can shift from wealth builder to operating burden. A 1031 exchange into Delaware Statutory Trusts can defer capital gain, move a concentrated hands-on property into professionally managed fractional interests, and create passive real-estate income. It can also support estate planning. DSTs remain illiquid, and distributions are projections that can be reduced or suspended. A retirement strategy needs diversification, realistic income expectations, and liquid reserves outside the DST allocation. This is educational information, not investment, tax, or legal advice; past performance does not guarantee future results.

If these structures are new, [the full guide to DST investing](dst-guide.html) explains the underlying mechanics before retirement planning begins.

## From active landlord to passive income

The core transition is [the transition from active landlord to passive income](dst-1031-exchange-case-study-from-rental-to-passive-income.html). Direct rental ownership requires tenant screening, repairs and maintenance, finance administration, vacancies, and problem solving. Those jobs can feel manageable at fifty and onerous at seventy. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) can sell the rental, defer the capital-gains tax that a taxable sale would create, and place proceeds into professionally managed DST real estate.

Instead of [managing a fourplex or a commercial building](1031-exchange-for-multifamily-apartment-owners.html), the retiree owns fractional beneficial interests and receives a share of rental income through distributions. The sponsor manages the assets. The deferred gain carries into the DST rather than being recognized on sale. For an owner who wants real estate exposure and income but no longer wants day-to-day property work or a large current tax bill, that trade can be the central appeal.

## Building a retirement DST portfolio

A retirement DST portfolio spreads exchange proceeds across several DSTs instead of placing everything in one trust. A single DST often owns one or a few properties, so it concentrates asset, sponsor, sector, and location exposure. Multiple DSTs can spread capital across multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, different sponsors, and different markets.

Relatively low DST minimums—often around $25,000 to $100,000—can make that possible even in a modest exchange. An owner selling one property worth several hundred thousand dollars or more may be able to create several positions rather than depend on one building or operator. If a property or sector underperforms, other holdings may soften the income impact. An income-oriented retirement portfolio may emphasize stabilized assets where current cash flow is a priority.

First-order thinking sees a passive distribution and assumes retirement income is handled. Second-order thinking asks whether the assets, sponsors, lease durability, expected exits, and allocation leave a retiree exposed to any one adverse event. Diversification does not promise an outcome, but it can reduce concentration risk.

> Low DST minimums can turn one sold property into exposure across sectors, sponsors, and markets rather than a single bet on one building.

## Income stability considerations

DST distributions come from property net rental income. Income-focused DSTs may aim for regular payments, but the payments are not bond-like guarantees. Vacancies, tenant defaults, rising expenses, and market conditions can reduce or suspend them.

More stable retirement income begins with diversification by DST, sponsor, and sector. It favors distributions supported by durable leases, creditworthy tenants, and reasonable leverage rather than the highest headline projected yield. It also treats every distribution as an estimate. Some retirees pair DST income with more defined sources such as Social Security, pensions, and bonds, so that variable property income supplements essential spending rather than bears all of it.

## Estate planning synergies

A 1031 exchange defers capital gain during life. If a DST interest is held until death, heirs generally receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) to fair market value under Section 1014. Under current law, that can eliminate the deferred capital-gains tax. This is often described as “swap till you drop.” It requires confirmation with an estate attorney and CPA because individual facts and current rules matter.

DST interests can also be divided more readily than a physical rental property. A building may force heirs to co-own, co-manage, or sell. Fractional interests are divisible, and a [diversified DST portfolio can be allocated among heirs](inherited-property-and-dst-1031-exchanges.html) without forcing a sale or shared management of one indivisible building. This estate-planning discussion is educational, not advice.

## Key Takeaways

- DSTs can let a retiring landlord exchange active rental ownership for passive, professionally managed real-estate income while deferring gain through a 1031 exchange.
- Spread exchange proceeds across DSTs, sectors, sponsors, and markets; minimums often around $25,000 to $100,000 can help make diversification practical.
- Income stability requires sustainable distributions, diversification, and an understanding that payments are projections rather than guarantees.
- A potential Section 1014 step-up and divisible fractional interests can support legacy planning, subject to guidance from an estate attorney and CPA.

## Managing sequence and liquidity risk

Liquidity risk is central in retirement. DSTs are designed for a full cycle, typically five to seven years or more, and have little or no secondary market. A retiree cannot generally draw capital on demand for a medical expense, home repair, or emergency. Funds that may be needed should not be invested in DSTs; accessible liquid reserves should remain outside the DST allocation.

Sequence risk is the risk that poor results early in retirement impair finances when there is less time to recover. Reduced distributions or an unfavorable early sale can be especially damaging if a retiree depends on DST income and cannot access principal. Manage this by limiting the illiquid allocation, keeping outside liquid reserves, and laddering DSTs with staggered projected exit dates. Laddering has capital expected to return at different intervals rather than at once; expected exit dates can be longer or shorter than projected.

> DSTs may anchor a retirement income plan, but emergency cash and near-term spending money need to be accessible somewhere else.

## Putting a retirement DST strategy together

The plan begins with exchange of active rental property into DSTs to defer gain and give up management. It then combines a diversified portfolio, income-oriented and well-supported distributions, estate-planning coordination, and liquidity management through reserves and staggered exits.

The DST sleeve should be one component of a broader retirement plan alongside liquid savings, more defined income sources, and other assets. The correct size depends on income needs, horizon, liquidity needs, risk tolerance, and the ability to absorb lower distributions or a delayed exit. DST interests are securities offered to [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html)s after suitability review. Financial advisor, CPA, and estate-attorney coordination is part of responsible planning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use DSTs for retirement income?

Yes. Owners often use DSTs when leaving active rental ownership. A 1031 exchange can defer gain and turn a concentrated, managed property into professionally managed fractional interests that distribute a share of rental income. DSTs are illiquid and distributions can be reduced or suspended, so they should be sized thoughtfully, diversified by sector and sponsor, and paired with liquid reserves and other income sources such as Social Security, pensions, and bonds. They are one component, not a guaranteed or sole income source.

### How do DSTs help a landlord retire?

They allow an owner to sell active rental real estate, use a 1031 exchange to defer capital gain, and receive passive distributions from professionally managed properties. The sponsor handles tenants, maintenance, vacancies, finances, and management. The investor retains real-estate exposure and income without managing property directly. The exchange must be planned carefully with advisors to satisfy the 1031 requirements.

### What is a diversified DST portfolio?

It is an allocation of exchange proceeds across multiple DSTs instead of one. Because a single DST may concentrate property, sponsor, sector, and location risk, a portfolio can use multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, different sponsors, and different geographies to reduce concentration and help smooth income. Minimums often around $25,000 to $100,000 can make several positions possible in a modest exchange.

### Are DST distributions guaranteed for retirement?

No. They come from property rental income and are projections, not promises. Vacancies, tenant defaults, rising costs, and market conditions may reduce or suspend distributions. Retirees can improve stability by favoring durable leases, creditworthy tenants, reasonable leverage, and diversification; by using more defined income for essential expenses; and by treating DST income as a supplement rather than a guaranteed payment. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How do DSTs fit into estate planning for retirees?

A 1031 exchange defers gain, and a DST interest held until death may receive a Section 1014 step-up in basis to fair market value that can eliminate the deferred gain. Fractional interests can be divided among heirs without making them co-own one building or forcing a sale. This can make a diversified portfolio flexible for a legacy plan. It is educational content, not estate advice; an estate attorney and CPA should confirm the structure and current law.

### What is liquidity risk with DSTs in retirement?

It is the danger of needing capital that cannot be accessed. DSTs are generally illiquid until full cycle, commonly five to seven years or more, and do not have a robust secondary market. A retiree cannot reliably liquidate an interest for an emergency, medical expense, or home repair. Keep near-term and emergency reserves outside DSTs, limit the illiquid allocation, and consider staggered expected exits.

### What is sequence risk and how do DSTs affect it?

Sequence risk is the danger that poor early-retirement outcomes reduce finances in a way that is difficult to recover from. Reduced DST distributions or an unfavorable early sale can hurt when a retiree depends on the income and cannot readily access principal. The response is diversification of income sources, liquid reserves outside DSTs, an appropriate DST allocation, laddered expected exits, and more defined sources for essential early-retirement spending.

### How much of my retirement should be in DSTs?

There is no universal amount. It depends on total assets, income needs, time horizon, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. Because distributions are not guaranteed and the interests are illiquid, DSTs generally belong in a measured allocation within a diversified plan, not a dominant position. Never place near-term or emergency funds in DSTs; use liquid savings, Social Security, pensions, bonds, or other defined sources for essential spending. A suitability review applies.

### Can I divide DST interests among my heirs?

Yes. Fractional beneficial interests can be allocated among heirs more readily than a single rental building. A portfolio of several DST interests can be divided in the proportions specified by an estate plan without a forced sale or shared management. Combined with a potential step-up in basis at death, that divisibility can be useful for passing real-estate wealth. Coordinate the division with an estate attorney and CPA.

### What is laddering DSTs and why do it?

Laddering means owning DSTs with staggered expected exits so capital is projected to return at different times. It can provide periodic opportunities to reinvest through another 1031 exchange, take proceeds for spending, or rebalance, and it reduces the chance that all capital is tied up until one date. It also spreads reinvestment and market-timing exposure. Actual hold periods and exit dates remain projections, not guarantees.

### Are DSTs a safe retirement investment?

No investment with illiquidity, variable income, vacancies, tenant defaults, market exposure, leverage, and sponsor risk is “safe” in the sense of guaranteed. DSTs may be a prudent retirement component when diversified, sized appropriately, paired with liquid reserves and defined income, and selected through quality sponsor review. They provide possible benefits—passive income, tax deferral, diversification, and estate flexibility—but carry real risk and require suitability review.

### Do I need to be accredited to invest in DSTs for retirement?

Yes, generally. DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors through a broker-dealer after suitability review. Common accreditation measures include income over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly in recent years, expected to continue, or net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence, among other criteria. Suitability considers finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance, which is especially important in retirement.

### Can DST income keep up with inflation in retirement?

It may help but cannot guarantee an inflation match. Rental income can rise through contractual rent escalations or market resets, and property values may appreciate as prices rise. Lease terms vary, distributions can be flat or reduced, and higher costs or interest rates can offset rent growth. Diversification across property types and sectors, including shorter leases or built-in escalations, can help. Do not rely on DST income alone for inflation protection.

### Should I put all my exchange proceeds into one DST for retirement?

Generally no. One DST concentrates a retiree in one property, sponsor, sector, and location. A portfolio across multifamily, industrial, net-lease, healthcare, sponsors, and markets can reduce the effect of one underperforming asset or sponsor. Minimums often around $25,000 to $100,000 make this practical, and laddered expected exits can further spread capital-return timing. Coordinate diversification with advisors and 1031 deadlines.

## Glossary

**Passive Income:** Income from real estate you don't actively manage.

**Active Landlord:** An owner who personally manages rental property.

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Diversified DST Portfolio:** Exchange proceeds spread across multiple DSTs.

**DST Minimum:** The smallest investment in a DST, often ~$25,000-$100,000.

**Distribution:** The share of rental income a DST pays investors.

**Income Stability:** How reliably DST distributions support retirement income.

**Sustainable Distribution:** Income well-supported by durable leases and tenants.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The §1014 basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain.

**Section 1014:** The tax-code section providing the step-up at death.

**Fractional Interest:** A divisible share of a DST, easy to split among heirs.

**Liquidity Risk:** The risk of not accessing illiquid DST capital when needed.

**Sequence Risk:** The danger of poor early-retirement outcomes impairing finances.

**Laddering DSTs:** Staggering DST exits so capital returns at different times.

**Liquid Reserves:** Accessible savings kept outside illiquid DSTs.

**Full Cycle:** A DST's hold from acquisition to sale, ~5-7+ years.
