# Mortgage REITs (mREITs) Explained

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing the yield on a mortgage REIT do most of the talking. It is a tempting number to lead with. The balance sheet, the funding line, and the sensitivity to rates are usually where the real conversation begins.

A mortgage REIT, or mREIT, is not a landlord. It usually owns mortgages and mortgage-backed securities (MBS), finances them with short-term borrowing, and earns the difference between what those assets yield and what funding costs. That difference is the net interest margin. Significant leverage turns it into a potentially meaningful return on equity—and into meaningful risk.

First-order thinking asks which mREIT has the highest dividend yield. Second-order thinking asks what is supporting the dividend: the asset mix, the amount and terms of leverage, the interest-rate spread, the yield curve, the hedge program, and the risk that mortgage holders repay early. Those questions matter because dividends can be cut and book value can move sharply.

This is a higher-risk, more volatile category than the equity REITs most investors picture. It is educational information, not investment advice. Demand, returns, and outlook are discussed in general, non-promissory terms; past performance does not guarantee future results, and current conditions should be verified. For broader context on the distinction, see [our in-depth REIT guide](reits-guide.html).

## How mREITs make money

An equity REIT owns physical real estate and receives rent from tenants. An mREIT owns real-estate debt: mortgages and MBS. It receives interest income. The useful analogy is a specialized lender or leveraged bond investor, not a landlord. It is one of the [three main types of REIT: equity, mortgage, and hybrid](types-of-reits-equity-mortgage-and-hybrid.html).

The basic trade is straightforward. The mREIT combines its own capital with money borrowed at short-term rates, buys mortgage assets with a higher yield, and captures the gap between the two. That gap is its net interest margin (NIM), before expenses. In this business, the spread—not rent—is the income source.

That distinction is easy to miss and hard to overstate. mREITs are spread-based lenders. They finance mortgages and MBS with short-term borrowing and use leverage to enlarge the spread earned on equity. The same structure that can create a high yield is also the source of its fragility.

## Net interest margin and leverage

NIM is the heart of the model: asset yield less funding cost. A wider and more stable margin is more profitable, all else equal. It is not fixed. Asset yields and short-term funding costs move independently, sometimes quickly, so the margin can widen or compress.

Leverage makes a thin spread matter. High-quality mortgage assets often do not offer a large raw margin, so an mREIT may hold far more assets than its equity alone could purchase. When the spread is positive and stable, that can magnify the return on equity.

It works in the other direction with equal force. If the margin narrows or assets fall in value, leverage magnifies losses. The mREIT also must keep rolling over short-term borrowings. If funding costs jump or lenders require more collateral, it may have to sell assets at unfavorable prices. That is funding risk.

## Key takeaways

- mREITs earn the interest-rate spread on mortgages and MBS, not rent. They usually finance those assets with short-term borrowing and significant leverage.
- Net interest margin is asset yield minus funding cost. Leverage magnifies returns and losses and adds funding risk.
- mREITs are higher risk and more volatile than equity REITs. Dividends can be cut, and book value can swing sharply.
- Interest-rate and yield-curve changes and prepayments are central risks. Agency mREITs mainly face rate and prepayment risk; non-agency mREITs add credit risk.

## Why mREITs are volatile

The volatility follows the business model. A modest move in rates, spreads, or asset prices is magnified at the equity level when borrowing is substantial. Book value—the net value of assets less borrowings—can therefore change quickly. Share price and net asset value can be far more variable than they are for a rent-earning equity REIT.

The dividend deserves the same skepticism. It depends on a leveraged NIM. Higher funding costs, an adverse curve move, or lower asset values can reduce income and make a distribution unsustainable. mREIT dividends have been cut when conditions deteriorated. A high headline yield can be compensation for that instability rather than proof of a durable payout.

Funding conditions can compound the problem. A disruption in short-term lending can lead to forced sales and deeper losses. An mREIT can offer attractive income, but it is not a substitute for the steadier income of many equity REITs. High yield is not a free lunch: leverage expands the spread in favorable conditions and expands the damage when conditions turn.

## Rate and prepayment risk

Interest-rate risk is central because the business relies on the spread between asset yields and funding costs. When short-term rates rise, funding becomes more expensive. If asset yields do not keep up, NIM contracts. The yield curve matters as well. mREITs commonly borrow short and invest long, so a flat or inverted curve—short rates approaching or exceeding long rates—can squeeze or even eliminate the spread.

Falling rates can create a different problem. Homeowners may refinance and repay mortgages early. For an mREIT holding those loans or MBS, principal returns when the available reinvestment yield is lower. That is prepayment risk. In other words, rising rates can hurt funding costs, while falling rates can bring early repayments and lower-yield reinvestment.

Agency mREITs hold government-backed MBS and mainly bear rate and prepayment risk. Non-agency mREITs hold non-guaranteed mortgage assets and also bear credit risk: borrowers may default. Hedging can reduce some rate and prepayment exposure, but it costs money, adds complexity, and is never perfect.

## Agency versus non-agency mREITs

The agency/non-agency split tells investors a great deal about the risk being accepted. Agency mREITs buy MBS guaranteed by government-related agencies. The guarantee effectively backstops credit losses caused by borrower defaults. Their principal exposures are interest rates, the yield curve, and refinancing activity. Leverage can still make their book values volatile.

Non-agency mREITs hold mortgage assets without that guarantee, including private-label MBS and non-agency loans. They retain rate and prepayment risk and add direct credit risk. If borrowers default—especially in a weakening housing market or economy—the mREIT can suffer losses that an agency vehicle would largely avoid. Those assets often offer higher yields precisely because they carry more risk.

Many mREITs own a blend of agency and non-agency assets. The blend matters. It determines how much of the portfolio depends mainly on rate and prepayment conditions and how much also depends on credit performance.

## Evaluating mortgage REITs

An mREIT needs a different lens from an equity REIT. There are no buildings or rents to underwrite directly. Start with the balance sheet and the spread.

Look first at leverage: how much is borrowed relative to equity? Higher leverage can raise potential return, but it also raises fragility. Then study book-value trends. Because book value is assets minus borrowings, a persistent decline can show that the dividend is masking erosion of shareholder capital through rate and spread cycles.

Next, ask how wide and stable the NIM is, and how exposed it is to rates and the yield curve. Examine the hedge program: what exposures are hedged, at what cost, and how effectively? Management quality and track record matter because navigating leverage, funding, rates, and prepayments is difficult. Determine whether the mREIT is agency, non-agency, or mixed.

Finally, read the dividend skeptically. A high yield is not enough. Verify current leverage and book-value trends, assess the spread, hedging, management, and asset mix, and remember that past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a mortgage REIT (mREIT)?

An mREIT finances real estate rather than owning buildings. It holds mortgages and MBS, funds much of that position with short-term borrowing, and earns NIM: the difference between asset yield and funding cost. Significant leverage turns it into a specialized lender or leveraged bond-investor structure, not a landlord. It can produce a high yield, but it is higher risk than an equity REIT because book value and distributions can move sharply with rates and prepayments.

### How do mortgage REITs make money?

They borrow at short-term rates, add their equity, and purchase higher-yielding mortgages and MBS. The difference between what assets earn and what borrowing costs is the NIM. Because that raw margin can be thin, leverage enlarges the asset base and the effect on equity. It also makes earnings sensitive to rates, the yield curve, and funding costs.

### What is net interest margin?

NIM is the spread between the yield on mortgage assets and the funding cost used to buy them, before expenses. It widens or narrows as both sides move. Higher short-term rates, a flat or inverted curve, and early repayments that require reinvestment at lower yields can all compress it. Leverage makes even small NIM changes matter more for returns and dividends.

### Why do mortgage REITs use leverage?

Leverage lets an mREIT earn a small spread on a much larger portfolio than its equity would otherwise support. That can amplify return when the spread is positive and stable. It also amplifies loss, increases rollover funding risk, and can create collateral calls and forced sales when lenders require more support. More leverage means more potential return and more fragility.

### Why are mortgage REITs more volatile than equity REITs?

mREITs use a leveraged spread model. Rate, spread, and asset-value changes are therefore magnified in book value. Their dividends depend on NIM and can be cut when conditions worsen. Funding disruptions can force asset sales. Equity REITs own buildings and collect rent; their values and income streams are generally less exposed to this particular combination of leverage and short-term funding.

### What is prepayment risk in mortgage REITs?

When rates fall, homeowners may refinance or pay off their mortgages early. Principal returns to the mREIT and must be reinvested at lower prevailing yields, which can compress the spread. The risk is especially associated with agency mortgage assets. Hedging may help manage it, but hedges add cost and complexity and cannot remove the exposure entirely.

### What is the difference between agency and non-agency mREITs?

Agency mREITs own guaranteed MBS. The guarantee makes credit loss from borrower default largely a rate-and-prepayment problem instead. Non-agency mREITs own unguaranteed mortgages or MBS, so they add borrower-default credit risk to rate and prepayment risk. Non-agency assets may offer higher yields as compensation, but the mix and the associated credit exposure must be understood.

### Are mortgage REITs risky?

Yes. They are a higher-risk specialized holding, not low-risk or bond-like income. Leverage, short-term funding, rate and curve changes, prepayments, and—where applicable—credit losses can cause sharp book-value, share-price, and dividend changes. Appropriate position sizing matters, and current conditions should be verified.

### How do interest rates affect mortgage REITs?

Rising short-term rates can increase funding costs faster than asset yields, reducing NIM. A flat or inverted curve can further squeeze the borrow-short/invest-long model. Falling rates can trigger refinancing and force lower-yield reinvestment. Since mREITs are leveraged, both kinds of rate move can have an outsized effect on earnings, book value, and dividends.

### Can mortgage REIT dividends be cut?

Yes. A dividend supported by a leveraged NIM can become unsustainable when funding costs rise, the curve flattens, prepayments increase, or asset values fall. Funding-market disruption can add pressure. An unusually high yield can even reflect a market expectation of a cut. Look at book-value trends and spread stability rather than treating the current yield as a bond coupon.

### What is book value and why does it matter for mREITs?

Book value is the net value of assets minus borrowings—shareholders' equity, often expressed per share. It matters because an mREIT is valued through that leveraged balance sheet rather than through owned buildings. Stable or growing book value can suggest capital is being preserved through rate and spread cycles. A persistent decline is a warning that a generous dividend may be accompanied by capital erosion.

### How do I evaluate a mortgage REIT?

Start with leverage and book-value trends. Then assess the NIM and its sensitivity to rates and the curve, the hedge program and its cost, management quality, and the agency/non-agency mix. A high dividend can be cut, so it should be evaluated as part of a complete balance-sheet analysis rather than in isolation.

### How are mortgage REITs different from equity REITs?

Equity REITs own assets such as apartments, warehouses, offices, and retail property; they earn rent, may benefit from property appreciation, and are commonly assessed with FFO, AFFO, and NAV. mREITs own mortgages and MBS, earn a leveraged interest-rate spread, and are assessed through leverage, book value, and NIM. The latter is generally much more rate sensitive, and dividends and book value can be less stable.

### What is yield-curve risk for mortgage REITs?

mREITs usually borrow at short-term rates and invest in longer-term mortgage assets. A positive, upward-sloping curve can support the gap between asset yield and funding cost. When the curve flattens, that gap narrows. When short-term rates exceed long-term rates, the spread can shrink dramatically or become negative. Leverage magnifies the result in earnings and book value.

## Glossary

**Mortgage REIT (mREIT).** A REIT that finances real estate and earns an interest-rate spread rather than rent.

**Mortgage-Backed Security (MBS).** A security backed by a pool of mortgages that an mREIT may hold.

**Net Interest Margin (NIM).** The difference between asset yields and funding costs.

**Interest-Rate Spread.** The gap an mREIT earns between mortgage-asset yield and borrowing cost.

**Leverage.** Borrowing used to enlarge the spread earned on equity, amplifying return and risk.

**Book Value.** The net value of an mREIT's assets less its borrowings.

**Interest-Rate Risk.** The risk that rate moves compress the spread or reduce asset values.

**Yield Curve.** The relationship between short- and long-term interest rates.

**Prepayment Risk.** The risk that falling rates prompt early mortgage repayment.

**Agency mREIT.** An mREIT holding government-backed MBS and facing mainly rate and prepayment risk.

**Non-Agency mREIT.** An mREIT holding non-guaranteed assets and adding credit risk.

**Credit Risk.** The risk that borrowers default on non-guaranteed mortgage assets.

**Funding Risk.** The risk in continually rolling over short-term borrowing that finances assets.

**Hedging.** Using instruments to mitigate rate and prepayment exposure.

**90% Distribution Rule.** The REIT requirement to distribute at least 90% of taxable income.

**Suitability Review.** The assessment of whether an offering fits an investor before it is recommended.
