# Building a Diversified DST Portfolio by Asset Class

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read

I have been thinking about how a DST portfolio is governed less by a performance chart than by projected full-cycle dates: the moments when capital may actually be available to redeploy. With DSTs, that calendar matters as much as the starting allocation.

A diversified DST portfolio is built deliberately, not assembled by accident. The work is to set objectives, organize income, growth, and defensive sleeves across asset classes, consider illustrative allocations, decide whether oil and gas royalty DSTs belong as a diversifier, and redeploy when a DST reaches full-cycle. DST interests are securities for accredited investors after suitability review. Illustrations are not recommendations; distributions and returns are never guaranteed; Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. Current rules belong with the investor’s advisors.

For an investor using DSTs through several 1031 exchanges over many years, the goal can move beyond completing one exchange. It becomes portfolio construction. Net-lease and storage can serve a defensive-income role; multifamily can provide a balance of income and growth; industrial and other sectors can offer growth exposure; and mineral or royalty interests may add an income source that is not driven by leasing. Each DST is finite and sells after a multi-year hold. When one goes full-cycle, proceeds can generally be rolled through the next 1031 exchange, gradually changing the overall mix.

Portfolio construction builds on the asset-class overview in [our 2026 DST guide](dst-guide.html).

## Setting your objectives

Start with the purpose of the capital. Is the principal aim current income to support living expenses or other income? Long-term growth and equity value? Capital preservation and defense through steadier, lower-volatility assets? Or a balance of all three? The answer determines which asset classes and structures deserve the largest role.

The blueprint also needs a time horizon, need for current cash flow, risk tolerance, and tax and estate-planning objectives. An investor needing stable income may weight income-producing, defensive sectors more heavily. An investor able to accept more variation for appreciation potential may favor growth sectors. Many will want both. The point is to make those choices before the next exchange, rather than letting whichever offerings happen to be available make the portfolio by default.

First-order thinking is to ask which offering has the highest indicated distribution today. Second-order thinking is to ask whether the result fits the investor’s income need, horizon, risk tolerance, estate plan, and the existing exposures already in the portfolio. Goals come before allocation.

## Income, growth, and defensive sleeves

A sleeve is a grouping of holdings by the job it performs. The income sleeve emphasizes steady current distributions: net-lease properties with creditworthy long-term tenants, certain stabilized multifamily assets, and similar holdings intended to produce predictable cash flow. The defensive sleeve emphasizes resilience through cycles. Net-lease can serve here as well, alongside self-storage, which has historically held up in downturns, and healthcare or medical office tied to non-cyclical demand.

The growth sleeve emphasizes appreciation potential and rising income over time. Multifamily in expanding markets can reset rents upward. Industrial and logistics can benefit from e-commerce and supply-chain demand. Those growth-oriented DSTs may offer less current income in exchange for more potential value creation. Sleeves are conceptual roles, not rigid boxes. Net-lease, for example, can be both an income and defensive holding.

An income-focused investor may place heavier weight on income and defense. A growth-focused investor may give growth more weight. A balanced investor may spread capital across all three. The sleeve framework turns a broad objective into a concrete way to allocate by asset class.

> Think in sleeves, not only sectors: income for cash flow, defense for resilience, and growth for appreciation—sized to the objectives actually in place.

## Sample asset-class allocations

The following are generic illustrations, not recommendations. An income-focused mix might lean toward net-lease retail and stabilized multifamily for distributions, then add self-storage and healthcare for defensive income, with a small allocation to growth. The emphasis is predictable income and resilience rather than appreciation.

A balanced mix may place a meaningful allocation in multifamily for both income and growth, use net-lease and storage for defensive income, and add industrial for growth. That can spread the portfolio across four or five asset classes so one sector does not dominate. A growth-oriented mix may weight growth-market multifamily and industrial more heavily, accepting lower current income for appreciation potential while retaining defensive assets as ballast.

Geography and sponsor exposure sit on top of asset-class exposure. The actual allocation depends on the investor’s objectives, suitability, available offerings, identification rules, and the need to reinvest fully in each exchange. A picture of a portfolio is not advice to use the displayed sectors or proportions. It is a way to see how the same available toolkit can serve different purposes.

## Layering in oil and gas royalty DSTs

Oil and gas royalty DSTs can add a different category to a portfolio of traditional property. Mineral and royalty interests are treated as real property for 1031 purposes, so a DST holding those interests can qualify as like-kind replacement property in the same general way as a DST holding apartments or warehouses. That permits an investor to add energy royalties to a property-based mix.

The income driver is different. A property DST earns tenant rent and is affected by leasing cycles, occupancy, and property values. A royalty DST earns royalties on energy production from underlying mineral interests. Commodity prices and production volumes drive that income. Because those drivers differ from rent, royalty income may behave differently from a rent-based DST and may diversify the portfolio.

Difference is not the same as safety. Commodity-price changes, production decline as wells deplete, and regulatory or environmental factors can make royalty income variable. It is not guaranteed. For most investors, an oil and gas royalty DST is a diversifying sleeve, not an automatic core holding. The appropriate size depends on objectives, risk tolerance, and suitability.

## Key Takeaways

- Build the portfolio from objectives—income, growth, defense, or balance—not from accident or availability.
- Use income, growth, and defensive sleeves across asset classes, weighting each to the investor’s stated goals.
- Oil and gas royalty DSTs can be 1031-eligible because mineral interests are real property; they add energy-royalty income and their own commodity and production risks.
- Rebalance when DSTs reach full-cycle, using a new 1031 exchange to redeploy proceeds toward the desired allocation.

## Rebalancing at full-cycle

A stock portfolio can be adjusted on any trading day. A DST portfolio cannot. A DST normally rebalances at full-cycle, when the trust sells its underlying property or interests and returns capital after a multi-year hold. The holdings are finite and illiquid; an investor generally cannot trim or add mid-stream. That makes each full-cycle event the natural opportunity to reassess and redeploy.

Full-cycle proceeds can generally be reinvested through a new [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), continuing deferral while the investor moves capital toward underweight sleeves, asset classes, geographies, or sponsors. An investor may replace a matured income DST, add to an underweight growth sleeve, or introduce a royalty position. Over several exchanges, this is how a DST portfolio is shaped. A new DST begins its own multi-year hold, so each cycle also starts a new timing period.

The sponsor controls when full-cycle occurs. It is generally the end of the trust’s investment life, often after five to seven years, though timing varies. Investors receive their pro-rata share of net sale proceeds, including original equity and any appreciation, as well as equity built through debt paydown in an amortizing structure. They can recognize the gain or attempt another exchange. A qualified intermediary and tax advisor should confirm the details.

> A DST portfolio does not rebalance on a Tuesday afternoon. It rebalances when a trust sells, returns capital, and creates a new 1031 decision.

## Monitoring and estate-planning fit

Illiquidity does not mean ignoring the holdings. Monitoring includes comparing each DST with its projections, reading sponsor updates, noting distribution changes, tracking approaching full-cycle events, and reconsidering whether the target allocation still fits as the investor’s life and market conditions change. Monitoring cannot create immediate liquidity, but it can make the next full-cycle redeployment more deliberate.

Estate planning can also affect portfolio design. Deferred gain carries forward through successive 1031 exchanges. If DST interests are held until death, heirs may receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) to fair market value, which may eliminate accumulated deferred tax under current law. A diversified, income-producing portfolio can then be a passive, professionally managed legacy asset, rather than a single building the heirs must manage or sell.

That outcome is not automatic. Structure, titling, and timing should be coordinated with the estate plan, attorney, and CPA. The tax and legal details are technical, and projected outcomes are not guarantees. Still, continued deferral plus a potential step-up is why some investors consider a long-running DST portfolio as part of a legacy plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean to build a diversified DST portfolio?

It means treating DST holdings as a coordinated whole rather than as disconnected exchanges. The investor sets objectives, assigns holdings to income, growth, and defensive roles, diversifies across multifamily, net-lease retail, industrial, storage, healthcare, and potentially royalty interests, then redeploys capital as trusts reach full-cycle. It can also diversify geography and sponsor exposure. This portfolio-management approach suits investors using DSTs repeatedly over many years.

### How do I set objectives for a DST portfolio?

Identify the primary aim—current income, long-term growth, capital preservation and defense, or a balance—then clarify horizon, cash-flow need, risk tolerance, and tax and estate aims. An investor prioritizing steady income may lean toward defensive income sectors; one accepting more variability for appreciation may lean toward growth-market multifamily and industrial. The objectives establish the target allocation before particular offerings enter the discussion.

### What are income, growth, and defensive sleeves?

They classify holdings by role. Income holdings aim for current distributions, including net-lease and certain stabilized multifamily assets. Defensive holdings emphasize resilience, including net-lease, storage, and healthcare or medical office. Growth holdings emphasize appreciation and rising income, including growth-market multifamily and industrial or logistics. Some assets can belong to more than one sleeve; the weights should match the investor’s goals.

### What asset classes belong in a diversified DST portfolio?

Possible categories include multifamily, net-lease retail, industrial and logistics, self-storage, medical office and healthcare, senior housing, and oil and gas mineral or royalty interests. Multifamily can balance income and growth; net-lease may provide long-lease defensive income; industrial has growth exposure; storage has historically held up in downturns; healthcare has non-cyclical, demographic demand; senior housing has demographic tailwinds. Royalties add an energy-driven income source. The right mix depends on the investor’s objectives and risk tolerance, and each offering needs individual review.

### Can I include oil and gas royalty DSTs in a 1031 portfolio?

Yes, generally. Mineral and royalty interests are treated as real property for 1031 purposes, so a DST holding them can be like-kind replacement property. Its income comes from energy production, not tenant rent, and is driven by commodity prices and production volumes. That can add a non-correlated source of income, but commodity-price volatility, production decline, and regulation make the income variable and not guaranteed. Confirm eligibility and risk with advisors before investing.

### How are oil and gas royalty DSTs different from property DSTs?

A property DST owns real estate and earns tenant rent; leasing cycles, occupancy, and property values influence results. A royalty DST holds mineral or royalty interests and earns production royalties, so oil and gas prices and volumes are central. Both may be 1031-eligible real property, but the royalty DST is an energy-income investment with distinct commodity, depletion, regulatory, and environmental risks. It diversifies a property position; it does not substitute for it.

### What does full-cycle mean for a DST?

Full-cycle is the point when a DST sells the underlying asset and returns capital, completing that trust’s life. It is typically after a multi-year holding period, often five to seven years, but timing varies and is at the sponsor’s discretion. Investors cannot generally exit midway. The returned pro-rata net sale proceeds create the choice to recognize gain or complete another 1031 exchange into a new DST.

### How do I rebalance a DST portfolio?

Redeploy full-cycle proceeds, because the investor generally cannot trade a DST during its hold. That redeployment can add an underweight growth sleeve, replace a matured income asset, introduce a royalty diversifier, or change geography or sponsor exposure. A new 1031 exchange can generally continue deferral. Plan the exchange with an advisor and qualified intermediary rather than treating it like a daily-traded portfolio adjustment.

### Are sample DST allocations recommendations?

No. A sample allocation is educational and does not recommend particular investments or proportions. The right allocation depends on personal financial circumstances, objectives, horizon, cash-flow needs, risk tolerance, available offerings, identification rules, full-reinvestment requirements, and suitability review. Actual recommendations for securities follow broker-dealer suitability review. Projected distributions and returns are not guarantees.

### How many asset classes should a DST portfolio include?

There is no single correct number. Many portfolios use roughly four to six asset classes—such as multifamily, net-lease retail, industrial, storage, healthcare, and perhaps royalty interests—which can limit single-sector concentration while remaining manageable. Too few can leave a portfolio exposed to one or two sectors; too many can spread capital thin, complicate diligence, and add administration with little added benefit. Sponsor and geography are additional diversification dimensions.

### Does a diversified DST portfolio reduce risk?

It reduces concentration risk but does not eliminate risk or guarantee against loss. Spreading holdings across sectors, geographies, sponsors, and sleeves can lessen the effect of an underperforming property, market, or sponsor. It cannot remove illiquidity, sponsor dependence, fees, broad market or interest-rate sensitivity, or the uncertainty of projected distributions and returns. Royalty holdings add commodity-price and production risk. A diversified portfolio can still decline in value.

### Can I keep deferring tax as I build a DST portfolio?

Generally, an investor can use a new 1031 exchange each time capital is redeployed. The first exchange defers gain from the relinquished property; a full-cycle event can start another exchange into the next DST. That rolling process can continue for many years. To continue deferral, each exchange must meet 1031 requirements, including reinvesting all equity, replacing all debt, and meeting identification and closing deadlines. Some investors hold until death, when a potential step-up may eliminate deferred tax; this is sometimes called a “swap till you drop” approach. Confirm current rules with a QI and CPA.

### Is an oil and gas royalty DST suitable as a core holding?

For most investors, it is better viewed as a diversifying sleeve than a core holding. Its usefulness is its different income driver, but that comes with commodity-price variation, natural production decline, and regulatory and environmental exposure. The appropriate allocation may be modest, larger, or zero depending on individual objectives and risk tolerance. A suitability review must address the investor’s circumstances. Returns are never guaranteed.

### How does a diversified DST portfolio fit into estate planning?

Successive 1031 exchanges can carry deferred capital-gains tax forward. If the investor holds DST interests until death, heirs may receive a step-up in basis to fair market value that can eliminate deferred tax under current rules. The portfolio may then pass as a diversified, passive real estate legacy rather than one property. Estate-plan structure, titling, timing, and individual tax facts must be coordinated with the attorney and CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide legal or tax advice.

## Glossary

**Diversified DST Portfolio:** A deliberate mix of DSTs across asset classes, built over time.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate or royalty interests in fractional shares.

**Income Sleeve:** Holdings emphasizing steady current distributions.

**Growth Sleeve:** Holdings emphasizing appreciation and rising income over time.

**Defensive Sleeve:** Holdings emphasizing stability and resilience across cycles.

**Asset Class:** A property or interest category, such as multifamily or royalties.

**Net-Lease Retail:** Single-tenant property on a long lease, often defensive income.

**Oil & Gas Royalty DST:** A 1031-eligible DST holding mineral and royalty interests.

**Mineral Interest:** An interest in oil and gas, treated as 1031-eligible real property.

**Full-Cycle:** When a DST sells its asset and returns capital to investors.

**Rebalancing:** Adjusting allocation toward targets, done at full-cycle for DSTs.

**Sponsor:** The company that acquires, manages, and sells a DST's asset.

**Step-Up in Basis:** Heirs' basis reset that can eliminate deferred gain at death.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds proceeds and facilitates each 1031 exchange.

**Suitability Review:** Confirming each holding fits the investor before investing.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.
