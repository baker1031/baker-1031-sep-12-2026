# 721 Exchange Downsides: Risks Every Investor Should Weigh

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › 721 Exchange Downsides

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep coming back to the one-way decisions in real estate. A 721 can turn a tiring property into a diversified REIT position while deferring tax. That can be exactly right. But the trade gives up options that are easy to underweight when the offer is presented as a tidy exit.

First-order thinking sees diversification and eventual liquidity. Second-order thinking asks what is being surrendered: future 1031 flexibility, direct control, on-demand liquidity, and the ability to exit without tax. This is not an argument against a 721; it is a case for entering one with full knowledge of its costs.

## Key Takeaways

- A 721 generally ends future 1031 exchanges on that equity. It is a one-way door.
- OP units are illiquid, subject to holding periods and, in a non-traded REIT, limited redemption programs.
- Converting OP units to REIT shares or redeeming for cash is taxable and can trigger deferred gain.
- You give up property-level control and depend on the REIT’s management, leverage, distributions, and valuation.

## You give up future 1031 exchanges

Once equity becomes [OP units](op-units-explained.html), it generally cannot be exchanged back into real estate under 1031 because a partnership interest is not like-kind real property. Until that point, an investor can keep exchanging into new property indefinitely. A 721 closes that option permanently.

That is a substantial loss of flexibility. A serial 1031 can be redirected as markets and circumstances change. Do not trade it away merely because a 721 is convenient at a DST full-cycle. If directly controlled real estate may be needed again, the 721 is likely the wrong choice.

## OP units aren’t liquid on demand

“Liquidity” needs a qualifier. OP units usually have an initial holding period, often around a year. Afterwards they may be converted to REIT shares or redeemed. In a non-traded REIT, redemption is subject to caps, queues, and sponsor discretion to limit or suspend requests, especially when many investors seek an exit. There is no public market backstop.

This can be more liquid than a single property or DST, but it is not the on-demand liquidity of public stock unless the REIT itself is publicly traded. Know which REIT you are entering before relying on an exit.

## Conversion and redemption are taxable

Accessing money generally recognizes the tax that was deferred. Converting OP units into REIT shares, or redeeming them for cash, can trigger some or all of deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture. Deferral lasts only while the units remain unconverted.

An investor may convert gradually and spread tax across years, or hold units for life so heirs may receive a stepped-up basis and the gain may be eliminated. But OP units are not freely spendable, tax-free cash. Every conversion is both a liquidity and tax decision. See [721 vs. 1031](721-exchange-vs-1031-exchange.html).

## Loss of control

A 721 moves the investor from owner to passenger. The investor no longer owns or controls a particular property; REIT management decides what to buy, sell, finance, and distribute. An owner who values tenant selection, sale timing, or forced appreciation through improvements gives that up.

The investor also accepts the REIT’s leverage policy, sector mix, distribution approach, and valuation practices. If those choices no longer fit, the practical remedy is exit—and exit is taxable.

## REIT, distribution, and valuation risk

REIT distributions are not guaranteed and may be cut. Significant leverage or concentration in a troubled sector can reduce income and value. With a non-traded REIT, unit and share values are set through periodic appraisals rather than a live market, which can lag conditions in either direction; redemptions may use that appraised value.

These risks are not unique to a 721. The distinction is commitment: the investor may be tied, often permanently, to a single REIT. REIT and sponsor quality therefore deserve the same scrutiny a DST sponsor receives at the outset.

## Your alternatives to a 721 at full-cycle

The 721 is normally a choice, not the only road. An investor can cash out and pay tax—simple and fully liquid. They can complete another [1031](721-exchange-vs-1031-exchange.html) into property or a DST—preserving flexibility and deferral but remaining in illiquid real estate and facing the deadlines again. Or they can take the 721—gaining diversification and eventual conditional, taxable liquidity while accepting the one-way commitment. [DST full-cycle decisions](dst-full-cycle.html) explores this fork in more detail.

## When a 721 is still worth it

These drawbacks do not make a 721 bad. They define who it suits: an investor ready to stop exchanging, seeking REIT diversification, valuing unit divisibility and eventual estate liquidity, and intending to hold for life or convert gradually with tax consequences understood. The mistake is walking through the door accidentally or expecting a flexible, freely liquid, tax-free position.

My current risk stance is simple: do not assess a 721 only by what it adds. Put a value on the options it removes before signing. Which of those lost options would matter most in your own plan?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the biggest downside of a 721 exchange?

The loss of future 1031 exchanges. Once equity is in OP units, it generally cannot be exchanged back into real estate.

### Are OP units liquid?

Only conditionally. After a holding period, they may be converted or redeemed; in non-traded REITs, that is a limited redemption program rather than a public market, and conversion is taxable.

### Is converting OP units to shares taxable?

Yes. Conversion to shares or cash redemption generally recognizes deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture. Deferral lasts while the units are held.

### Do I lose control in a 721 exchange?

Yes. You hold an interest in a REIT; its management controls purchases, sales, financing, and distributions across its portfolio.

### Is a 721 exchange ever the right choice?

Yes, for an investor ready to stop exchanging who wants REIT diversification and plans to hold units for life or convert gradually with tax consequences in mind.

## Glossary

### OP Units

Operating-partnership units received in a 721 exchange, convertible to REIT shares in a taxable event.

### One-Way Door

The fact that OP units generally cannot be exchanged under 1031 back into directly owned real estate.

### Redemption Program

A non-traded REIT mechanism for buying back units or shares, usually with caps and discretion to limit redemptions.

### Built-In Gain

Deferred gain on contributed property that may be recognized if the REIT later sells it.
