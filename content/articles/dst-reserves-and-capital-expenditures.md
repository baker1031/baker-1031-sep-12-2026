# DST Reserves & Capital Expenditures

[Back to All Insights](insights.html) · [Home](baker1031.html)

*Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I have been thinking about the expenses that do not appear in the first year’s distribution. Every building eventually needs major work: a roof, an HVAC replacement, tenant improvements needed to attract or retain occupants. An ordinary owner can write a check or arrange financing. A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) cannot do either with the same freedom.

That is why a DST sets aside reserves at the offering. The IRS restrictions that support DST 1031 eligibility generally bar the trust from calling new capital from investors or freely refinancing debt. The no-new-capital restriction makes an upfront cushion essential for future capital expenditures and contingencies. Adequate reserves protect the investment; inadequate reserves can leave the trust with very few choices when a large cost arrives. The cash held back also reduces current distributions.

First-order thinking sees a higher current distribution and assumes it is better. Second-order thinking asks whether the reserve policy can carry roofs, HVAC units, tenant build-outs, and contingencies through the expected hold. This guide explains why DSTs hold reserves, how capital projects are funded, why the restriction matters, what reserves do to distributions, and how to review the policy. ## Key Takeaways

- A DST cannot call new capital from investors, so it sets aside reserves upfront for future capital expenditures and contingencies.
- Reserves fund capital expenditures such as roofs, HVAC, and tenant improvements. The sponsor estimates and pre-funds them because the trust cannot raise money later.
- The no-new-capital restriction makes reserve adequacy essential. If reserves run short, springing-LLC conversion is a drastic last resort that ends the DST’s favorable status.
- Reserves reduce current distributions because withheld cash is not paid out. Review the reserve policy and adequacy: a higher headline yield is not always better.

## Why DSTs Hold Reserves

Once an offering closes, a DST has limited ways to pay unexpected or large expenses. The IRS restrictions that keep it passive and 1031-eligible—the “seven deadly sins”—prohibit new investor capital contributions and renegotiation or refinancing of debt, except in narrow situations such as a tenant bankruptcy. If a major repair or unexpected expense comes due, the trust generally cannot ask investors for more money or simply take out a new loan. Reserves address that constraint.

They are pools of cash set aside upfront to cover anticipated capital expenditures—such as roof or HVAC replacement and tenant improvements—and unforeseen contingencies, including an unanticipated repair or temporary income decline. The sponsor estimates likely capital needs during the expected hold and builds that cushion into the offering. The trust can draw on it as needs arise, maintaining and improving the property within DST rules without violating the no-new-capital restriction.

Put simply, reserves replace an ordinary owner’s ability to raise more money later. They are a structural necessity, not an afterthought.

## Funding Capital Expenditures

Capital expenditures, or **capex**, are the larger, longer-lived investments a property needs over time rather than its routine operating expenses. They can include replacing a roof or HVAC system, repaving a parking lot, upgrading building systems, and funding tenant improvements—the build-out costs needed to attract or retain tenants. Day-to-day maintenance comes from operating income. Capex arrives episodically, can be large, and can occur at unpredictable points in a multi-year hold.

In a DST, reserves are the primary funding source for these costs. The sponsor estimates likely capex from the building’s age and condition, property type, and lease rollover schedule, then pre-funds reserves and draws on them as projects arise. That initial estimate and the discipline to maintain the reserve are central to proper upkeep. If a capital need exceeds reserves and operating cash, the trust has few good options because it cannot raise new money later.

The reserve set aside on day one must anticipate years of roofs, HVAC units, and tenant build-outs. That is why adequacy is consequential.

## The No-New-Capital Restriction

The no-new-capital restriction is a defining limit that keeps a DST passive and 1031-eligible. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a DST generally cannot accept new capital contributions from investors after the offering closes; it cannot return later to raise more equity. It also generally cannot renegotiate or refinance existing debt, except in narrow circumstances such as tenant bankruptcy. In broad terms, the capital at closing is the capital available for the hold.

If reserves run short and the property needs a major repair, a costly tenant build-out, or faces a series of contingencies, the structure has limited choices. In a genuine emergency, the last resort is a **springing LLC**, a provision that converts the trust into a limited liability company so it can take active steps a DST cannot, including raising capital or refinancing.

That conversion typically ends the DST’s favorable status and can have serious consequences for investors. It is a drastic fallback, not routine funding. Reserves exist to avoid needing it.

## Impact on Distributions

Reserves directly affect what investors receive because cash held in reserve is not distributed. A DST can distribute current cash flow and cannot accumulate cash beyond reasonable needs under the rules, yet it must retain capital reserves. That creates a real tension between income paid now and protection held for later. Larger reserves generally mean somewhat less current income; thinner reserves can mean more current income and more exposure when a major expense arrives.

This matters in comparisons. A higher current distribution is not automatically superior when it comes at the expense of adequate reserves. Remember that [one of the more persistent DST misconceptions is that a bigger headline yield signals a better deal](common-dst-myths-and-misconceptions.html). Thin reserves can instead bring trouble later, including reduced or suspended distributions when the trust cannot fund needed capital.

Well-funded reserves protect the investment and the durability of distributions over the hold, even if they reduce headline yield somewhat. They are a form of self-insurance: less current income in exchange for protection against future shortfalls. The appropriate balance depends on the property’s age, condition, and likely capital needs.

## Reviewing Reserve Policies

Because reserves matter so much, the reserve policy belongs in the offering review. Offering documents typically describe the reserves the DST will hold, how they are funded, and the sponsor’s capital plan over the hold. The central question is whether they appear adequate for likely needs.

That depends on the building’s age and condition, property type, lease rollover schedule—which drives tenant-improvement costs—and expected hold length. An older property or one with significant near-term lease turnover generally requires larger reserves.

Ask how the reserves were estimated and whether they reflect a realistic capital plan, including a property condition assessment. Is there a contingency cushion beyond anticipated capex? How does the reserve amount compare with the distribution rate—is the sponsor offering a high yield by underfunding it? What is the plan if the reserves prove insufficient? Those answers sit in the offering documents and sponsor underwriting. They are fact-specific, so read the materials carefully and review them with your financial professional.

When two DSTs have different yields, look behind the gap. A thinner reserve can purchase a higher headline distribution today while increasing the chance of a shortfall years later.

## Reserves vs. Operating Expenses

Reserves and capex differ from ordinary operating expenses, and DSTs fund them differently. Operating expenses are recurring costs of running the property: property management, utilities, insurance, routine maintenance, and property taxes. They are paid from current operating income before distributions and are predictable, recurring costs embedded in ongoing cash flow.

Capital expenditures and contingencies are different: episodic, potentially large costs that operating income may not cover when they hit. Reserves are the separate cushion for these lumpy costs. Distributions come from operating income after operating expenses; reserves handle capital needs and contingencies.

The distinction helps an investor read a DST’s financials. Both the operating budget and reserve cushion must be realistic. Underestimating either can cause problems.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are reserves in a DST?

Reserves in a Delaware Statutory Trust are cash pools set aside upfront at the offering to fund future capital expenditures and unexpected contingencies during the hold. A DST cannot call new investor capital or freely refinance debt under IRS rules supporting 1031 eligibility, so it cannot simply raise money later when major work is needed. The sponsor estimates needs such as roof or HVAC replacement and tenant improvements, funds the cushion into the offering, and the trust draws on it as needs arise. Reserves are an upfront financial cushion replacing an ordinary owner’s ability to raise more money later. Their adequacy is central to whether the DST can weather capital needs and is an important item to evaluate.

### Why do DSTs need reserves?

DSTs have limited ways to pay unexpected or large expenses after the offering closes. The “seven deadly sins” under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 prevent new investor capital and generally prevent debt renegotiation or refinancing, except in narrow circumstances such as tenant bankruptcy. The trust generally cannot ask investors for more money or take a new loan for a major repair, tenant build-out, or other unexpected cost. Reserves fill that gap by covering anticipated capex and contingencies over the hold. Without adequate reserves, a DST facing a major need has few good choices. They are a structural necessity, not an optional extra.

### What are capital expenditures in a DST?

Capital expenditures, often called capex, are larger, longer-lived property investments rather than routine operating expenses. They include roof or HVAC replacement, parking-lot repaving, building-system upgrades, and tenant improvements. Unlike daily maintenance paid from operating income, capex is episodic, sometimes large, and can arise unpredictably during a multi-year hold. A DST primarily funds it from reserves because it cannot raise new capital. The sponsor estimates needs from age, condition, lease rollover schedule, and property type, then funds reserves to cover them. Evaluating likely capex and reserve sufficiency is therefore important.

### What is the no-new-capital restriction?

The no-new-capital restriction is one of the “seven deadly sins” (the industry’s name for the Rev. Rul. 2004-86 restrictions) under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 that keep a DST passive and 1031-eligible. It generally bars new investor capital after closing, so the trust cannot raise more equity once funded. A related rule generally blocks debt renegotiation or refinancing except in narrow circumstances such as tenant bankruptcy. The trust is financially locked: broadly, the capital it has at closing is its capital for the whole hold. That is why it must establish an adequate reserve cushion upfront, and why a major expense can be difficult when reserves are insufficient.

### What is a springing LLC in a DST?

A springing LLC is a provision in many DST structures that allows conversion to a limited liability company in an emergency. It permits active steps, including raising capital or refinancing debt, that a DST cannot take. It is a safety valve when a major capital need or financial distress exceeds reserves and operating cash. But conversion typically ends the DST’s favorable status, importantly including 1031 eligibility for a future exchange, and can have serious consequences for investors. It is a last resort, not a normal source of funding; adequate reserves are intended to avoid it.

### How do reserves affect my distributions?

Cash held in reserve is not paid out, so reserves directly reduce current distributions. A DST with larger reserves generally distributes somewhat less current income; one with thinner reserves may pay more now but takes more risk if a major expense comes due. This is important in offering comparisons: a higher current distribution is not automatically better when it comes from inadequate reserves. Thin reserves can lead to reduced or suspended distributions later. Well-funded reserves can protect the investment and distribution durability over the hold, even with a lower headline yield. Consider whether yield differences reflect reserve adequacy.

### Does a higher DST distribution rate mean a better investment?

Not necessarily. A higher distribution rate can signal more risk. A sponsor can lift a headline yield by retaining thinner reserves and distributing cash that might otherwise support future capital needs. That raises current income but leaves the investment exposed if required capital cannot be funded, potentially resulting in later distribution reductions or suspension, or even springing-LLC conversion. A lower distribution paired with well-funded reserves can better protect income durability. High yields are not inherently bad, but investors should understand what supports them, including reserve adequacy for the property’s age, condition, and likely needs, and review the policy with advisors.

### How are DST reserves funded?

Reserves are typically funded upfront as the sponsor structures the offering. The sponsor estimates likely capital needs over the expected hold from building age and condition, property type, and lease rollover schedule, then sets aside cash in the offering economics. In some structures, operating cash flow may supplement reserves over time where rules and offering terms allow. The primary funding is generally at the start because new investor capital cannot be called later. The reserve remains available for capex and contingencies. Since the initial estimate cannot easily be remedied with new money, the sponsor’s underwriting and capital planning are important. Review how reserves were estimated and whether they appear adequate.

### What happens if a DST’s reserves run out?

If reserves run out and operating cash cannot cover a capital need, the DST has limited options. It generally cannot call new capital or freely refinance. In a genuine emergency, springing-LLC conversion may allow the entity to raise capital or refinance, but it typically ends the DST’s favorable status, including 1031 eligibility, and can seriously affect investors. A shortfall can also mean deferred maintenance, reduced or suspended distributions, or other property and investor problems. This is why reserve adequacy matters before investment. Discuss the reserve policy and contingency plans with advisors.

### How do I evaluate a DST’s reserve policy?

Start with the offering documents, which generally describe reserves, their funding, and the sponsor’s capital plan. Assess adequacy against building age and condition, property type, lease rollover schedule and tenant-improvement costs, and expected hold length. Older properties and those with substantial near-term lease turnover generally need larger reserves. Ask how the estimate was prepared, whether it reflects a realistic capital plan and property condition assessment, whether there is a contingency cushion, how the reserve compares with the distribution rate, and what happens if it falls short. These technical questions are best reviewed with a financial professional. Adequate reserves can indicate disciplined underwriting.

### Are DST reserves the same as operating expenses?

No. Operating expenses are recurring property costs—management, utilities, insurance, routine maintenance, and property taxes—paid from current operating income before distributions. They are ongoing, predictable items in cash flow. Reserves are for episodic, potentially large capital expenditures and contingencies, including roof or HVAC replacement and tenant improvements, which operating income might not cover when they arise. The difference matters because distributions follow operating expenses while reserves are a separate cushion. Reading the two buckets separately helps judge whether the operating budget and reserve balance are realistic.

### Do all DSTs hold reserves?

Most hold some reserves, but the amount and structure can vary substantially with property type, age, condition, and the sponsor’s approach. A newer property under a long-term net lease to a single creditworthy tenant, where the tenant often bears many capital costs, may require smaller trust-level reserves than an older multifamily property with frequent turnover and ongoing capital needs. The proper question is not whether every DST holds the same amount, but whether the amount suits that property’s likely needs over the hold. Because a DST cannot raise new capital later, under-reserving remains a real risk across property types. Judge each policy against age, condition, lease structure, and expected hold with a financial professional.

### Can a DST raise more money if it needs capital?

Generally, no. IRS rules preserving 1031 eligibility prohibit new investor capital after closing and generally restrict debt renegotiation or refinancing, except in narrow cases such as tenant bankruptcy. The trust cannot issue new equity or take a new loan for an unexpected capital need as an ordinary owner might. Its main resources are upfront reserves and current operating cash flow. The only true fallback is springing-LLC conversion, a drastic last resort that typically ends favorable DST status. The DST is largely financially locked at closing, which makes reserve adequacy one of the most important pre-investment questions.

### How do reserves relate to DST risk?

Reserves are central to risk because they are the trust’s primary buffer for future capital needs it cannot fund with new money. Adequate reserves position a DST to handle a new roof, HVAC replacement, tenant improvements, and unexpected repairs during a multi-year hold without disrupting property operations or investor distributions. Thin reserves raise the possibility of deferred maintenance, reduced or suspended distributions, or springing-LLC conversion. Review reserve adequacy with property quality, debt structure, the [master lease and master tenant arrangement that operates the property](dst-master-lease-structure-explained.html), and sponsor track record. A higher distribution funded with thin reserves can mean more risk, not a better deal. Reserves do not eliminate risk, but they reduce a specific and important one.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate as 1031-eligible fractional interests.

**Reserves:** Cash set aside upfront to fund future capital needs and contingencies.

**Capital Expenditure (Capex):** A larger, longer-lived property investment like a roof or HVAC.

**Tenant Improvement:** Build-out costs to attract or retain a tenant.

**Operating Expense:** A recurring cost of running the property, paid from current income.

**No-New-Capital Restriction:** The DST rule barring new investor capital after closing.

**Seven Deadly Sins:** The Rev. Rul. 2004-86 restrictions keeping a DST passive.

**Rev. Rul. 2004-86:** The IRS ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible like-kind property.

**Springing LLC:** A last-resort conversion letting a DST act actively in emergencies.

**Contingency:** An unanticipated cost the reserve cushion is meant to cover.

**Distribution:** The current cash flow a DST pays investors from operating income.

**Property Condition Assessment:** An evaluation of a building’s capital needs over time.

**Lease Rollover:** The schedule of lease expirations that drives capital costs.

**Hold Period:** The expected duration of a DST investment, often 5–7 years.

**Sponsor:** The firm that structures and offers the DST and sets reserves.

**Underwriting:** The sponsor’s analysis of a property’s income, costs, and reserves.
