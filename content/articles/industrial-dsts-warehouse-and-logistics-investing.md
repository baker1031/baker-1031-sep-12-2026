# Industrial DSTs: Warehouse & Logistics Investing

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the same picture: a delivery arrives, a store shelf is replenished, and it is easy to forget the warehouse network in between. Industrial real estate—warehouses, distribution centers, and last-mile logistics facilities—has become one of the more sought-after property types. This guide explains the demand case, the sub-types, net-lease income, risks, and the kinds of industrial DST offerings an investor may encounter.

An industrial DST holds one or more of those properties. Investors own fractional beneficial interests that qualify as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. That can let an exchanger defer capital-gains tax while gaining passive, professionally managed exposure to logistics real estate. DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after a suitability review. This is educational information, not investment, tax, or legal advice. For broader asset-class context, read [the full guide to DST investing](dst-guide.html).

The first-order view is straightforward: e-commerce grew, so warehouse space must be a winner. Second-order thinking asks what is already priced in, where new supply is arriving, how good the tenant and lease actually are, and whether the particular building will still be useful when the lease rolls. Those questions decide whether a strong sector story becomes a sound investment.

## Why Industrial Real Estate Is in Demand

Industrial demand reflects changes in how goods are bought, stored, and moved. The largest driver has been e-commerce. When shopping shifts online, retailers and logistics companies need more space to store inventory and fulfill orders. Online sales are widely estimated to require several times the warehouse space of store-based retail because goods are held closer to customers and handled more intensively.

Supply chains have changed too. Companies have added redundancy, held more safety stock, and moved distribution closer to end markets. Population growth and consumption provide a base level of demand. Last-mile delivery—the final push of a package to a customer—has added demand for smaller facilities near population centers.

Those are long-run structural forces, not promises about a particular property or market. Local supply and demand still vary. The useful conclusion is not that every industrial building is safe; it is that e-commerce growth, supply-chain reconfiguration, and last-mile delivery set the background against which each building, tenant, and submarket must be judged.

## Warehouse, Distribution & Last-Mile

Industrial is not one thing. Bulk warehouses and distribution centers are large facilities, often hundreds of thousands of square feet, used to store and move high volumes of goods. They are commonly near major highways, ports, rail lines, or airports so they can serve regional or national distribution. High ceilings, ample loading docks, and efficient layouts help tenants move freight at scale. These properties often lease on long terms to large, sometimes creditworthy tenants.

Last-mile logistics is a different niche. These are smaller buildings close to population centers, used for the final leg of a delivery. Their locations are often in dense, expensive markets, where speed matters and land can be scarce. Between the two sit light-industrial and flex properties that combine warehouse, light manufacturing, or office uses.

Location is the common thread. Proximity to highways, ports, rail, and the customers a facility serves drives value and tenant retention. A property’s type and location offer an early view of its demand durability and its re-leasing prospects.

## Net Leases & Income Stability

Industrial often appeals to income-focused 1031 investors because of net leases. In a net lease, a tenant pays base rent plus some or all property expenses, including taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The result can be more predictable owner income and less exposure to rising operating costs.

Single-tenant warehouses and distribution facilities often use long-term net leases—frequently many years and sometimes a decade or more—with built-in rent escalations. That can support contractually defined income during a DST hold. It is a different pattern from [multifamily rents, which reset roughly annually](multifamily-dsts-apartment-investing-for-your-1031.html).

The trade-off is concentration. A long lease to a strong, investment-grade tenant can be more valuable and lower-risk than a short lease to a weaker tenant. But the income is only as reliable as the tenant that pays it and the time left on the lease. Industrial may be steadier than an apartment property in one sense, while apartments spread tenant risk among many residents and reset rents more often in both directions.

## Risks to Consider

Strong demand can invite too much capital. New warehouse construction can flood a submarket with space, raise vacancy, and pressure rents when leases roll. The construction pipeline therefore matters. E-commerce growth can also slow or normalize after a surge, reducing the absorption of new space.

Tenant concentration is central in single-tenant industrial. A default, non-renewal, or downsizing can sharply reduce income. Re-leasing a large or specialized building may take time and money. Older or poorly located facilities can become obsolete as logistics requirements change. Lease term, tenant credit, and building functionality are not secondary details; they are core risk variables.

The usual DST risks remain: illiquidity during a multi-year hold, leverage, sponsor execution, and fees. A passive investor relies on the sponsor for tenant selection, lease structuring, and property management. Leverage amplifies gains and losses; floating-rate or short-maturity debt adds more uncertainty.

## Key Takeaways
- E-commerce and supply-chain reconfiguration support long-run demand, but new construction can still pressure rents and occupancy.
- Long net leases can support income stability, yet that income depends on tenant credit and the remaining lease term.
- A default or non-renewal can sharply reduce income in a single-tenant property.
- Evaluate tenant credit, lease term, and location near transportation and markets before deciding whether an industrial DST is durable enough for your capital.

## Sample Industrial Offerings

Industrial DST offerings tend to follow recognizable profiles. One may hold a single large distribution or warehouse facility, leased long-term on a net lease to one creditworthy tenant. Another may hold several industrial buildings across markets or tenants, spreading tenant and market exposure.

Some offerings emphasize bulk distribution near major logistics hubs; others emphasize last-mile facilities near population centers; others mix property types. Tenant credit quality and remaining lease term are major differentiators. A long lease to an investment-grade tenant generally supports a more conservative, income-stable profile. Shorter leases or weaker tenants carry more risk and can sometimes create more re-leasing upside.

The usual DST pattern is a roughly five-to-seven-year target hold with debt set at the trust level. A long net lease extending past that expected hold can improve income visibility, but it does not promise an outcome. These are illustrative characteristics, not specific securities or guaranteed returns. Projected income depends on tenant performance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an industrial DST?

An industrial DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust holding one or more warehouse, distribution, or last-mile logistics properties. Investors own fractional beneficial interests that qualify as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. A professional sponsor handles leasing, management, and an eventual sale while investors receive their share of income and any appreciation. The appeal is passive, 1031-eligible access to a sector supported by e-commerce and supply-chain change, often with net-lease income. Oversupply and tenant concentration still matter.

### Why is industrial real estate in such high demand?

E-commerce is widely estimated to require several times the warehouse space of store-based retail because goods are held closer to customers and handled more intensively. Businesses are also holding more inventory, adding redundancy, and locating distribution closer to end markets. Last-mile delivery creates demand for smaller customer-near facilities, while population growth and consumption support the base. These forces are broad and long-run, not a guarantee for a particular market or property.

### What are the different types of industrial property?

Bulk warehouses and distribution centers are often hundreds of thousands of square feet near highways, ports, rail, or airports. Last-mile buildings are smaller sites near population centers for rapid final-leg delivery. Flex properties mix warehouse, light manufacturing, and office uses. Transportation access and proximity to customers are central to value and tenant appeal in each type.

### What is a net lease and why does it matter for industrial?

Under a net lease, a tenant pays rent plus some or all taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Long industrial leases can include rent escalations and leave an owner less exposed to operating-cost increases. They can make income more predictable, but only while the tenant performs and the lease remains in place. Tenant credit and remaining term are therefore essential.

### How does industrial income stability compare to apartments?

Industrial often has long net leases that lock in rent and shift expense exposure to the tenant for years. Apartments typically reset leases roughly annually, allowing rents to respond more quickly to strong and weak markets. Industrial can thus have steadier contract income, but a single tenant’s default or non-renewal can be severe. Apartments spread tenant exposure across residents.

### What is tenant concentration risk in industrial DSTs?

It is the risk that one tenant supplies most or all property income. In a single-tenant facility, a default, downsize, or non-renewal can sharply cut income and leave the sponsor with a potentially slow, costly re-leasing process. A long lease to an investment-grade tenant generally presents less risk than a short lease to a weaker tenant. Multi-tenant and multi-property offerings can spread, but not eliminate, the risk.

### What is the risk of oversupply in industrial?

The demand story can prompt heavy construction. When a submarket receives too much new warehouse space, vacancy may rise and rents can weaken at lease rollover. Investors should examine local supply, the construction pipeline, the in-place lease, and whether e-commerce demand is slowing or normalizing.

### How do I evaluate an industrial DST?

Start with tenant credit, remaining lease term, rent escalations, and whether the property is single- or multi-tenant. Then assess location, property type, modern functionality, supply conditions, debt—its leverage, rate, fixed-versus-floating structure, and maturity—sponsor experience, fees, and projected distributions. A projected distribution is an estimate, not a guarantee. Do not choose solely on the highest projected yield.

### What types of industrial DST offerings are available?

Typical offerings include single large, net-leased warehouses or distribution centers and portfolios of several industrial buildings across markets and tenants. They can emphasize bulk logistics, last-mile sites, or a mix. Tenant quality, lease term, property type, trust-level debt, and a roughly five-to-seven-year target hold distinguish their risk profiles. These descriptions are general and illustrative, not specific securities or return promises.

### Can I use an industrial DST as 1031 replacement property?

Yes, a properly structured industrial DST can be replacement property because Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats its beneficial interests as direct interests in real estate for 1031 purposes. An investor must identify it within 45 days and close within 180 days, with a qualified intermediary holding sale proceeds. DST financing is generally non-recourse debt at the trust level and can assist with debt replacement. Interests remain securities for accredited investors after suitability review.

### What is last-mile logistics and why does it matter?

Last-mile logistics is the final trip from a local distribution facility to a customer. Smaller facilities near population centers allow fast, often same- or next-day delivery. Their proximity to dense markets and scarce land can make location especially valuable. Their durability also depends on the tenant and the population they serve.

### Are industrial DST distributions guaranteed?

No. Sponsor figures are projections based on assumptions about the lease, tenant, expenses, and financing. A tenant default, downsizing, non-renewal, oversupply at rollover, slower e-commerce demand, or leverage can change income. Floating-rate or short-maturity debt can add risk. Past performance does not guarantee future results, so the allocation should reflect that uncertainty.

### How does industrial compare to other DST sectors?

Industrial is tied to logistics demand and long net leases. Multifamily is more tied to household formation and frequent rent resets; senior housing to aging demographics and operating performance; medical office to healthcare demand, reimbursement, and tenant credit. Industrial can offer contract income stability but carries its own concentration, oversupply, and e-commerce risks.

### What are the main risks of an industrial DST?

They include oversupply, slower demand, tenant concentration, obsolescence, lease rollover, illiquidity, leverage, sponsor execution, and fees. The passive structure means the investor relies on the sponsor’s tenant selection and management. Tenant credit, lease term, and location deserve particular scrutiny, and distributions are projections rather than promises.

## Glossary

- **Industrial DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding warehouse or logistics property.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust whose fractional interests are 1031-eligible like-kind real property.
- **Beneficial Interest:** An investor’s fractional ownership share in a DST.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.
- **IRS Rev. Rul. 2004-86:** The ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible real property.
- **Net Lease:** A lease in which the tenant pays rent plus property expenses.
- **Distribution Center:** A large warehouse used to store and move goods at scale.
- **Last-Mile Logistics:** Small facilities near customers for fast final-leg delivery.
- **E-Commerce:** Online retail, a key driver of warehouse demand.
- **Tenant Concentration:** Reliance on one tenant for most of a property’s income.
- **Tenant Credit:** A tenant’s financial strength, which supports lease income.
- **Lease Term:** The remaining years on a lease; longer is generally steadier.
- **Oversupply:** Excess new construction that pressures rents and occupancy.
- **Hold Period:** The roughly five-to-seven-year expected DST ownership term.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that structures and manages the DST and its property.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DST securities.
