# Data Center REITs Explained

I have been watching the data-center conversation get reduced to two letters: AI. The demand matters. But the real work of underwriting this sector still begins with power, capital spending, tenants, and price.

A data center REIT owns the specialized facilities that hold the computing infrastructure behind cloud services, enterprise IT, and increasingly artificial intelligence. Those facilities lease space, power, and cooling to cloud providers, enterprises, and large hyperscale tenants. They have benefited from a long-run rise in data and computing. They are also unusually capital-hungry, critically dependent on electrical power, and exposed to obsolescence and tenant concentration.

These are general, educational observations—not promises about demand, returns, or outlook. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and current market conditions should be verified with your adviser. For private and non-traded structures beyond this sector, see the [complete guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

First-order thinking sees cloud demand and AI demand and assumes the outcome follows. Second-order thinking asks whether capacity can obtain power, how much cash must be reinvested to keep facilities competitive, how concentrated the rent roll is, and how much of the growth thesis is already priced into shares. That is the difference between identifying a secular trend and underwriting a REIT.

## What Data Center REITs Own

A data center REIT owns and operates data centers: specialized facilities built for computer servers and the systems that keep them running. These are not ordinary buildings. They are engineered around power delivery, cooling, redundancy, and security, often using raised floors or specialized layouts, backup generators, and uninterruptible power supplies.

The REIT leases space, power, and cooling capacity to tenants that place their computing equipment there. Like other equity REITs, it distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders; [REITs must pay out at least 90% of taxable income](reit-investing-for-beginners-how-to-get-started.html).

Tenant models differ:

- **Enterprise customers** lease space to run their own IT.
- **Colocation customers** rent cabinets, cages, or partial halls within a shared facility.
- **Hyperscale tenants**—very large cloud and technology companies—may lease whole data halls or campuses, sometimes constructed to their specifications.

Some REITs operate dense urban interconnection hubs where networks meet. Others build large suburban or rural campuses built for scale and power. A lease can cover a powered shell—the physical building and power, with the tenant completing the buildout—or a fully built and operated facility. That choice affects operating costs and risk allocation.

The asset is therefore a purpose-built server facility, but the rent roll can range from many smaller colocation customers to a few very large hyperscalers. That mix, and the sector’s much greater capital intensity than ordinary buildings, frames the demand, capital, and concentration questions that follow.

## Cloud & AI Demand

The core growth case is a secular rise in computing and data. Cloud computing moved significant amounts of IT out of company-owned server rooms and into large, professionally operated data centers. Enterprise digitization, streaming, e-commerce, and the broader growth of data all add demand for the space, power, and cooling those facilities provide. It is a structural change in how computing is delivered, not simply an economic-cycle event.

AI has added another source of demand. Training and inference need dense, power-hungry computing. That has increased interest in data-center capacity and especially in facilities with access to substantial electrical power.

The caveat is important. Demand can be lumpy, competitors can add capacity, and a compelling secular thesis can be priced into a security before an investor arrives. Cloud and AI are favorable inputs to evaluate, not a guarantee of any individual REIT’s results.

> Data centers sit at the intersection of cloud computing and AI. That is a secular tailwind, but it still has to be evaluated REIT by REIT rather than treated as a guarantee.

## Power, Cooling & Capex

Servers consume substantial electricity and create heat. A data center needs reliable, large-scale power and sophisticated cooling to operate continuously. In many markets, available power and the ability to connect to the grid now limit where and how quickly new capacity can be built. Power access is not a minor operating input; it can be a strategic asset.

The facilities are expensive to build and maintain. Construction costs are high, and power systems, cooling equipment, and backup generators require continuing investment and eventual replacement. Computing needs change over time, so designs may need upgrades to remain competitive. Older facilities can become less efficient or obsolete.

That spending affects free cash flow. A data center REIT must keep investing both to develop new capacity and to maintain what it already owns. The same high capital requirement that can create barriers to entry makes balance-sheet strength, cooling efficiency, power access, and capital discipline central to the economics.

## Tenant Concentration Risk

Hyperscale cloud and technology companies are a major source of demand, but they are also a small group of very large customers. A REIT focused on hyperscale leasing can have a meaningful portion of revenue tied to a handful of tenants. Those tenants are often creditworthy and sign substantial, long-term leases. Their scale also gives them negotiating power.

The exposure becomes visible if a key tenant reduces its footprint, builds its own facilities, or does not renew. Concentration can also exist by geography: a REIT built around a few large campuses or one market is exposed to local conditions. Large hyperscale signings can make leasing lumpy—strong in a year with a major deal and weaker in a year without one.

Colocation-focused REITs may have more diversified rent rolls because they serve many smaller customers, though their economics differ. The practical question is not merely whether a REIT is growing. It is how much revenue comes from the top tenants, what their credit quality and lease terms are, and whether tenant, market, and lease-expiration exposures are diversified or staggered.

## Key Takeaways

- Cloud computing, digitization, and AI support a secular demand case for data centers, but they do not guarantee the outcome for any REIT.
- Data centers are power-, cooling-, and capital-intensive. Access to electrical power is a key constraint, and ongoing capex is high.
- Hyperscale demand can also create tenant concentration, with a few customers moving a meaningful portion of revenue.
- Technology changes quickly and development is difficult, so obsolescence and execution risk matter.

## Obsolescence & Execution Risk

Data centers are high-technology real estate. Server density, power requirements, and cooling approaches evolve. A facility designed for one generation of workloads may require upgrades—or become less competitive—as tenant requirements change. AI workloads have increased desired power density beyond assumptions used in some older designs. A data center is not simply a building that can be completed and left to collect rent; it needs ongoing reinvestment to stay current.

Execution has equal weight. The operator must secure power, land, and permits; build on time and on budget; lease new capacity; and run the facilities reliably. Power and supply-chain constraints can delay a project. Construction can exceed budget. Newly completed capacity still must be leased.

An operator that secures power, delivers projects, leases them, and maintains its facilities can benefit from the demand backdrop. An operator that stumbles in power, construction, or leasing can disappoint even when the market looks favorable. Management quality and operating capability are therefore central.

> A data center is high-technology real estate: a strong demand tailwind rewards operators that can secure power, build on time, and keep facilities current—and exposes those that cannot.

## Evaluating Data Center REITs

Start with the details that set the sector apart:

- **Tenant quality and concentration.** Review the rent roll, top-tenant exposure, credit quality, lease terms, and expiration schedule.
- **Power access.** Examine where the REIT can obtain power and connect to the grid; power availability is a key growth constraint.
- **Leasing and pre-leasing.** Pre-leasing on development projects can indicate demand for capacity that is being built.
- **Location.** Interconnection-rich markets and power-rich regions serve different functions and have different economics.

Then use standard REIT measures with the capital burden in view. FFO and AFFO are cash-flow measures REIT investors use in place of net income, but they are not enough by themselves. Capital expenditures and development spending can consume cash that a simpler property type might distribute. Review the balance sheet, development pipeline and its funding, and management’s history of building, leasing, and operating.

These are evaluation factors, not predictions. Share prices and distributions can fluctuate, and a secular narrative does not determine any one outcome. Verify current conditions in the REIT’s markets and balance each item against valuation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a data center REIT?

A data center REIT is an equity Real Estate Investment Trust that owns and operates data centers. These specialized facilities house computer servers and their power, cooling, redundancy, and security systems. The REIT leases space, power, and cooling to enterprise customers, colocation customers, and hyperscale cloud and technology companies. It distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders because REITs must pay out at least **90%** of taxable income.

These facilities are more capital-intensive than ordinary buildings and sit at the center of cloud computing and increasingly AI. The investment is exposure to physical digital infrastructure and the rental income it generates.

### What drives demand for data centers?

Demand comes from the long-term rise in computing and data. Cloud migration moved IT from company server rooms to professionally managed facilities. Enterprise digitization, streaming, e-commerce, and the growth of data reinforce that trend. AI training and inference add demand for dense, power-hungry capacity and for facilities with substantial electrical power.

The trend is secular because it reflects a structural change, not only a business cycle. Yet demand can be lumpy, competition can add new capacity, and a strong thesis can already be reflected in share prices. Treat the demand case as a favorable input to assess, not as a promise.

### Why is power so important for data center REITs?

Servers consume electricity and create heat that must be removed. A facility therefore needs reliable, large-scale power and advanced cooling. Power availability and grid-connection capacity now limit where and how quickly new data-center capacity can be built in many markets.

REITs and tenants compete for sites and connections that can support large and growing loads. AI has intensified the competition by requiring higher power densities. Power access is a first-order consideration: it affects capacity growth, build speed, and the value of particular facilities. Current local power conditions should be checked rather than assumed.

### What is tenant concentration risk in data center REITs?

Tenant concentration risk is the risk that a large share of revenue depends on a small number of customers. In data centers, a few hyperscale companies can account for a meaningful portion of revenue. They are often creditworthy and sign long-term leases, but they also have negotiating power. A reduction in footprint, a decision to self-build, or a nonrenewal can matter materially.

Concentration can also be geographic or deal-specific, with large signings making leasing lumpy year to year. Review the rent roll, top-tenant share, credit quality, and lease expirations alongside market concentration.

### Are data center REITs capital-intensive?

Yes. They require costly power systems, sophisticated cooling, backup generators, uninterruptible power supplies, and robust security. The equipment requires continuing investment and replacement, while technology can require upgrades to maintain competitiveness.

Those needs consume free cash flow through capex and development spending. Capital intensity can create barriers to entry, but it makes balance-sheet strength and disciplined capital allocation especially important. Look beyond headline revenue or FFO to how growth is funded.

### How does AI affect data center REITs?

AI training and inference require dense, power-hungry computing. That has increased demand for capacity and for sites with substantial power, reinforcing the sector’s growth case and competition for suitable locations. It has also increased desired power density, which can favor newer, purpose-built capacity and put upgrade or obsolescence pressure on some older designs.

AI remains a thesis to evaluate. Demand can be lumpy, substantial new capacity is planned or under construction, and an exciting trend can be priced into shares. The pace and durability of AI-related demand are uncertain.

### What is obsolescence risk for data centers?

Obsolescence risk is the possibility that a facility becomes less efficient or competitive as computing needs change. Server density, power requirements, and cooling methods evolve. AI loads, for example, can call for higher power density than older facilities assumed.

The consequence is continuing reinvestment. An operator that keeps facilities modern and efficient can remain attractive to tenants. One that under-invests can see its facilities age and pricing power weaken. Consider facility age and design, as well as the commitment to reinvestment, not only current occupancy.

### How do data center REITs make money?

They primarily lease space, power, and cooling capacity to tenants that house equipment in their facilities. A lease can be for a powered shell or a fully built and operated site, affecting who bears operating costs. Customers include enterprises, colocation tenants, and hyperscale companies, whose leases can be long-term and may include escalators.

After operating costs, REITs distribute most taxable income as dividends because of the **90%** distribution rule. Some operators also earn interconnection revenue by connecting tenant networks within interconnection-rich facilities; that can be a sticky, high-margin source of revenue. Growth can come from developing new capacity, leasing it, and raising rents where demand allows. Heavy capital spending offsets part of that cash flow.

### What should I look at when evaluating a data center REIT?

Review tenant quality, concentration, power access, leasing and pre-leasing, and location. Study the rent roll, tenant credit, lease expirations, and the role of power-rich and interconnection-rich markets. Then assess FFO and AFFO together with capital expenditures and development spending, the balance sheet, how the pipeline is funded, and management’s record in development, leasing, and operations.

These are general factors rather than a forecast. Shares and distributions can change, the secular case does not guarantee an outcome, and current conditions should be checked.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange into a data center REIT?

No. Data center REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property, so they are not eligible replacement property for a direct §1031 exchange. An investor cannot sell investment real estate and directly defer capital-gains tax by acquiring data-center REIT shares.

There is an indirect sequence that can ultimately lead to REIT exposure. An investor may exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), which can be like-kind real property for §1031 purposes. The DST’s property may later be acquired by a REIT in a **721** UPREIT exchange, potentially converting the interest into operating-partnership units and eventually REIT shares while preserving deferral. The mechanics are technical and situation-specific. Consult a tax adviser before relying on them.

### How are data center REIT dividends taxed?

They are generally taxed like other REIT dividends. Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at lower qualified-dividend rates because the REIT did not pay corporate tax. A **20%** Section **199A** deduction can apply to qualified REIT dividends, lowering the effective top federal rate on those dividends.

Some distributions can be return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than being immediately taxed, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the mix on Form **1099-DIV** each year. Treatment can vary as a REIT reinvests and from year to year, and rules can change. ### Are data center REITs a growth or income investment?

They are often considered growth-oriented because cloud computing and AI have supported capacity expansion and cash-flow growth. They still pay dividends because REITs distribute most taxable income. Their heavy development spending can favor growth over a high current yield relative to some other REIT sectors.

That profile has risks. The sector is capital-intensive, power-constrained, concentrated among large tenants, exposed to obsolescence, and subject to the possibility that a strong trend is already priced into shares. It can suit an investor seeking growth-oriented real-estate exposure who understands these risks, but growth is not guaranteed. These are general observations, not a specific recommendation; past performance does not guarantee future results and current conditions should be verified.

### What is the difference between hyperscale and colocation data centers?

Hyperscale facilities serve very large cloud and technology companies that lease entire data halls or campuses, sometimes built to their specifications. Their contracts can be large, long-term, and with creditworthy tenants, but they can concentrate revenue and make leasing lumpy.

Colocation facilities host many smaller tenants that rent cabinets, cages, or partial halls within a shared site. That can produce a more diversified rent roll and frequently includes interconnection services, which can be sticky and high-margin. Some REITs emphasize one model and others use both. The model shapes the risk and economics: hyperscale offers scale but more concentration, while colocation offers diversification and numerous smaller relationships.

### What are the main risks of data center REITs?

Tenant concentration is one risk: a few hyperscale customers can drive revenue and change plans. Power risk is another: limited electrical access can slow growth or leave sites unable to support intended capacity. Capital intensity and obsolescence require continuing investment. Execution risk arises in securing power, constructing facilities, leasing them, and operating them; supply-chain or construction problems can delay projects.

Competitive-supply risk matters because a favorable demand outlook draws new development and can pressure rents in some markets. Valuation risk matters because a powerful secular thesis can be priced into shares. General REIT risks also remain, including interest-rate sensitivity, market volatility, possible distribution cuts, and the possibility of losing value. Past performance does not guarantee future results; verify current conditions.

## Glossary

- **Data Center REIT:** An equity REIT that owns and operates data-center facilities.
- **Data Center:** A facility built to house servers, power, and cooling.
- **Hyperscale Tenant:** A very large cloud or technology company leasing substantial capacity.
- **Colocation:** A shared-facility model that hosts many smaller tenants.
- **Interconnection:** Connecting tenants’ networks within a data center.
- **Powered Shell:** A leased building and power, built out by the tenant.
- **Cloud Computing:** Delivering computing over the internet from large data centers.
- **Secular Demand:** Long-term demand growth driven by structural change.
- **Power Access:** Availability of electrical power, a key sector constraint.
- **Cooling:** Systems that remove the heat generated by running servers.
- **Capex:** Capital expenditures used to build and maintain facilities.
- **Obsolescence Risk:** The risk that a facility falls behind as technology evolves.
- **Pre-Leasing:** Signing tenants for capacity before it is built.
- **FFO:** Funds from operations, a REIT’s core cash-flow measure.
- **AFFO:** Adjusted FFO, net of recurring capital expenditures.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement that a REIT pay out at least **90%** of taxable income.

## A practical stance before committing capital

I would treat data-center exposure as a question of cash-flow durability, not merely of how many servers the world will need. Before committing capital, I would focus on power rights, funded capex, tenant concentration, leasing terms, facility design, and the price paid for the thesis. A strong story can remain a poor investment when its risks are underweighted or its price leaves little room for them.
