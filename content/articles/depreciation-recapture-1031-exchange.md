# Depreciation Recapture and the 1031 Exchange

I keep seeing sale projections that treat every dollar of gain as capital gain. On a long-held rental, that can be a costly mistake. The deductions that sheltered rental income each year may create a depreciation-recapture layer at sale, often at a rate up to **25%** and sometimes large enough to exceed the capital-gains tax.

First-order thinking says depreciation was a benefit received in the past, so the sale price is the only number that matters now. Second-order thinking recognizes the lower basis, the accumulated recapture, the potential boot, and the choice between a taxable sale and a properly structured exchange. Price is visible. The embedded tax is often what changes the economics.

Depreciation is valuable during ownership. It can shelter income. But it also reduces basis and sets up recapture when you sell. This guide covers what that means, how it is calculated, why it has a higher rate, how a 1031 exchange defers it, and how to plan for the eventual sale.

## What depreciation recapture is

While you own rental property, you deduct depreciation each year. The deduction reduces taxable rental income and lowers the property’s adjusted basis. When the property is sold, the portion of gain attributable to accumulated depreciation is generally recaptured as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, rather than treated solely as ordinary long-term capital gain.

The basic logic is that the IRS allowed deductions during ownership and recovers part of that benefit at sale. Recapture applies to depreciation you actually took **or were allowed to take**. Skipping a deduction does not generally make the recapture disappear; it can simply mean you gave up the deduction.

This is why many long-term landlords are surprised. They think their gain is appreciation. In fact, a meaningful share can be prior depreciation, carrying a potentially higher tax rate.

## How depreciation works during ownership

Residential rental property is generally depreciated straight-line over **27.5 years**. Commercial property is generally depreciated straight-line over **39 years**. The deduction applies to the building value, not the land, because land is not depreciable.

That annual deduction can shelter some or all rental income. It is one of real estate’s important tax benefits. But each dollar of depreciation reduces basis by a dollar, which helps set up the recapture amount at sale.

Some investors use [cost segregation](cost-segregation.html) to classify components as shorter-lived property and front-load deductions. That can improve early cash-tax results, but it can also increase recapture later. Some segregated personal-property components can be Section 1245 property, which may face higher ordinary-income recapture.

## Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain

For real property, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) is technically called unrecaptured Section 1250 gain. Its maximum federal rate is **25%**. The “unrecaptured” label distinguishes real-property treatment from the full ordinary-income recapture that can apply to certain personal property.

The **25%** figure is a maximum. If an investor’s ordinary rate is lower, the recapture is taxed at that lower rate. For many investment-property sellers with meaningful income, however, the maximum rate applies to this portion of gain.

Section 1245 property—such as some equipment or cost-segregated components—can be subject to ordinary-income recapture at a higher rate. That is one reason cost-segregation history belongs in the sale and exchange analysis.

## Why the rate is higher

Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain can be taxed at up to **25%**, above the **15–20%** long-term capital-gains rate. The higher rate recovers part of the benefit from deductions that may have offset income taxed at ordinary rates during ownership.

The longer the hold and the more depreciation claimed, the larger this layer can become. That is why recapture is often the largest single tax on a long-held rental, and why its treatment is central to an exchange decision.

## Calculating recapture

Start with the total depreciation taken or allowed. That amount is generally your unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, taxed at up to **25%**. The remaining gain—the appreciation above original cost—is generally taxed at [capital-gains rates](capital-gains-tax-rates-investment-property.html).

Consider a simple example. You buy for **$300,000**, take **$100,000** of depreciation, reducing basis to **$200,000**, and sell for **$500,000**. Total gain is **$300,000**. Of that, **$100,000** is recapture, potentially taxed up to **25%**, and **$200,000** is appreciation, generally taxed at **15–20%** capital-gains rates.

Your CPA should calculate the actual figures, including any cost-segregation effects. The working framework is still useful: depreciation taken or allowed is generally the recapture layer; the balance is appreciation.

## How a 1031 defers recapture

A 1031 exchange [defers depreciation recapture along with the capital gain](capital-gains-tax-deferral-1031-exchange.html). Nothing is recognized at a properly structured exchange. The recapture exposure carries into the replacement property and is postponed until a later taxable sale.

That matters because the recapture layer can be taxed at the higher **25%** rate. Deferral keeps that capital invested rather than paying it to the IRS at sale. The mechanism is carryover basis: the depreciated adjusted basis follows the investor into the replacement property along with the rest of the deferred gain.

### Key takeaways

- Prior depreciation can be recaptured at up to **25%** and is often the biggest tax on a long-held rental.
- A 1031 exchange defers recapture and capital gain through carryover basis.
- Deferral is not elimination during life, although a step-up in basis at death can eliminate deferred recapture for heirs.

## Carryover basis and recapture

An exchange does not create a fresh basis in the replacement. The depreciated adjusted basis carries over, increased by any additional cash invested. Because the low basis carries the deferred gain, it also carries the recapture exposure.

The exposure is not erased. It is preserved in the replacement property and generally recognized if you later sell without another exchange, unless it is eliminated by a step-up in basis at death. Accurate basis records are therefore essential, especially across a multi-exchange lifetime. Your CPA should track both the carryover basis and the embedded recapture.

## Continuing and restarting depreciation

After a 1031 exchange, you generally continue depreciating the carried-over basis on its existing schedule. Any additional basis created by new investment is depreciated separately as though it were newly placed in service.

That lets the replacement property continue providing depreciation deductions. It also means new depreciation adds to future recapture, which a later exchange may again defer. The technical treatment of carried-over and excess basis affects the schedule, so the CPA should handle the mechanics.

## Recapture on eventual sale

If you eventually sell the replacement property outright rather than exchanging again, the deferred recapture and deferred capital gain come due. The recapture accumulated through the chain is then recognized, at up to **25%** for the Section 1250 portion.

That is why a 1031 is a deferral, not an exemption. A longer exchange chain can carry a larger embedded recapture amount. Many long-term investors therefore plan not to sell outright, instead exchanging until death and relying on a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) as part of the endgame.

## How the step-up eliminates recapture

When an owner dies holding the replacement property, heirs generally receive a basis equal to fair-market value at the date of death. That reset can wipe out both deferred capital gain and deferred depreciation recapture.

Heirs may then sell shortly after inheritance with little or no taxable gain or recapture. Decades of deferred recapture across multiple exchanges can be eliminated entirely. This is the capstone of the “swap till you drop” approach, but it requires coordination of final exchanges and estate planning with a CPA and attorney.

## Recapture, boot, and the order of taxation

When an exchange recognizes partial gain—for example, because the investor takes boot—recapture is generally recognized first. Even a modest amount of boot can therefore face the **25%** recapture rate instead of the lower capital-gains rate.

On a heavily depreciated property, that makes avoiding boot especially important. A zero-boot exchange preserves the deferral of the high-rated recapture. Your CPA accounts for the ordering on Form **8824**, but the planning lesson is plain: treat cash or debt shortfalls with extra caution.

## Recapture and cost segregation

Cost-segregation studies classify portions of a property as shorter-lived assets, accelerating deductions and front-loading tax savings. They can also increase recapture at sale. Some segregated components are Section 1245 property, subject to higher ordinary-income recapture rather than the **25%** Section 1250 framework.

A 1031 exchange can still defer the gain, but cost segregation and bonus depreciation make the analysis more technical. If you have used either, work closely with your CPA; the standard **25%** shorthand may not describe the whole picture.

## A worked recapture example

Suppose you bought a rental for **$400,000** twenty years ago, allocating **$300,000** to the building and **$100,000** to land. Over those **20** years, you depreciated the building by roughly **$218,000**, reducing adjusted basis to about **$182,000**. You now sell for **$700,000**.

Your total gain is about **$518,000**. Roughly **$218,000** is depreciation recapture, potentially taxed at up to **25%**, or as much as **$54,500**. The remaining **$300,000** is appreciation, taxed at **15–20%** capital-gains rates. Add the **3.8%** [NIIT](net-investment-income-tax.html) and any state tax, and recapture becomes one of the largest pieces of a substantial bill.

The **$54,500** recapture tax at **25%** is comparable to or larger than the capital-gains tax on **$300,000** of appreciation—**$45,000–$60,000** at **15–20%**. With a longer holding period and more depreciation, the recapture layer can matter even more.

[A 1031 exchange can defer the entire amount](how-much-tax-does-a-1031-exchange-defer.html): recapture, capital gains, NIIT, and state tax. It carries the **$182,000** basis into the replacement property. Rather than paying well over **$100,000** in combined tax, the investor reinvests full proceeds and keeps the high-rated recapture deferred. These figures are illustrative, but they show why recapture can be the heart of deferral on a long-held rental.

## Common recapture mistakes and misunderstandings

The first mistake is not recognizing recapture before listing the property. An investor expects only capital-gains tax, then is surprised by a large bill at up to **25%**. Awareness is the start of planning.

The second is declining to claim depreciation on the theory that it avoids recapture. It does not. Recapture generally applies to depreciation taken or allowed. Failing to claim it may forfeit the deduction without avoiding recapture; a CPA can often correct missed depreciation.

The third is taking boot on a heavily depreciated property without recognizing that recapture generally comes first. The fourth is mishandling cost-segregated property, where some components can face ordinary-income recapture. These mistakes are avoidable with records, planning, and a good CPA. A properly structured, zero-boot 1031 exchange is the source’s most effective way to defer this tax layer.

## Coordinating with your CPA

Depreciation schedules, basis, recapture, and carryover treatment are technical and interrelated. Bring your depreciation history, basis records, and exchange documents to your accountant before closing, so recapture can be calculated and reported correctly.

Your CPA reports the exchange on Form **8824**, tracks the carryover basis and embedded recapture in the replacement property, sets continuing and new depreciation schedules, and handles the recapture-first treatment of boot. Those records become essential over multiple exchanges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is depreciation recapture in a 1031 exchange?

It is tax on gain attributable to depreciation taken while you held the property. On sale, it is generally unrecaptured Section 1250 gain taxed at up to **25%**. A 1031 defers it with the capital gain and carries it into the replacement property through carryover basis.

### Why is recapture taxed at 25%?

The **25%** cap is above the long-term capital-gains rate because it recovers part of the benefit from depreciation deductions. On a long-held rental, it is often the largest tax layer.

### How do I calculate depreciation recapture?

Start with total depreciation taken or allowed. That amount is generally Section 1250 gain taxed up to **25%**. The remaining appreciation above original cost is generally taxed at capital-gains rates. Your CPA computes the exact result.

### Does a 1031 exchange eliminate recapture?

No. It defers recapture. The exposure carries through the replacement property and is recognized on a later taxable sale unless a step-up in basis at death eliminates it.

### How does the step-up in basis affect recapture?

Heirs generally receive basis equal to fair-market value at death. That can wipe out deferred capital gain and deferred depreciation recapture, including amounts accumulated through multiple exchanges.

### Can I keep depreciating after a 1031 exchange?

Generally, yes. You continue the carried-over basis on its existing schedule and depreciate additional basis from new investment separately. New depreciation creates future recapture that a later exchange can defer.

### Is recapture taxed before capital gains when I take boot?

Generally, yes. Partial recognized gain, including boot, recognizes recapture first. Even modest boot can face **25%** recapture, which makes avoiding boot important on heavily depreciated property.

### How long is rental property depreciated?

Residential rental property is generally depreciated straight-line over **27.5 years**. Commercial property is generally depreciated straight-line over **39 years**. Only the building is depreciated; land is not.

### What is unrecaptured Section 1250 gain?

It is the technical name for real-property depreciation recapture: gain tied to prior depreciation, taxed at a maximum **25%**. It is distinct from ordinary-income recapture on certain personal property.

### Does cost segregation increase my recapture?

Yes. Cost segregation accelerates deductions through shorter-lived components, which can increase recapture. Some components can be Section 1245 property with higher ordinary-income recapture. Work with your CPA if you have used it.

### Why does recapture surprise long-term landlords?

They often see gain as appreciation alone. Years of deductions can create a large recapture amount at the higher **25%** rate that is easy to overlook until sale.

### Is recapture always taxed at 25%?

No. **25%** is the maximum federal rate for unrecaptured Section 1250 gain. If your ordinary rate is lower, that lower rate can apply. The maximum often applies to investment-property sellers with meaningful income.

### Do I owe recapture if I never claimed depreciation?

Generally, yes. The calculation uses depreciation taken or allowed. Not claiming the deduction generally forfeits it without avoiding recapture. Claim and track depreciation, and ask your CPA about correcting a missed deduction.

### How does a 1031 help with recapture specifically?

It defers the recapture, potentially taxed at the higher **25%** rate, along with capital gain. That keeps the tax amount invested and compounding. For a heavily depreciated, long-held property, this can be a major part of an exchange’s value.

### Who calculates and reports my recapture?

Your CPA. The CPA calculates it, tracks carryover basis and embedded recapture, establishes continuing and new schedules, and reports the exchange on Form **8824**, including recapture-first ordering when there is boot.

### Can recapture be larger than my capital-gains tax?

Yes. Decades of depreciation can create a large amount taxed up to **25%**, which can exceed the tax on appreciation at **15–20%**. It is frequently the single largest component of the tax bill on a long-held property.

### Does taking boot affect my recapture?

Yes, significantly. Recapture is generally recognized first when boot causes partial gain. On a heavily depreciated property, avoiding boot helps preserve deferral of the high-rate layer.

### What happens to recapture across multiple exchanges?

Deferred recapture carries forward through each exchange with carryover basis, along with new depreciation taken on replacement properties. It is recognized if you eventually sell without another exchange, or it can be eliminated by a step-up in basis if the final property is held until death.

### Is land subject to depreciation recapture?

No. Land is not depreciable, so it produces no depreciation and no recapture. Only the building portion is depreciated and recaptured, which makes the land/building basis allocation important.

### How does bonus depreciation affect recapture?

Bonus depreciation accelerates some first-year deductions. It can increase recapture at sale, and some bonus-depreciated components can be Section 1245 property with ordinary-income recapture. Work with your CPA on the exchange treatment.

### Why is recapture central to the value of a 1031 exchange?

Long-held, heavily depreciated properties often have a large recapture layer taxed up to **25%**. Deferring that layer keeps substantial capital invested, and a final step-up in basis can eliminate it. It is often the heart of the deferral.

### Should I avoid depreciation to avoid recapture?

No. Depreciation is a useful income shelter, and recapture generally applies whether you claim it or not because the rule looks to depreciation taken or allowed. Claim it, track it, and consider deferring the recapture through a 1031 exchange.

## Glossary

- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on gain attributable to prior depreciation, up to **25%** on real property.
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain:** The real-property recapture portion of gain, taxed at a maximum **25%**.
- **Section 1245 Property:** Certain personal property, including some cost-segregated components, that can face higher ordinary-income recapture.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Cost plus improvements minus depreciation; each year’s depreciation lowers it.
- **Carryover Basis:** The relinquished property’s adjusted basis carried into replacement property, preserving recapture exposure.
- **Straight-Line Depreciation:** Even building depreciation over **27.5 years** for residential property or **39 years** for commercial property.
- **Cost Segregation:** A study that classifies components as shorter-lived, increasing early deductions and recapture.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The reset to fair-market value at death that can eliminate deferred gain and recapture for heirs.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form reporting a like-kind exchange, including recognized gain and recapture.
- **Boot:** Taxable value received in an exchange; recapture is generally recognized first when boot is taxed.
- **Bonus Depreciation:** An accelerated first-year deduction for certain property that can increase recapture at sale.
- **Land vs. Building:** Only the building is depreciable, so the allocation affects both depreciation and recapture.
