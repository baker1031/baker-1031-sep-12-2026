# Estate Planning Benefits of DSTs: Step-Up in Basis

I have been thinking about how often a tax benefit gets described as if it settles an estate decision. It does not. A step-up in basis can be enormously valuable, but the family still has to decide who will own the asset, who will manage it, how much liquidity is needed, and whether the structure fits the estate plan.

The first-order view of a DST is that a 1031 exchange defers capital-gains tax. The second-order view is what happens if a passive, fractional interest is held for life: under current law, the combination of deferral and a death-time basis reset may allow heirs to receive a more easily divided interest without inheriting the accumulated income-tax gain.

## The Step-Up in Basis Advantage

The [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) is the central estate-planning feature of a DST held until death. Under Section 1014, the basis of property is generally reset to fair market value as of the owner's date of death. For appreciated assets, heirs start with current value rather than the original cost. If they sell near that value, their gain is measured from the stepped-up basis, not from the decedent's low basis.

That rule matters particularly after a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). A 1031 defers capital-gains tax rather than eliminating it. The low carryover basis from the relinquished property moves into the DST; depreciation may reduce that basis further. At a lifetime taxable sale, deferred gain and [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) would ordinarily be recognized.

Holding the DST until death can change that outcome. If the Section 1014 step-up applies, the heirs' basis is reset to fair market value. The deferred gain carried through the exchanges can be erased for income-tax purposes. This is the idea behind the informal phrase “swap till you drop”: defer through life, then allow the step-up to reset the basis for heirs.

## Erasing Deferred Capital Gains

It helps to follow the basis rather than the slogan. An investor may sell a long-held rental with a low basis, exchange into a DST, and continue to defer. The carryover basis stays low, while depreciation can reduce it further. The investment may therefore carry a large embedded deferred gain and potential recapture. If it is sold during the investor's lifetime, that amount is generally recognized unless the investor defers again.

If the interest is held until death and the step-up applies, the heirs receive a fair-market-value basis. The old low basis—and the embedded gain and recapture it supported—can disappear for income-tax purposes. A sale shortly afterward may produce little or no gain because the new basis is near the sale price.

> A 1031 exchange defers the tax. A step-up at death can erase the deferred gain for income-tax purposes. The result can be a lifetime of deferral followed by an heir's basis at current value.

That is a planning opportunity, not a guarantee. Tax law can change, the asset must actually be held until death, and the estate plan has to be structured correctly. A CPA and estate attorney should apply the rules to the investor's facts.

## Dividing Fractional Interests Among Heirs

Direct real estate is often hard to split. An apartment building, an office property, or a ranch cannot neatly be divided into three working pieces for three children. A forced sale can create tax or end an income stream; continued co-ownership can create conflict over management and timing.

DST beneficial interests are fractional. An estate plan can allocate equal shares, weighted portions, or interests held in separate trusts for different beneficiaries. Each heir can receive an interest without being forced into the joint management of a single building. At a later full-cycle event, heirs with different goals can make different choices with their own proceeds.

That does not make a DST liquid or guarantee that every beneficiary will prefer it. It does replace the mechanical indivisibility of one building with units that can be allocated with far more precision.

## Simplifying Estate Administration

A DST is passive and professionally managed. Heirs do not suddenly need to operate the real estate, manage tenants, coordinate repairs, or maintain the property while the estate is being settled. The sponsor and trustee continue operating the property, and distributions may continue.

The estate also holds a defined, valued interest rather than a building that may need appraisal, maintenance, insurance, and a sale under time pressure. A fractional interest can be distributed under the estate plan without a forced sale. Heirs who do not want to continue holding may generally wait for the next full-cycle event rather than trying to liquidate in the middle of a difficult period.

These conveniences do not replace proper estate documents or eliminate investment risk. They can reduce the operational burden placed on heirs.

### Key Takeaways

- Section 1014 generally resets an heir's basis to fair market value at death and may erase deferred 1031 gain.
- Holding a DST until death can leave heirs with little or no income-tax gain on a sale near the stepped-up value.
- Fractional DST interests can be allocated cleanly among multiple heirs, unlike an indivisible building.
- Passive management and defined interests can simplify administration and avoid a forced sale of physical property.

## Coordinating With Your Estate Plan

The potential benefit exists only if the interest is integrated into the estate plan. Title matters. An interest held individually, jointly, in a revocable living trust, or through another entity can pass differently at death, have different probate consequences, and affect how the step-up applies. Beneficiary designations should align with the will or trust so the interest reaches the intended people in the intended proportions.

Community-property rules can affect the extent of the step-up for married couples. Titling can affect creditor exposure and probate. The federal estate tax is separate from the income-tax step-up and can apply to a large estate even if the step-up applies; its exemption threshold can change through legislation. The interaction among deferral, the investor's other assets, state law, and the beneficiaries' goals belongs with the estate attorney and CPA.

> The step-up can be powerful, but it works as intended only when the DST is titled and integrated correctly. Estate planning is work for the attorney and CPA, not something to leave to chance.

## The “Swap Till You Drop” Strategy in Context

“Swap till you drop” is an informal description of successive 1031 exchanges over a lifetime. An investor may exchange out of direct property into DST interests and, when a DST reaches a full-cycle sale, exchange again into another DST or other replacement property. The strategy seeks to defer gain rather than recognize it during life, then rely on the Section 1014 step-up at death.

DSTs can fit the later stages because they are passive. An aging investor can remain invested in real estate without the work of active ownership. But the strategy is not right for everyone. It assumes the investor can tolerate long-term illiquidity and does not need to take cash during life. A lifetime sale or cash received at a full-cycle event can recognize deferred gain and recapture unless another 1031 exchange or a 721 UPREIT is used.

The plan also depends on the availability of current tax law and on the family actually wanting the interest. A tax-efficient structure can still be a poor fit if it leaves heirs with an asset they do not want or cannot hold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the step-up in basis for a DST?

Section 1014 generally resets an owner's basis to fair market value at death. A DST acquired through a 1031 carries a low basis and deferred gain during life; if the interest is held until death and the step-up applies, the heirs' basis may reset to fair market value and the deferred gain may be erased for income-tax purposes. That is the basis of “swap till you drop.” Rules can change, so confirm them with the CPA and estate attorney.

### How does a DST erase deferred capital gains at death?

The DST itself does not erase tax during life. The Section 1014 step-up may reset the inherited interest's low carryover basis to its date-of-death fair market value. That can eliminate the embedded deferred gain and depreciation recapture for income-tax purposes, leaving an heir who sells near that value with little or no gain.

### Can DST interests be divided among multiple heirs?

Yes. Beneficial interests are fractional and can be allocated in the proportions the estate plan specifies, including equal shares, weighted portions, or separate trusts. This can avoid selling a building just to divide it or leaving heirs in contentious co-management. At a full-cycle event, heirs may make their own choices.

### How do DSTs simplify estate administration?

Professional management means heirs do not inherit landlord duties. The DST interest is a defined holding that can be valued and distributed without immediately maintaining, insuring, or selling a physical building. Heirs may generally wait for a full-cycle event rather than scramble to liquidate. Proper estate documents remain necessary.

### What is “swap till you drop”?

It is a long-term strategy of deferring gain through successive 1031 exchanges during life and relying on the Section 1014 step-up at death. DSTs may fit later stages because they are passive. The strategy requires tolerance for illiquidity, no need for lifetime cash-out, continued availability of the tax rules, and a fit with the family's goals.

### Does the step-up apply to depreciation recapture too?

Generally, when a Section 1014 step-up applies, it resets the basis to fair market value and may eliminate both deferred capital gain and recapture embedded in that low basis. Depreciation passed through a DST can reduce basis during life, and a lifetime sale can create recapture. Community-property rules and individual facts can matter, so the CPA and attorney should confirm the current rules.

### Do I need to hold a DST until death to get the step-up?

Yes. The step-up applies at death. If an investor sells a DST interest or takes cash at a full-cycle sale during life, deferred gain and recapture are generally recognized unless the investor defers again through another 1031 exchange or a 721 UPREIT. The benefit is therefore a death-time benefit, and it must be weighed against lifetime liquidity needs.

### How should I title a DST interest for estate planning?

Individual ownership, joint title, a revocable living trust, and other entities can affect transfer at death, probate, the step-up, and creditor exposure. A revocable living trust can help the interest avoid probate and pass under trust terms. Joint title and community-property rules can affect the extent of the step-up. Title and beneficiary designations should match the will or trust and be designed by the estate attorney and CPA.

### Are DST estate benefits guaranteed?

No. Section 1014 and 1031 deferral are current-law features that can change. The interest must be held and titled properly, and the DST itself is illiquid, can lose value, and does not guarantee distributions or returns. Treat the benefits as planning opportunities, not certainties.

### Can heirs continue holding the DST or must they sell?

Heirs generally may continue holding the inherited interest until the next full-cycle event. They can receive distributions while the DST continues. At the eventual sale, an heir may choose another 1031 exchange, a 721 UPREIT, or cash. Given a stepped-up basis, cash may involve little gain measured from that basis, but DST terms and individual facts control.

### How does the step-up interact with estate taxes?

They are different taxes. The Section 1014 step-up is an income-tax-basis rule that may erase deferred capital gain. Federal estate tax is a separate tax that can apply to the value of a large estate despite the step-up, above a changing exemption threshold. The attorney and CPA should plan for both.

### Why are DSTs good for estate planning specifically?

They can combine lifetime 1031 deferral and a potential death-time step-up with passive management, divisible interests, and simpler administration. That can fit a long-horizon investor who can tolerate illiquidity. The benefits depend on holding period, proper planning, and the rules remaining available.

### Can a DST be held in a trust for estate planning?

Generally, yes. A revocable living trust can hold a DST interest, help it avoid probate, and pass it under trust terms while generally preserving the Section 1014 step-up. Irrevocable trusts, trusts for particular beneficiaries, marital planning, and credit-shelter arrangements can have different tax and control consequences, so they should be designed by the attorney and CPA.

## Glossary

- **Step-Up in Basis:** A §1014 reset of basis to fair market value at death.
- **Section 1014:** The code section providing the step-up at death.
- **Fair Market Value:** The date-of-death value used to step up basis.
- **Carryover Basis:** Low basis carried from a relinquished property into a DST.
- **Deferred Gain:** Capital gain postponed through 1031 exchanges.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Gain from prior depreciation, potentially erased at death.
- **Swap Till You Drop:** Deferring through exchanges during life, then relying on the step-up.
- **Beneficial Interest:** A divisible fractional ownership share in a DST.
- **Heirs:** Those who inherit the DST interest at death.
- **Estate Administration:** Settling and distributing a deceased person's assets.
- **Revocable Living Trust:** A trust that can help a DST interest avoid probate.
- **Probate:** The court process of administering an estate.
- **Estate Tax:** A separate tax on large estates, distinct from the step-up.
- **Community Property:** A regime that can affect the extent of the step-up.
- **Full Cycle:** The eventual sale of the DST's property.
- **1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange deferring capital-gains tax.
