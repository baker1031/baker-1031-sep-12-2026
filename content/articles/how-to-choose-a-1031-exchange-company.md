# How to Choose a 1031 Exchange Company

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep seeing the phrase “1031 exchange company” used as if it describes one service. It does not. It can describe the qualified intermediary that holds the money, the advisor who helps an investor find and assess a replacement, or the sponsor that creates the investment. Each role carries a different exposure. Each deserves a different set of questions.

That distinction is more than vocabulary. A qualified intermediary handles the exchange mechanics and custody of proceeds. An advisor or broker-dealer helps evaluate replacement property and coordinate the moving parts. A sponsor acquires property, packages an offering such as a DST, and manages it. This guide covers the three roles, the credentials and fund protections that matter, how to read fees, and the questions and warnings that can separate a careful choice from an expensive one.

First-order thinking asks, “Who can get this exchange done?” Second-order thinking asks who holds the money, who is paid to recommend what, what controls protect the proceeds, and what happens if the primary replacement fails while the calendar is still running. That is the work here. The goal is not a quick answer; it is a team that can withstand scrutiny before the sale closes.

## The three roles hiding behind one label

The phrase “[1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) company” is loose enough to create real confusion. In practice, it can describe three different firms. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html), or QI, is the independent party that holds sale proceeds, prepares exchange documents, and prevents the taxpayer from receiving the money. An advisor or broker-dealer helps decide what to buy, source and evaluate replacement property, and manage deadlines. A sponsor acquires real estate, structures it into a DST or similar investment, and manages it.

The rules and risks differ. There is no national licensing regime for QIs, so the burden of vetting them is heavy. Advisors recommending securities such as DSTs work through a broker-dealer under FINRA and SEC oversight, with suitability duties and often fiduciary-style obligations. Sponsors are judged by their real-estate record, their fee structure, and the assets they choose to assemble. One firm can wear more than one hat, but the standard should follow the function, not the marketing label.

Getting this straight protects an exchanger twice. It makes clear that hiring a QI does not automatically solve the replacement-property question. It also directs the diligence: fund security and track record for a QI; independence and a real suitability process for an advisor; asset quality and fee transparency for a sponsor.

## What a qualified intermediary does — and how to vet one

The QI is the role a deferred exchange cannot do without. An independent party holds the sale proceeds so neither you nor your agent has actual or constructive receipt. The QI prepares the exchange agreement, is assigned into the sale and purchase contracts, receives proceeds into a segregated account at closing, holds them through the [identification period](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html), and releases them to acquire the replacement property. If you or your agent controls the funds, the exchange fails.

The basic QI question is therefore not “What is the fee?” It is “How is my money protected?” Insist on segregated qualified escrow or qualified trust accounts, not an account mixed with the firm’s operating cash. Ask who the custodian is, how accounts are titled, and who may authorize a transfer. Confirm a fidelity bond for employee theft or misappropriation and errors-and-omissions (E&O) insurance for mistakes. Ask for the coverage limits relative to the funds held, how long the firm has operated, its exchange volume, and whether it has had a fund loss.

There have been QIs that misappropriated or lost client funds. That is why a low fee can be a false economy when protections are thin. Look for Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA) membership and adherence to its standards, dual-authorization controls for fund movement, and a plain written explanation of where funds sit and who can move them. [Choosing a qualified intermediary](how-to-choose-a-qualified-intermediary.html) is diligence, not a social test. A QI that will not answer these questions clearly is giving you useful information.

## What an advisor does — and how to vet one

The advisor addresses the decisions a QI does not make. Often through a registered broker-dealer or RIA, an advisor can estimate the deferral, map goals to replacement choices, source and screen property, conduct or guide diligence, and coordinate the QI, CPA, and [attorney](role-of-your-attorney-in-a-1031-exchange.html) against the deadlines. When the replacement is a security, such as a DST, the advisor must be properly licensed and conduct a suitability analysis for the investor’s finances and objectives.

Check both the advisor and firm on FINRA BrokerCheck and the SEC’s Investment Adviser Public Disclosure database. Ask whether the advisor is independent and can recommend offerings from many sponsors, or captive to one sponsor’s products. Ask how the advisor is paid and request the answer in writing. Ask to see the diligence process: how sponsors are evaluated, how projections are stress-tested, and how risks are disclosed. A useful advisor explains trade-offs and will say no to an unsuitable idea. A weak one tends to pitch what pays best.

Exchange experience matters because the deadlines make this work different from ordinary investing. Look for someone who has guided many exchanges through closing, routinely builds a backup such as a fast-closing DST, and coordinates smoothly with QIs and CPAs. Past-client references and a record of reaching the 180-day line tell an investor more than a brochure.

## What a sponsor does — and how to vet one

A sponsor creates the investment. In a DST, the sponsor acquires the underlying real estate, arranges financing, structures the trust, and manages the property through an eventual sale. Buying a DST interest means relying heavily on the sponsor’s acquisition judgment, operating ability, financing choices, and candor about fees and reporting. The sponsor deserves as much attention as the property.

Ask how long the sponsor has operated, how many offerings have completed a full cycle from acquisition through sale, and what realized returns were—not only projections. Examine the “load”: acquisition fees, ongoing management fees, disposition fees, and the portion of each invested dollar that reaches the real estate. Alignment can include sponsor capital invested alongside investors. Read the [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html), especially its risk factors, because it governs the offering.

The underlying real estate remains the base case. Strong sponsors may use well-located apartments, industrial, medical office, or necessity retail, with conservative leverage and creditworthy tenants. Aggressive projections, thin reserves, high leverage, and assets in declining markets call for greater caution. An advisor can screen sponsors and offerings, but the investor still needs to understand the sponsor and why the offering is considered sound before sending exchange proceeds.

## Credentials and experience to look for

Across the three roles, credentials can help separate a serious operator from a casual participant. For QIs, FEA membership, the Certified Exchange Specialist designation, and written fund-security standards are meaningful. For advisors, current FINRA and/or SEC registration, a clean BrokerCheck/IAPD record, and relevant designations indicate accountability. For sponsors, a multi-decade history, many full-cycle offerings, and experience through at least one real-estate downturn offer evidence that is more useful than a polished forecast.

Specific 1031 experience is the thread that runs through all of them. The deadlines and constructive-receipt rules are unforgiving. Ask how many exchanges or offerings the candidate has handled, how recently, and what it does when something goes wrong. Specific answers generally come from firms that work in this area every day; vague answers often come from firms that do not.

Do not skip reputation. Ask for clients or professionals who have worked with the firm and call them. Search for regulatory actions, complaints, and litigation. In a transaction where a bad actor can cost both proceeds and deferral, a few hours of reference checking is inexpensive insurance.

## Fee transparency

Every role has its own fee model. A QI commonly charges a modest flat fee per exchange—often a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars—and sometimes a small charge for each additional property. Ask how the QI treats interest earned on held funds. A reputable firm explains it plainly. The QI fee should be straightforward; if it is not, ask why.

Advisor and sponsor compensation is where the larger dollars can accumulate. For a DST or another security, commissions and the total offering load—the share consumed by selling costs, sponsor fees, and reserves before capital reaches the real estate—are disclosed in the PPM. Ask an advisor whether compensation changes among available products. A difference can create a conflict that belongs in the open. The aim is not zero fees; it is knowing what you pay, to whom, and what service or exposure comes with it.

Evasiveness about compensation is a warning. A trustworthy QI, advisor, or sponsor should explain its fees, compare them with alternatives, and let you weigh cost against value. If “How exactly do you get paid?” does not produce a clear answer, keep looking.

## Independence vs. captive recommendations

An advisor may be independent or captive. An independent advisor can recommend offerings from many sponsors and choose from a broader menu. A captive advisor is tied to one sponsor or a narrow proprietary shelf and is structurally inclined to recommend that inventory. Neither arrangement automatically ends the discussion. It does mean the relationship, its conflicts, and its disclosures must be understood.

This matters most when an exchange deadline applies pressure. A broad sponsor network can supply diversified options and a dependable backup. A limited shelf can offer only what is available that month. Ask directly: “How many sponsors can you place my money with, and how do you decide what to recommend?” The answer shows whether the investor’s needs or the firm’s inventory drive the choice.

The same test applies to a firm that is both advisor and sponsor. Recommending an affiliated product can be appropriate in some circumstances, but it creates a conflict. Ask how it is disclosed and managed, and make sure suitability is being assessed rather than assumed. Transparency is not a guarantee, but its absence is a poor sign.

## Questions to ask before you hire

These questions work well alongside [a full real estate investor’s 1031 exchange checklist](the-real-estate-investors-1031-exchange-checklist.html):

- Which role do you play—qualified intermediary, advisor, sponsor, or more than one—and how do you manage conflicts?
- If you are the QI, where exactly are my funds held, in whose name, who authorizes movement, and what bonding and E&O coverage protects them?
- Are you an FEA member, and have you ever had a fund loss?
- If you are an advisor, what are your and your firm’s registrations, and can I verify them through BrokerCheck or IAPD?
- How many sponsors can you place my money with, and how exactly are you paid across those options?
- If you are a sponsor, how many offerings have completed a full cycle, and what were the actual realized returns rather than projections?
- How many 1031 exchanges have you handled, and how do you build in a backup if a deal stalls?

Specificity and comfort with these questions matter. The answers are part of the diligence.

## Red flags to walk away from

Some warnings justify ending the conversation. A QI that is vague about fund location or control is disqualifying because the money may be irreplaceable if lost. Reluctance to disclose compensation suggests a conflict not being shown. Pressure to commit quickly, particularly to one product, takes advantage of deadline anxiety. A trustworthy firm helps an exchanger slow down enough to understand the decision.

Other warnings are less obvious but still consequential: an advisor who only recommends one sponsor’s products or cannot explain the risks; a sponsor relying on aggressive projections, thin reserves, high leverage, or property in a weak market; a firm with regulatory disclosures it refuses to discuss; or resistance to reference requests. The common principle is simple. When both funds and a tax deferral are exposed, missing transparency is itself an answer.

### Key takeaways

- “1031 company” can mean a QI, advisor, or sponsor. Judge each on its own standards.
- For a QI, segregated escrow, fidelity bonding, and E&O coverage outweigh the appeal of the lowest price.
- For an advisor, verify licensing, value independence, and insist on written fee disclosure.
- Walk away from evasiveness about fund security or fees, pressure to commit, and single-product bias.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a “1031 exchange company” actually do?

The phrase covers three roles: a QI holds proceeds and handles the mechanics; an advisor helps find and evaluate replacement property; and a sponsor creates investments such as DSTs. They face different rules and risks, so each should be judged by its actual function rather than its label.

### Is a qualified intermediary required?

Yes. A deferred exchange requires an independent QI to hold proceeds so the taxpayer does not receive them. If the taxpayer or agent controls the funds, the exchange is disqualified. That makes the QI’s integrity and solvency central.

### How do I know my exchange funds are safe with a QI?

Require segregated qualified escrow or trust accounts rather than commingling with the firm’s cash. Confirm adequate fidelity bonding and E&O insurance, identify the custodian and authorized movers of funds, and look for FEA membership. Evasiveness is a reason to choose another QI.

### How is an advisor different from a qualified intermediary?

The QI holds money and documents the exchange. The advisor helps decide what to buy, screen replacement property, conduct suitability for securities such as DSTs, and coordinate the team against deadlines. Many investors need a QI and CPA, while an advisor can be valuable before the clock begins.

### Should I use an independent advisor or a captive one?

An independent advisor can recommend across many sponsors. A captive advisor is limited to one sponsor’s products and has a built-in recommendation bias. Neither is automatically unacceptable, but the arrangement and its conflict disclosures should be clear.

### How are these firms paid?

A QI commonly charges a modest flat fee. Advisors and sponsors may receive commissions and an offering load disclosed in the PPM. Ask each firm directly, get the answer in writing, and ask whether advisor compensation changes across products.

### What credentials should I look for?

For QIs, look for FEA membership and the CES designation. For advisors, look for current FINRA/SEC registration and a clean BrokerCheck/IAPD record. For sponsors, look for a long record of full-cycle offerings through a downturn. In every case, deep 1031 experience matters.

### How do I evaluate a DST sponsor?

Ask about operating history, full-cycle offerings, and actual realized returns rather than projections. Examine the load, look for sponsor co-investment, read PPM risk factors, and assess property quality and leverage. An advisor can screen sponsors, but the investor should still know who is running the offering.

### Can one firm play more than one role?

Yes. A firm may be advisor and sponsor, or offer QI services alongside advice. That is not inherently wrong, but it creates conflicts. Ask how they are disclosed and managed and make sure suitability does not become a path to the firm’s own product every time.

### What are the biggest red flags?

Major warnings include vagueness about fund custody or control, reluctance to disclose compensation, pressure to commit to one product, an advisor who only recommends one sponsor, and regulatory disclosures the firm will not discuss. Treat a lack of transparency as meaningful when proceeds and deferral are at risk.

### How much should a 1031 exchange cost?

QI fees are generally modest and flat. Larger costs can be commissions and load on securities such as DSTs, disclosed in offering documents. The objective is not the lowest fee; it is understanding every cost and preserving fund security.

### Do I really need all three—a QI, advisor, and sponsor?

You always need a QI. You need a sponsor only if you buy a sponsored product such as a DST. An advisor is optional but can source replacements, perform suitability work, create a backup, and coordinate deadlines. Hiring only a QI and searching later can leave an investor under time pressure.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds proceeds and documents an exchange so the taxpayer does not receive them.
- **Advisor:** A licensed professional who helps source and evaluate replacement property and coordinate the exchange team.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that acquires real estate, structures it into a DST or similar offering, and manages it.
- **Broker-Dealer:** A firm registered to offer securities such as DSTs; Baker 1031’s is Aurora Securities, member FINRA/SIPC.
- **Federation of Exchange Accommodators (FEA):** The trade body that sets standards and certification for QIs.
- **Certified Exchange Specialist (CES):** A designation for experienced QI practitioners.
- **Qualified Escrow Account:** A restricted, segregated account where exchange funds are held beyond the taxpayer’s reach.
- **Fidelity Bond:** Insurance protecting client funds against misappropriation by a firm or its employees.
- **Errors-and-Omissions (E&O) Insurance:** Coverage for mistakes in handling an exchange.
- **BrokerCheck / IAPD:** FINRA and SEC databases for verifying registrations and disclosures.
- **Suitability Review:** The assessment that a security such as a DST fits a particular investor.
- **Load:** Selling costs, sponsor fees, and reserves consumed before invested dollars reach the real estate.
- **Private Placement Memorandum (PPM):** The disclosure document governing a DST or other private offering.
- **Captive Advisor:** An advisor limited to one sponsor’s products, creating a structural recommendation bias.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Control over exchange proceeds that disqualifies the exchange; a QI exists to prevent it.
- **Full-Cycle Offering:** A sponsored investment completed from acquisition through sale, with realized rather than projected returns.
