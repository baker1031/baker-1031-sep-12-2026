# 7 Legal Ways to Avoid Capital Gains Tax on Investment Property

**Capital Gains · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read**

I have been thinking about how quickly a profitable property sale becomes a tax-planning decision. The offer price gets attention first. The combined federal, recapture, NIIT, and state bill is often what changes the economics.

Sell investment property at a profit and the IRS takes a share, often a large one. Real estate is unusual in the number of legal provisions available to defer that bill, reduce it, or, in a few circumstances, eliminate it. This article covers seven of them, what each does, who it can fit, and the catch attached to it. It is educational, not advice. Review the specifics with your own CPA and attorney before acting.

First-order thinking says, “avoid the tax.” Second-order thinking starts with a more useful question: what do you actually want the capital to do next, and what restrictions, deadlines, liquidity needs, and risks arrive with the tax choice? Most of these tools defer tax. A couple reduce it. Two can eliminate gain on future growth or at death. None is a loophole; each is a deliberate provision with limits and trade-offs.

- The 1031 exchange is the workhorse for remaining in real estate, and a DST is its passive version for an investor done with toilets and tenants.
- Opportunity Zone funds can accept any capital gain, defer it, and eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation after a ten-year hold.
- An installment sale spreads gain across years, but depreciation recapture is still generally taxed in the year of sale.
- The long-hold endgame is a step-up in basis at death: hold, exchange, and allow heirs to inherit at fair market value with the gain erased.

## Why the gain gets taxed so hard

Investment real estate held for more than a year produces long-term capital gain. Federally, it is taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% based on taxable income. In 2026, the 0% rate runs to roughly $49,450 of taxable income for single filers and about $98,900 for married couples filing jointly. The 20% rate begins above roughly $545,500 single and $613,700 joint. Most meaningful property sales fall into the 15% band, though a large sale can push part of the gain into 20%.

That is rarely the whole bill. Depreciation deductions are generally recaptured as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at a rate up to 25%. The net investment income tax adds 3.8% once modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 for a single filer or $250,000 for a joint filer. Most states tax the gain again, from a few percent to north of ten in the highest-tax states. In combination, federal capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax can take roughly a quarter to a third of the profit.

For the mechanics, see [how capital gains tax on real estate works](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html) and [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html).

## Defer, reduce, or eliminate

Keep the categories straight. Deferral postpones tax; the gain remains on the books and may be carried for decades. Reduction can smooth gain into lower tax brackets or more years. Elimination is narrower: an Opportunity Zone fund can eliminate tax on its own appreciation after ten years, and a step-up at death can eliminate built-in gain and deferred recapture for heirs.

Congress wrote these provisions to encourage reinvestment, charitable giving, or long-term holding. The choice should follow the goal. Do you want to keep owning real estate, leave active management, diversify, receive income, or pass wealth to children? The size of the tax bill is important, but it should not pick the vehicle by itself.

## 1. The 1031 exchange

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) lets an owner sell investment real estate and defer capital gains tax, depreciation recapture, and the NIIT by reinvesting into like-kind replacement property. Like-kind is broad for real estate: an apartment building can become raw land, a rental house can become a retail strip, and a duplex can become a share of a warehouse. The gain does not vanish. It carries into the basis of the replacement property until a sale without a further exchange, or until death.

The rules are strict. From the sale closing, there are 45 days to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days to close. Those clocks run at the same time; the 180 days is not a new window after day 45. Proceeds must pass through a qualified intermediary, never the seller's own bank account. To defer the full tax, an investor generally buys equal or greater value and replaces paid-off debt, or supplies fresh cash for the difference. Cash retained or debt not replaced is boot and is taxable.

This fits an owner who wants to remain invested in real estate and compound pre-tax capital. Its price is deadline discipline.

## 2. Delaware Statutory Trust (a passive 1031)

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html), or DST, is a way to complete a 1031 exchange without becoming a landlord again. The IRS recognizes a properly structured DST interest as like-kind replacement property, so it can defer the same capital gains, recapture, and NIIT as a direct exchange. Instead of directly owning and operating property, the investor holds a fractional, professionally managed beneficial interest in institutional real estate, often a large multifamily, industrial, or net-lease asset. The sponsor makes operating decisions. There are no tenants, repairs, or 3 a.m. calls for the investor.

Two details often matter. A DST can absorb an exact dollar amount, which can solve leftover 1031 equity that a direct purchase cannot use to the penny. Prepackaged DST offerings can also be identified and closed quickly, easing the 45-day identification problem. A DST is a grantor trust, so an investor generally receives a Form 1099 and a grantor letter rather than a K-1.

The trade-offs remain real. DSTs are illiquid, speculative securities sold only to accredited investors through a private placement memorandum. The investor gives up control to the sponsor and can lose principal. It can fit someone who wants 1031 deferral but is finished with active management.

## 3. Opportunity Zone fund

An [Opportunity Zone fund](opportunity-zones-guide.html) can take any capital gain, not only real-estate gain. A gain from a building, stock portfolio, business, or crypto position can be invested—though only the taxable gain, not the entire sale proceeds—into a qualified opportunity fund within 180 days. That differs from a 1031 exchange, where the whole sale price must be reinvested. The original basis remains available for other uses. If the fund is held for at least ten years, appreciation inside the fund can be permanently tax-free on exit.

Timing matters in 2026. The 2025 tax law made the program permanent beginning January 1, 2027, with a rolling five-year deferral and a single 10% basis step-up for gains invested under the new rules. Investments under the original program still recognize deferred gain on December 31, 2026, the longstanding sunset date. The ten-year benefit for fund appreciation survives in both versions.

This can fit a large gain of any kind and a genuine decade-long horizon, provided the investor accepts development and lease-up risk. Opportunity Zone funds are private placements, with the illiquidity and principal risk that entails.

## 4. Installment sale (Section 453)

An [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) under Section 453 spreads gain, and the tax on it, across the years in which the seller receives payments rather than recognizing it all in the sale year. The seller becomes the lender; the buyer pays over time, and a slice of gain is reported with each payment.

The appeal is bracket management. A single large gain can put part of income in the 20% capital-gains band, trigger the 3.8% NIIT, and expand the state tax hit in one year. Spreading the same gain across five or ten years may keep more of it in the 15% band, keep income below NIIT thresholds, and soften state tax.

There are two important cautions. An installment sale does not defer depreciation recapture. Section 1250 recapture is generally taxed in full in the year of sale even if most cash has not been collected, potentially leaving a first-year tax bill larger than first-year cash. And the seller has become the bank: buyer default can leave the seller pursuing a property no longer fully under the seller's control.

An installment sale can fit someone comfortable carrying paper for a trusted buyer when bracket smoothing is meaningful. It is simpler and more limited than an exchange, but it can finance one part of a sale while the rest is exchanged.

## 5. Section 1033 involuntary conversion

[Section 1033](section-1033-involuntary-conversion.html) applies when a sale is forced. If property is condemned, taken by eminent domain, or destroyed by fire, flood, or storm, gain on insurance or condemnation proceeds can be deferred by reinvesting in similar property.

It is in some ways friendlier than a 1031. The replacement window is generally two years from the end of the tax year in which gain is realized, and three years for real property taken by condemnation, rather than 1031's 180 days. A qualified intermediary is not required; the owner can receive proceeds directly and still reinvest in time.

The similar-use standard is generally stricter than the broad like-kind standard. Only proceeds need be reinvested; any amount retained is taxable gain. This is not a strategy to manufacture. It is the provision to use when government action or casualty causes a sale that was not wanted, so a forced event does not become a forced tax bill.

## 6. Section 121 primary-residence exclusion

The [Section 121 rules for 1031 exchanges and a primary residence](1031-exchange-primary-residence.html) concern a home, not a rental, but matter when an investor has lived in or may move into a property that was rented. Section 121 can exclude up to $250,000 of gain for a single filer or $500,000 for married taxpayers filing jointly when the seller has owned and used the property as a main home for at least two of the last five years. The two years need not be continuous, and a qualifying homeowner can generally use the exclusion every two years.

The rental conversion details matter. A former rental that becomes a primary residence does not receive the exclusion on all gain: the exclusion is reduced proportionally for periods of nonqualified use, the years it was a rental rather than a home. Depreciation claimed during rental use is still recaptured and taxed. Section 121 fits a true primary-home sale and can partly shelter a converted rental, but it never shelters recapture.

## 7. Hold and step up at death

The most complete elimination is simple in theory: do not sell. If appreciated real estate, DST interests, or OP-unit interests are held until death, heirs generally receive a basis stepped up to fair market value at the date of death. The built-in capital gain and deferred depreciation recapture are erased. An heir who sells soon after inheriting generally owes little or no capital-gains tax because the basis has reset.

Paired with serial 1031 exchanges during life, this is often called “swap till you drop.” An investor exchanges from property to property, leaves the final holding at death, and the step-up clears the deferred gain so heirs begin fresh. It is part of broader [estate planning for real estate](estate-planning-real-estate-guide.html).

It is not a free lunch. A large estate can face estate tax, and Congress periodically debates basis-at-death rules. This path needs real planning and suits a legacy-focused owner who does not need to liquidate.

## A bonus: the charitable remainder trust

A charitable remainder trust, or CRT, earns a place even though the headline has seven strategies. An investor can contribute appreciated property to an irrevocable trust, which can sell it without immediate capital-gains tax, pay the donor an income stream for life or for a term of years, and leave the remainder to a named charity. The donor receives an upfront income-tax deduction for the present value of the eventual charitable gift.

This is for the charitably inclined investor who values income and tax efficiency together. The remainder is given away, and the trust is irrevocable. That makes it a serious commitment for an attorney and CPA, not a checklist item.

## How to choose among them

Start with the desired outcome, not the tax. Want to stay in real estate and keep capital working pre-tax? A 1031 exchange. Want that deferral but no more active management? A DST. Have a large gain of any kind and accept a ten-year, illiquid development-risk investment? An Opportunity Zone fund, while paying attention to 2026 and 2027 timing. Want to finance a trusted buyer and smooth brackets? An installment sale, remembering recapture comes due up front. Facing condemnation or destruction? Section 1033. Selling the home actually lived in? Section 121. Focused on legacy and able to hold? The step-up at death, potentially after a lifetime of exchanges.

Strategies can combine. A rental-property gain can go through a 1031 while a separate stock gain goes into an Opportunity Zone fund. An investor can 1031 into a DST and later move into a REIT. A seller can finance part of a sale by installment while exchanging the rest. The facts matter: basis, debt, brackets, state, timeline, and heirs. Work through the deeper guides and choose with a CPA and attorney, not a summary table alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the best way to avoid capital gains tax on investment property?

There is no single best method. A 1031 exchange or a DST is the usual route to defer real-estate gain when an investor wants to remain invested. An Opportunity Zone fund can eliminate tax on future fund appreciation after ten years. Holding until death can give heirs a stepped-up basis that erases gain. Match the method to whether the goal is continued ownership, less management, diversification, or legacy.

### Can I avoid capital gains tax entirely, not just defer it?

Sometimes. An Opportunity Zone fund can eliminate tax on its own appreciation after ten years. A basis step-up at death can erase deferred gain and depreciation recapture for heirs. Section 121 can exclude up to $250,000 for a single filer or $500,000 for joint filers on a qualifying primary home. Most other methods defer rather than eliminate tax.

### Does an installment sale defer depreciation recapture too?

No. The capital gain can be reported as payments arrive, but Section 1250 depreciation recapture is generally taxed in full in the sale year. It can leave a recapture bill larger than the first year's cash, so plan for it before structuring the sale.

### Can I use the home-sale exclusion on a rental property?

Only partly, and only after making it a primary residence and living there at least two of the five years before sale. The exclusion is reduced for nonqualified-use rental years. Depreciation taken during rental use is still recaptured and taxed. Section 121 is for a home, not an investment property.

### What happens to Opportunity Zone investments in 2026?

Gains invested under the original program recognize deferred tax on December 31, 2026. Starting January 1, 2027, the permanent program uses a rolling five-year deferral and a single 10% basis step-up for new investments. The ten-year benefit for fund appreciation remains under both versions.

### Is a DST different from a 1031 exchange?

A DST is a passive way to complete a 1031. A properly structured DST interest is like-kind replacement property, so it defers capital gains, recapture, and NIIT while the investor owns a managed fractional interest rather than buying and running property directly. It generally reports on a 1099 and grantor letter rather than a K-1.

### How much does capital gains tax actually cost on a sale?

It layers. Long-term federal rates are 0%, 15%, or 20%; unrecaptured Section 1250 gain can be taxed up to 25%; NIIT is 3.8% above $200,000 single or $250,000 joint; and states commonly add tax. The combined cost can be roughly a quarter to a third of profit.

### Can these strategies be combined?

Yes. A real-estate gain can be exchanged through a 1031 while a separate stock gain is invested in an Opportunity Zone fund. A 1031 can go into a DST and later move into a REIT. A sale can be partly financed by installment and partly exchanged. Use a CPA and attorney because dollar requirements and deadlines interact.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange of investment real estate under Section 1031 that defers capital gains tax, depreciation recapture, and NIIT, and can be repeated indefinitely.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** An IRS-recognized form of like-kind replacement property that lets an investor complete a 1031 exchange passively by owning a fractional interest in institutional real estate.

**Opportunity Zone Fund:** A qualified fund that accepts any capital gain, defers it, and can eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation after a ten-year hold.

**Installment Sale:** A sale under Section 453 that spreads the capital gain, and the tax, across the years payments are received, though recapture is taxed up front.

**Section 1033 Involuntary Conversion:** A deferral of gain when property is condemned, taken by eminent domain, or destroyed, by reinvesting proceeds in similar property within an extended window.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of an asset's basis to fair market value at the owner's death, which can eliminate deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture for heirs.

**Section 121 Exclusion:** The exclusion of up to $250,000 (single) or $500,000 (joint) of gain on a primary residence owned and used as a main home for two of the last five years.

**Depreciation Recapture:** The portion of gain attributable to prior depreciation deductions, taxed as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at a rate up to 25%.
