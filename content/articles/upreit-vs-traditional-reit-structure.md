# UPREIT vs. Traditional REIT Structure

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I find that the ownership chart behind a REIT usually seems unimportant—until a property owner wants to contribute appreciated real estate instead of selling it. Then the chart becomes the tax mechanism.

A traditional REIT owns properties directly. An UPREIT, or Umbrella Partnership REIT, owns them through an operating partnership. That intermediate partnership permits an outside owner to contribute real estate for OP units under Section 721, generally deferring capital gain. A traditional REIT has no equivalent partnership layer, so its property purchase is generally a taxable sale to the owner.

The distinction explains DST-to-721 roll-ups, OP-unit taxation, and why most large modern REITs use the UPREIT model. It also explains why an OP unitholder and an ordinary shareholder can have similar economics but different tax positions. This is educational information, not investment, tax, or legal advice; current rules and the investor’s facts need confirmation with a tax advisor.

## The traditional REIT structure

A traditional REIT holds title to its apartment buildings, warehouses, shopping centers, or other income-producing properties directly on its own balance sheet. Investors own shares of the REIT, and the REIT owns the buildings. There is no partnership layer between shareholders and real estate.

The ownership chain is direct: the REIT owns property; shareholders own the REIT. When a traditional REIT acquires a building, it generally pays cash or stock, and the seller recognizes capital gain on the sale. The direct structure is simple, clean, and common among older or smaller REITs. It does not create a partnership into which an owner can contribute property tax-deferred.

## The UPREIT structure

An [UPREIT](721-exchange-guide.html) holds real estate through an operating partnership, or OP. The REIT owns an interest in the OP and typically serves as its general partner and majority owner, controlling it and consolidating it for financial reporting. The OP holds the actual buildings.

The chain is therefore shareholders → REIT → operating partnership → properties. Outside partners can also own interests in the OP. Those limited partners hold [OP units](op-units-explained.html), which are partnership interests rather than REIT stock.

OP units are generally designed to track the economics of REIT shares: distributions normally correspond to the REIT’s dividends, and units are typically convertible to shares. They remain legally distinct. That legal distinction is the feature that enables a Section 721 contribution. Most large, modern REITs use the UPREIT structure because the operating partnership makes acquisitions from tax-sensitive owners possible.

> The operating-partnership layer is the point of an UPREIT. It lets an owner contribute real estate for partnership units tax-deferred—something a traditional direct-owning REIT cannot do.

## Why UPREITs enable 721 transactions

Section 721 generally provides that a property owner who contributes real property to a partnership for partnership interests does not recognize gain on the contribution. An UPREIT has exactly the required partnership: the operating partnership.

An owner can contribute a building to that OP and receive OP units. The gain is deferred. A traditional REIT owns assets directly, so it has no operating partnership to accept the contribution; purchasing the property from the owner is generally a taxable sale.

This is also the mechanism behind a DST-to-721 roll-up. An investor may first complete a 1031 exchange into a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html). Later, when an UPREIT acquires the DST property, the DST interest can be contributed to the OP for OP units under Section 721. The deferral runs through direct property, DST, and OP units.

Conversion does not continue deferral. Converting OP units to REIT shares is generally a taxable disposition of the partnership interest. Deferral lasts while the investor holds the units, not after conversion or another taxable sale.

## Investor implications: units versus shares

An OP unitholder has contributed property under Section 721. The unitholder owns a partnership interest, carries deferred capital gain, receives distributions designed to track the REIT’s dividends, and may receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death. Holding until death can generally eliminate the deferred gain for heirs under current law.

An ordinary shareholder simply owns REIT shares. The shareholder did not contribute property and therefore has no deferred gain from a 721 transaction. The shareholder receives the standard REIT experience: dividends, mostly ordinary income with a potential 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends, liquidity where the REIT is traded, and a step-up in the shares themselves at death.

The two ownership types may receive similar cash distributions, but the tax treatment differs. OP-unit holders receive Schedule K-1 reporting; shareholders generally receive Form 1099-DIV. OP units are commonly subject to a holding period before conversion, are generally illiquid until conversion, and conversion is generally taxable. Similar economics do not mean the two positions are legally or tax-wise interchangeable.

## UPREIT versus DownREIT

A DownREIT can also use partnerships to accept tax-deferred property contributions, but it does so differently. An UPREIT uses one operating partnership directly below the REIT and places all or most of the portfolio in that entity. A DownREIT may own some property directly and participate in one or more lower-tier partnerships holding particular contributed properties.

The DownREIT’s multiple partnerships can have different partners and terms, making the accounting, tax analysis, and owner rights more complicated. Both structures can facilitate Section 721-style contributions. The UPREIT’s single umbrella partnership is simpler and far more common; DownREITs are generally used for specific joint ventures or property-specific transactions where one operating partnership does not fit.

## What to evaluate in an UPREIT offering

Structure alone is not enough. An investor considering a 721 contribution—frequently as the back end of a DST-to-721 roll-up—should first evaluate the REIT’s portfolio and management. What does the OP own? How diversified is it? Which sectors and markets does it favor? What is the sponsor’s track record? The OP units eventually link the investor to the whole REIT, not only the contributed property.

Review the unit terms: distribution rate relative to the REIT dividend, any holding period before conversion, conversion mechanics, and treatment upon an acquisition or restructuring of the REIT. Evaluate liquidity. OP units are generally illiquid until converted, and conversion is taxable. Finally, weigh the loss of property-specific control: the owner trades one building for a smaller interest in a large, professionally managed portfolio.

First-order thinking focuses on the Section 721 deferral. Second-order thinking asks whether the REIT’s portfolio, sponsor, unit agreement, liquidity limits, tax cost of conversion, and loss of control justify that deferral for the owner’s time horizon.

## Key Takeaways

- A traditional REIT owns property directly; an UPREIT owns property through an operating partnership controlled by the REIT.
- The UPREIT’s operating partnership enables Section 721 contributions for OP units, which a traditional direct-owning REIT cannot offer.
- OP-unit holders have deferred gain, K-1 reporting, conversion restrictions, and a possible step-up at death; ordinary shareholders have standard dividend-and-share treatment, including potential Section 199A treatment.
- OP-unit conversion to shares is generally taxable. UPREIT due diligence must cover the whole REIT, unit terms, liquidity, conversion, and loss of control.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between an UPREIT and a traditional REIT?

A traditional REIT holds title to its real estate directly, with shareholders owning the REIT and no partnership in between. An UPREIT owns assets through an operating partnership; the REIT is the general partner and majority owner, and outside investors may hold OP units. The partnership layer allows tax-deferred contributions under Section 721, which a traditional REIT cannot offer.

### What does UPREIT stand for?

It means Umbrella Partnership Real Estate Investment Trust. The operating partnership is the umbrella beneath the REIT and above the properties. The REIT controls the OP as general partner and majority owner; outside partners can hold limited-partnership OP units. The UPREIT model became popular in the 1990s because it lets property owners contribute assets for units without current capital-gain recognition.

### Why do UPREITs enable 721 exchanges?

Section 721 applies when property is contributed to a partnership for partnership interests. The UPREIT’s OP is that partnership. A contributor can receive OP units and defer gain. A traditional REIT has no partnership and therefore acquires the asset in a taxable cash-or-stock sale. Units can later convert to shares, but that conversion is generally taxable.

### What are OP units, and how do they differ from REIT shares?

OP units are partnership interests in the operating partnership; REIT shares are corporate securities. Units generally receive distributions that track REIT dividends and can typically be converted to shares. An OP unitholder carries the deferred gain from the original Section 721 contribution, receives a K-1, and generally triggers tax when converting. A shareholder receives a 1099-DIV and has no 721 deferred gain.

### What is a DownREIT and how is it different from an UPREIT?

A DownREIT uses lower-tier partnerships, sometimes alongside directly held REIT property, to accept contributed assets. An UPREIT has one portfolio-wide operating partnership. Both can facilitate tax-deferred contributions, but a DownREIT’s multiple partnerships make its terms, accounting, and tax analysis more complex. The UPREIT is the more common structure.

### Is converting OP units to REIT shares taxable?

Generally yes. The original Section 721 contribution defers gain while OP units are held. Conversion or redemption of units is generally a disposition of the partnership interest and triggers deferred gain. Investors may hold units for distributions and deferral or convert only when they accept the tax and need liquidity. Confirm transaction mechanics with a tax advisor.

### How does a DST-to-721 roll-up work?

First, an investor exchanges appreciated real estate into a DST through a 1031 exchange. Later, an UPREIT acquires the DST property, and the investor’s DST interest becomes OP units in a Section 721 contribution. Both stages generally defer gain. The investor can then hold units for distributions and potential step-up or later convert to shares in a taxable transaction. Timing and documents depend on the DST and REIT.

### Why are most large REITs UPREITs?

The operating partnership lets a REIT acquire property from tax-sensitive owners without requiring them to recognize an immediate gain. A traditional REIT can generally offer cash or stock in a taxable sale. The UPREIT can offer OP units under Section 721, making it a more attractive and competitive acquirer of properties. This deal-making advantage is why large modern REITs often use the model.

### Do OP unitholders get REIT dividends?

They receive operating-partnership distributions designed to track the REIT’s per-share dividends. The cash economics are similar, but the tax reporting differs. The partnership reports through a K-1, and distribution treatment reflects partnership income character and the unitholder’s basis rather than ordinary shareholder dividend reporting.

### What is the step-up benefit for OP unitholders?

If an investor holds OP units until death, heirs generally receive a basis equal to fair market value at death under current rules. The step-up can erase the deferred capital gain carried in the units. This is sometimes described as applying a defer-until-death approach through the 1031, DST, and 721 sequence. Estate and tax advisors should confirm current law and the investor’s circumstances.

### Are OP units liquid?

Generally no. Units are partnership interests, not exchange-listed securities. The typical liquidity path is conversion to REIT shares after an applicable holding period, but conversion is generally taxable. Until then, units are longer-term and illiquid, so they are not appropriate for an owner who needs ready access to capital.

### Can any property owner contribute to an UPREIT?

No. The REIT must want the specific property and agree to a contribution that fits its portfolio strategy and standards. In practice, many individuals reach an UPREIT through a DST-to-721 path rather than a direct property contribution. Suitability requirements also apply; opportunities are commonly for accredited or otherwise suitable investors through a broker-dealer.

### What are the risks of holding OP units?

Risks include illiquidity; the taxable cost of conversion; dependence on the full REIT portfolio, management, values, dividends, and share price; K-1 and conversion tax complexity; lost control of a specific property; and potential tax effects from a merger, change of control, or restructuring. Deferral is valuable, but it does not eliminate these risks.

### Is an UPREIT contribution right for me?

It may fit an owner of appreciated real estate or a DST interest who wants to defer gain, move to passive diversified management, does not need near-term liquidity, and values a potential step-up for heirs. It can fit poorly for someone who wants direct control, needs liquidity soon, or simply wants liquid REIT exposure with new capital. The decision depends on tax position, time horizon, estate goals, and suitability review with a CPA and attorney.

## Glossary

**Traditional REIT:** A REIT that owns real estate directly.

**UPREIT:** An umbrella partnership REIT that owns property through an operating partnership.

**Operating Partnership (OP):** The partnership beneath an UPREIT that holds real estate.

**OP Units:** Partnership interests received in a Section 721 contribution and generally convertible to shares.

**General Partner:** The REIT’s controlling role in the operating partnership.

**Section 721:** The provision allowing a tax-deferred property contribution to a partnership.

**721 / UPREIT Exchange:** A property contribution to an OP for OP units, generally deferring gain.

**DownREIT:** A REIT using lower-tier partnerships rather than one umbrella OP.

**DST-to-721 Roll-Up:** The path from a 1031 DST into OP units through an UPREIT contribution.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A fractional 1031-eligible real-estate interest, often the front end of a 721 roll-up.

**Conversion:** A generally taxable exchange of OP units for REIT shares.

**Step-Up in Basis:** Basis adjustment at death that can erase deferred gain in OP units under current rules.

**Limited Partner:** An outside OP-unit owner who does not control the REIT.

**Section 199A Deduction:** The potential 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

**Schedule K-1:** Partnership tax form received by OP unitholders.

**Suitability Review:** Review of whether a 721, UPREIT, or DST opportunity fits an investor.

## Which should you choose?

This is less a choice between two security types than a question of transaction mechanics. The UPREIT operating-partnership structure is what makes a 721 exchange possible. If the owner’s goal is to move appreciated real estate into a REIT while deferring gain, the owner needs an UPREIT willing to accept a Section 721 contribution—and must remember conversion to shares is generally taxable.
