# 1031 vs. DST vs. Opportunity Zone vs. 721: Which Fits Your Gain?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about how often a large capital gain is framed as a search for the biggest tax break. That framing is understandable. It can also send an investor toward a structure that does not accept the gain, does not match the desired degree of control, or locks up capital longer than the investor can live with.

The four common routes in this discussion are the 1031 exchange, the Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), the Opportunity Zone fund, and the 721 or UPREIT exchange. All defer tax in some form, but they apply to different gains, follow different clocks, and leave the investor with very different assets and exit paths.

The first-order question is, “Which route postpones the most tax?” The second-order question is, “What gain qualifies, what do I give up in control and liquidity, what clock am I accepting, and what happens when I need to exit?” The second question usually decides whether a structure is usable. This is a simplified educational comparison, not investment, legal, or tax advice; the result always depends on individual facts and professional advice.

### Key Takeaways

- Three of the four are real-estate tools. Only an Opportunity Zone fund can accept any capital gain, including gain from stock or a business sale.
- All four defer. Only an Opportunity Zone fund can also eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation, and only after a 10-year hold.
- A basis step-up at death can erase deferred gain for heirs on direct 1031 property, a DST interest, and 721 OP units.
- A DST reports on a 1099 plus a grantor letter. An Opportunity Zone fund reports on a K-1 with Forms 8997 and 8949. A 721 reports on a K-1 from the operating partnership. A direct 1031 reports on Form 8824.
- The 2026 law makes Opportunity Zones permanent from January 1, 2027. It does not alter the 1031, DST, or 721 rules.
- The right route follows the type of gain, the control an investor wants, and the lock-up the investor can sustain—not the headline tax result alone.

## The four routes at a glance

This table is an orientation. It puts the principal mechanical differences in one frame; each route carries additional conditions and depends on individual facts.

| Factor | 1031 Exchange | DST | Opportunity Zone Fund | 721 / UPREIT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Qualifying gain | Real estate | Real estate via 1031 | Any capital gain | Real estate or DST interest |
| Deadline | 45 / 180 days | 45 / 180 days | 180 days | At sponsor's roll-up |
| Reinvest | Full equity, replace debt | Full equity | The gain only | Property or OP-unit swap |
| Core benefit | Defer + step-up | Defer, passive, step-up | Defer, then eliminate appreciation after 10 years | Defer + diversify + estate liquidity |
| Control | High | Low | Low | Low |
| Liquidity at exit | Illiquid, 1031 again | Illiquid, 1031 or 721 | 10-year lock | OP units convertible to REIT shares |
| Step-up at death | Yes | Yes | N/A on appreciation already tax-free | Yes on OP units |
| Tax form | Form 8824 | 1099 + grantor letter | K-1 + 8997 / 8949 | K-1 from the OP |

## How each route actually works

The differences are mechanical, not cosmetic.

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) is a swap of investment real estate. The seller uses a [qualified intermediary](real-estate-tax-forms.html) to hold the sale proceeds so the seller does not receive them, then acquires like-kind replacement real estate within the applicable deadlines. When requirements are met, the tax bill on the old property is deferred. The exchange is reported on Form 8824, and the process can be repeated.

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) can fill the replacement side of a 1031 for an investor who does not want to become a landlord again. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a beneficial interest in a properly structured DST is treated as direct ownership of real estate for 1031-replacement purposes. A sponsor assembles institutional assets, divides the trust into fractional interests, and an investor purchases an interest. Because the DST is a grantor trust for tax purposes, income is reported on a 1099 and an accompanying grantor letter rather than on a K-1.

An [Opportunity Zone fund](opportunity-zones-guide.html), formally a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF), breaks from the real-estate-only pattern. An investor can reinvest a capital gain of any kind into the fund within 180 days. The fund deploys capital in businesses or property inside designated zones. The fund issues a Schedule K-1; the investor files Form 8997 to track the qualifying investment and Form 8949 to report the original deferred gain when it is recognized.

A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), also called an UPREIT, contributes real estate to a REIT's operating partnership in exchange for OP units. Section 721 makes that contribution tax-free. In practice, many investors use a two-step path: they complete a 1031 into a DST earmarked for sponsor roll-up, then make a [721 contribution of that DST interest](1031-to-721-exchange.html) when the sponsor calls it into the REIT. The operating partnership reports the investor's share on a K-1. OP units can later convert to publicly traded or non-traded REIT shares.

## Qualifying gain by route

This is the first filter, and it rules out options quickly. The 1031, DST, and 721 routes require real estate. A 1031 needs real property held for investment or business use. A DST is a replacement leg of a 1031, so the same real-estate condition applies upstream. A 721 accepts real property or a DST interest into the operating partnership.

A QOF is the exception. It can accept any capital gain. An appreciated-stock sale, business exit, or collectible gain can be invested even though none of those are real property. If the gain did not arise from real estate, three of the four routes are unavailable before any other comparison begins.

## Deferral versus elimination

Every route in this article defers tax. Only one eliminates tax on part of the economics, so the distinction matters.

Deferral is postponement, not erasure. A 1031, DST, and 721 move a real-estate gain forward until a taxable sale. There is an important estate-planning result: if the asset is held until death, heirs receive a basis step-up to fair market value. The deferred gain disappears for them. That step-up applies to directly owned 1031 property, DST interests, and 721 OP units. Used patiently, deferral can become permanent without a taxable sale.

A QOF also defers the original gain, but it can do something the other routes cannot. If the investor holds the fund interest for ten years, tax on the fund's own appreciation is eliminated rather than merely deferred. The original gain rolled in is still deferred and ultimately recognized, but fund appreciation after investment can be tax-free after the required hold. No other route on this page eliminates tax on appreciation. The trade-off is a long lock-up and development-style risk.

## Deadlines and reinvestment amounts

The clocks vary, and so do the amounts that must move.

A 1031 has two firm deadlines beginning on sale closing: 45 days to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days to close. A DST follows that same 45 and 180-day schedule because it is a 1031 replacement. Its practical advantage is that an interest can be bought in an exact dollar amount, which can make matching the exchange amount easier.

An Opportunity Zone investment runs on a single 180-day window from the date of the gain. For gain that flows through a partnership or S corporation, the window begins from the entity's year-end. A 721 has no statutory identification clock of its own. Its timing depends on when the sponsor rolls the asset into the REIT, which is why an investor may first use a DST and wait for the sponsor's call.

Reinvestment amounts also differ. A full 1031 requires reinvestment of all net equity and replacement of the debt on the relinquished property, or enough additional equity to cover it. A shortfall is taxable boot. A DST follows the same full-reinvestment rule because it is a 1031 replacement. A QOF requires reinvestment of the gain only, not the full sale proceeds, so the investor can retain original basis. A 721 is a contribution of property or a DST interest itself; there is no separate cash-reinvestment amount to calculate.

## Control and management

Directly held 1031 property is the high-control option. The investor selects the asset, sets rents, chooses whether and when to refinance, and chooses when to sell. The investor also performs the work or pays someone to do it.

The other three routes place operating decisions with a sponsor or manager. A DST is passive by design and by law. Its trust agreement prevents beneficiaries from making major decisions, which is part of why the IRS respects the structure. A beneficiary receives distributions but has no operational say. A QOF manager sources deals, develops property or businesses, and times exits. A 721 places the investor in a REIT, where professional management operates a diversified portfolio and the investor holds units.

If hands-on control is essential, only a direct 1031 into owned property provides it. If the goal is to stop managing real estate, the DST, QOF, and 721 routes are designed for that change in role.

## Liquidity and exit paths

None of these routes is liquid in the way a brokerage account is. The exit doors differ, and the paths can connect to each other.

Direct 1031 property is illiquid until it is sold. At sale, the investor can complete another 1031, move into a DST, or contribute into a 721. A DST is generally illiquid during its hold period, typically several years. When the sponsor sells the asset, the investor may complete another 1031 into a DST or direct property, or enter a 721 if that path is offered.

A QOF is the most locked route. The ten-year hold is central to its tax treatment, so an investor should expect a decade with little or no interim liquidity. A 721 ends in OP units, which may convert to REIT shares. Non-traded REIT shares can offer periodic, limited redemptions; publicly traded shares can be sold on an exchange. That is the closest this set gets to liquidity, but conversion and sale are taxable events that give up the deferral.

The routes can be read as a chain: a 1031 can lead to another 1031, a DST, or a 721; a DST can lead to a 1031 or a 721; a QOF leads toward its ten-year hold and then out; and a 721 leads to OP units and then, when the investor chooses, REIT shares. Investors can sequence them rather than select one forever.

## The 2026 Opportunity Zone changes

One route changed under 2026 law. The timeline matters because it determines which version of the rules applies.

Opportunity Zones were made permanent. Starting January 1, 2027, the program continues on an ongoing basis with a rolling five-year deferral and a single ten percent basis step-up at year five. That replaces the prior fixed sunset and tiered step-ups. Investments made under the original program continue under the original rules, meaning deferred gain invested under that earlier regime is still recognized on December 31, 2026. The ten-year exclusion for the fund's own appreciation continues to run from the investment date, so that clock is unchanged.

The scope is important. These changes apply only to Opportunity Zones. They do not change the 1031, DST, or 721 rules, deadlines, or tax forms. An investor who used the original program should mark December 31, 2026 with a CPA. An investor using the program in 2027 or later will work under the new rolling structure. The permanence can make a QOF a more durable planning tool for a large gain with a long horizon, but it does not reduce the need to understand the lock-up and underlying investment risk.

## How to choose

A short decision tree can narrow the options.

- Did the gain come from real estate? If not, a QOF is the route among these four. The other three require real property and cannot accept a stock or business-sale gain.
- Do you want to keep control and remain hands-on? If so, use a 1031 into property you own. If not, a DST can defer the same type of real-estate gain without the management work.
- Are you ready to leave active real estate for a diversified REIT on a long-term basis? If so, the 721 path—often reached through a DST first—can consolidate into professionally managed, divisible, estate-friendly units.
- Can you lock capital up for a decade to seek tax-free appreciation? If so, a QOF deserves consideration even for a real-estate gain, because it is the only route here that can eliminate tax on appreciation.

The examples make the filter practical. A tired landlord selling a rental and seeking passive income may use a 1031 into a DST. A founder with a gain from selling a company cannot use a 1031 and may use a QOF. An investor nearing retirement who wants to move a property into a diversified, divisible holding may use a 1031 into a DST and then a 721 into a REIT. These are three different answers to a shared tax-deferral question, shaped by gain type, control, and time horizon. [Installment sales](installment-sale-real-estate.html) are another deferral option for a seller who wants to spread a gain over time rather than reinvest it.

## Using more than one in the same year

These routes are not mutually exclusive in one tax year. A common sequence is to complete a 1031 into a DST, retain flexibility in that DST stage, and later allow the DST interest to roll into a REIT through a 721 when the sponsor calls it up. The investor commits to the permanent REIT move only when ready.

Different gains can also take different routes in the same year. A seller can 1031 the proceeds from a rental property while investing gain from appreciated stock into a QOF. Each gain follows the route it qualifies for. The discipline is to keep the separate deadlines straight: the 45 and 180-day 1031 clocks and the 180-day Opportunity Zone clock run independently, and a missed date is not forgiven.

## Common mistakes

Several errors recur, and most can be avoided.

- **Touching 1031 proceeds.** Constructive receipt of the sale funds ends the exchange. A qualified intermediary must hold the money from closing to closing.
- **Using a 1031 for a non-real-estate gain.** Stock and business-sale gain do not qualify. That gain belongs in a QOF or another eligible vehicle, not a 1031.
- **Under-reinvesting and creating boot.** A full 1031 requires all equity to be reinvested and debt to be replaced. Leftover cash or reduced debt is taxable boot.
- **Expecting a K-1 from a DST.** A DST reports on a 1099 and grantor letter, not a K-1. Confusing the forms creates filing problems.
- **Treating a QOF as liquid.** The ten-year hold is the feature, not a suggestion. Money an investor may need before then does not belong there.
- **Assuming a 721 can be undone.** After the investor holds OP units, there is generally no 1031 back out. Converting to REIT shares and selling is taxable. A 721 is a one-way door and should be used only with that understanding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which route defers the most tax, 1031, DST, Opportunity Zone, or 721?

They defer differently. The 1031, DST, and 721 defer a real-estate gain. A QOF defers any capital gain and is the only route here that can eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation after a ten-year hold. The fit depends on gain type and horizon, not a single ranking.

### Which one works for a non-real-estate gain like stock or a business sale?

Only an Opportunity Zone fund. The 1031, DST, and 721 require real property or an interest in it. A QOF can accept stock or business-sale gain, and the investor reinvests the gain amount within 180 days.

### What tax form does each route use?

A direct 1031 reports on Form 8824. A DST reports on a 1099 and grantor letter under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, not a K-1. A QOF issues a Schedule K-1 and uses Forms 8997 and 8949. A 721 reports on a K-1 from the REIT operating partnership.

### Did the 2026 law change the 1031, DST, or 721 rules?

No. The 2026 law made Opportunity Zones permanent beginning January 1, 2027 with a rolling five-year deferral and a single ten percent step-up at year five. Original-program investments still recognize deferred gain on December 31, 2026. None of that changes the 1031, DST, or 721 rules.

### Can I combine these routes?

Yes, often in sequence. A common path is a 1031 into a DST, followed by a 721 into a REIT to consolidate. Different gains can also use different routes in the same year, such as a real-estate 1031 and a QOF investment for stock gain.

### What happens to the deferred gain if I never sell?

If the asset is held until death, heirs receive a basis step-up to fair market value. That erases deferred gain for them on directly owned 1031 property, a DST interest, and 721 OP units. In that way, deferral can become permanent without a taxable sale.

### How do I choose among the four?

Choose by gain type, desired control, and time horizon. A 1031 suits hands-on real-estate ownership; a DST suits passive deferral of a real-estate gain; a QOF suits any capital gain or a decade-long path to tax-free appreciation; and a 721 suits a move into a diversified REIT. Individual facts determine the result.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange of investment real estate that defers capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, and the surtax. It is reported on Form 8824 and can be repeated.

**Delaware Statutory Trust:** A passive, 1031-eligible fractional interest in institutional real estate, treated as direct ownership under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 and reported on a 1099 plus a grantor letter.

**Opportunity Zone Fund (QOF):** A Qualified Opportunity Fund that accepts any capital gain, defers it, and can eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation after a ten-year hold; it reports on a K-1 with Forms 8997 and 8949.

**721 Exchange (UPREIT):** A tax-free contribution of property or a DST interest into a REIT operating partnership for OP units, reported on a K-1 and convertible to REIT shares.

**Boot:** Cash or relieved debt received in a 1031 exchange that is not reinvested. It is taxable and reduces the amount of gain deferred.

**Basis Step-Up:** The reset of an asset's tax basis to fair market value at death, which erases deferred gain for heirs on 1031 property, DST interests, and 721 OP units.

**Depreciation Recapture:** The portion of a real-estate gain tied to prior depreciation deductions, taxed at a higher rate unless deferred through a 1031 or DST.

**Qualified Intermediary:** The independent party that holds 1031 sale proceeds between closings so the seller never takes constructive receipt and preserves the exchange.
