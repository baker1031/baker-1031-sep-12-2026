# How OP Unit Distributions Are Taxed

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing a cash distribution from OP units described as a dividend. That shortcut can lead to a wrong expectation. OP units are partnership interests, so their income and distributions are governed by partnership tax rules rather than the simpler rules people often associate with REIT dividends.

This guide explains the partnership framework, the Schedule K-1, the different components that can appear in the tax reporting, return of capital and basis, the depreciation pass-through, and the contrast with REIT dividend taxation. It is educational information only. The tax result for a particular owner depends on that owner's facts, and a CPA should handle the specific situation.

First-order thinking says, “How much cash did I receive?” Second-order thinking separates the cash from the distributive share of income, asks what the K-1 reports, tracks whether basis is being reduced, and considers how depreciation affects taxable income. Those amounts can be related without being the same.

## OP units and partnership taxation

The starting point is that [OP units](op-units-explained.html) are partnership interests. The operating partnership is a partnership for tax purposes, and an OP unit holder is a partner. That makes the partnership a pass-through entity: its income and deductions flow to the partners rather than being taxed first at the partnership level in the way a corporation is taxed.

As a result, an OP unit holder is generally taxed on the holder's distributive share of the operating partnership's income, whether or not the partnership distributes that income in cash. The relevant income is net of the partnership's deductions, including depreciation. The cash distribution itself is handled separately under partnership distribution rules.

That is the core difference from a dividend frame. A dividend shorthand focuses on the amount paid. Partnership taxation begins with the owner's allocated share of income and deductions, then considers how a cash distribution affects basis. The framework is important because it explains why cash received and taxable income can differ.

## The Schedule K-1

The practical record of this partnership treatment is the Schedule K-1, not a 1099. The operating partnership provides each partner a K-1 showing the partner's share of income, deductions, depreciation, and other tax items. The owner and the owner's CPA use that information to prepare the tax return.

In contrast, REIT dividends are commonly reported on Form 1099-DIV. A K-1 can include ordinary income, capital gains, interest income, depreciation deductions, and other items with their own tax character. It is more detailed than a simple dividend form.

There is also a practical timing issue. K-1s can arrive later than 1099s and can be more complex to interpret. That can affect when a return can be filed and may make an extension appropriate in some situations. A CPA experienced with partnership reporting can work through the form and the basis calculations.

## Components of the distribution

The K-1 can report several components that receive different tax treatment. A common item is ordinary income: the owner's share of the partnership's net operating income, meaning rental income less expenses and depreciation. Ordinary income is generally taxed at ordinary rates.

Other components may include capital gains if the partnership sells property at a gain, interest income, and other items. Each is taxed according to its character. The K-1 is where the allocation is reported, and the CPA uses those component amounts in the return.

Cash distributions should not be confused with those income components. A partnership may distribute cash, but the cash payment is generally assessed under partnership distribution rules. The relevant question is often basis, covered next. For a direct-ownership comparison, see [income and distributions for OP units versus direct ownership](income-and-distributions-op-units-vs-direct-ownership.html).

## Return of capital and basis

Under partnership rules, cash distributions are generally not immediately taxable to the extent they do not exceed the holder's basis in the OP units. Instead, the distribution reduces basis and is treated as a return of capital. The investor receives cash without immediate tax on that distribution, but the basis in the units becomes lower.

That can make a cash distribution tax-efficient, but it does not mean the tax question disappears. If cash distributions exceed basis after it has been reduced to zero, the excess is generally taxable as gain. Basis also changes over time: allocated partnership income can increase it, while cash distributions decrease it.

This is an area where a ledger matters. A reported K-1 income amount and a cash-distribution amount may differ, and the correct treatment requires tracking the basis adjustments. The CPA tracks those movements and applies the partnership rules to the owner's facts.

## Depreciation pass-through

The operating partnership owns real estate and depreciates its properties. That depreciation passes through to OP unit holders on the K-1. It reduces the holder's share of partnership income, which can shelter some income from tax.

For that reason, taxable income from OP units can be lower than the cash received. The amount depends on the partnership's depreciation and is reflected on the K-1. This is one potential tax-efficiency feature of the pass-through structure.

Conceptually, the effect resembles the way depreciation can shelter income in direct rental ownership. The mechanism differs: an OP unit holder receives the allocated depreciation through the partnership instead of preparing a separate depreciation schedule for directly owned property. For more on that comparison, see [OP unit income versus rental income](op-unit-income-vs-rental-income-comparison.html).

The first-order view treats the distribution as the whole story. The second-order view asks how much of the partnership's net income was allocated, what depreciation was passed through, whether the cash reduced basis, and how these items will be reconciled on the K-1. That is a more accurate way to plan for a tax filing.

### Key Takeaways

- OP units are partnership interests. Owners report their share of partnership income, not only their cash distributions.
- The tax document is a Schedule K-1, not a 1099. It reports income, deductions including depreciation, and other items.
- Cash distributions often reduce basis as return of capital rather than being immediately taxable, to the extent of basis.
- Depreciation passes through to shelter some income. A CPA should handle the K-1, basis tracking, and calculations for the particular owner.

## Comparing to REIT dividend taxation

REIT shares, including shares received after conversion of OP units, pay dividends that are reported on Form 1099-DIV and taxed under corporate/dividend rules. REIT dividends are generally taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rates, although a portion may be eligible for the qualified business income deduction. Some dividend components can also be capital gain or return of capital.

OP unit taxation is different. While the owner holds OP units, the owner receives a K-1 and reports a share of partnership income, deductions, and other items. Cash distributions often reduce basis, and the partnership's depreciation can pass through. Those features can produce tax efficiencies that differ from the dividend treatment of REIT shares.

Conversion matters. While holding OP units, the ongoing income reporting is partnership reporting and K-1 based. After converting to REIT shares, the ongoing income reporting becomes dividend reporting and 1099-DIV based. The conversion also has its own deferred-gain implications. A CPA should consider both the conversion and the resulting ongoing tax reporting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How are OP unit distributions taxed?

They are taxed under partnership rules because OP units are partnership interests. The owner generally reports a distributive share of operating-partnership income on Schedule K-1, not simply the cash distributions received. Cash distributions often reduce basis as return of capital, and partnership depreciation can shelter some income. This differs from REIT dividend taxation. A CPA should apply the rules to the owner's particular facts.

### Do I get a 1099 or a K-1 for OP units?

You receive a Schedule K-1, not a 1099. The operating partnership uses the K-1 to report your share of income, deductions including depreciation, and other items. A REIT would instead generally report dividends on Form 1099-DIV. K-1s can arrive later and are more complex, so plan for partnership reporting with a CPA.

### What is reported on the K-1?

The K-1 reports your distributive share of the operating partnership's income, deductions, and other tax items. It may include ordinary income from operations, capital gains if the partnership sells property at a gain, interest income, and depreciation. Those components are reported on your return according to their character, and the K-1 is more detailed than a dividend 1099.

### Are my OP unit cash distributions taxable when I receive them?

Often not immediately. Cash distributions are generally not taxable to the extent they do not exceed your basis in the units. They reduce basis as return of capital. If distributions exceed basis after it reaches zero, the excess is generally taxable as gain. This treatment is separate from tax on your distributive share of partnership income shown on the K-1.

### What is return of capital for OP units?

It is the treatment of a cash distribution as a reduction in your basis rather than as immediate taxable income, to the extent of basis. You receive the cash without immediate tax on the distribution, but your basis falls. The effect can be tax-efficient because tax is deferred through the basis reduction. Distributions in excess of basis are generally taxable, and a CPA tracks the basis.

### How does depreciation affect my OP unit income?

The operating partnership depreciates its real estate, and that depreciation passes through to you on the K-1. Your share of partnership income is reduced by your share of depreciation, which can leave taxable income lower than cash received. The amount depends on the partnership's depreciation and is a feature of partnership pass-through treatment.

### Why is my taxable OP unit income different from my cash distributions?

Partnership taxation separates income allocation from cash distributions. You are taxed on your share of net partnership income, which can be reduced by depreciation. Cash distributions are handled separately and often reduce basis as return of capital. The combination can make taxable income lower than cash, but it can also produce different amounts in other circumstances. A CPA reconciles the K-1 and basis records.

### How does OP unit taxation compare to REIT dividends?

REIT dividends are reported on Form 1099-DIV and generally receive corporate/dividend treatment. They are generally ordinary income, though a portion may qualify for the qualified business income deduction, and some components can be capital gain or return of capital. OP units use partnership treatment: K-1 reporting, distributive share, return-of-capital basis treatment, and depreciation pass-through. Conversion changes the ongoing treatment.

### Does converting OP units to shares change my taxation?

Yes. While you hold OP units, you have partnership taxation and K-1 reporting. After conversion to REIT shares, ongoing income is generally dividend taxation reported on Form 1099-DIV. In addition, conversion can trigger the deferred gain on the conversion itself. A CPA should handle both the conversion and the change in ongoing reporting.

### Is OP unit income tax-efficient?

It can be. Depreciation can reduce taxable income below cash received, and return-of-capital treatment can make some cash distributions non-taxable when received by reducing basis. The actual outcome depends on the partnership's activity and the investor's facts. Tax efficiency is a potential feature, not a personal tax conclusion.

### Should I get help with OP unit taxation?

Yes. Partnership reporting, K-1 interpretation, the different income components, and basis tracking can be complex. Work with a CPA experienced in partnership taxation. Baker 1031 can explain the general framework, but the CPA handles the specific tax situation and calculations.

### Will OP units complicate my tax return?

They can. A K-1 adds income items, deductions, and basis adjustments that must be entered and reconciled. K-1s can also arrive later than 1099s, which may affect filing timing or lead to an extension. An experienced CPA handles K-1 reporting routinely, but the added work is a practical part of OP unit ownership.

### Could I owe tax on income I didn't receive in cash?

Potentially. Partnership tax applies to your distributive share of income whether or not the partnership distributes that amount in cash. In real-estate partnerships, depreciation often keeps taxable income at or below cash distributions, but that is not assured. Your CPA can determine whether allocated income exceeds cash for your specific circumstances.

### Is the depreciation pass-through similar to owning property directly?

Conceptually, yes. Direct ownership lets an owner use depreciation against rental income. OP unit ownership passes partnership depreciation through on the K-1 to shelter the owner's share of income. The mechanism is different, but the potential benefit—depreciation sheltering some income—is comparable.
