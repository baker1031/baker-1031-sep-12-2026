# TIC (Tenants-in-Common) 1031 Exchanges Explained

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 14 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about how often an investor says they want more control, when what they may actually want is more confidence in a decision. Those are not quite the same thing. A tenants-in-common interest gives a 1031 exchanger a real vote and direct deeded ownership. It also gives them a group decision process that can be harder to move when conditions change.

Before Delaware Statutory Trusts became the leading fractional 1031 option, the TIC filled that role. It still fits certain exchanges. A TIC lets several investors own a larger, often institutional-quality property directly, with more control than a DST but with added financing and governance complexity.

The first-order appeal is clear: direct title and a vote. The second-order question is whether that control is worth the lender underwriting, the co-owner interdependence, the limited liquidity, and the possibility of deadlock. This guide covers what a TIC is, how it works in a 1031 exchange, its comparison with a DST, financing and decision-making, the history, risks, and when it fits.

## What Is a TIC?

A tenants-in-common arrangement is direct co-ownership of a property by multiple investors. For 1031 purposes, there can be up to 35 co-owners. Each investor holds an undivided, deeded fractional interest: a percentage of the whole property, not a specific apartment, storefront, or physical portion. Each receives a proportionate share of income, expenses, and gain.

Because the interest is direct ownership of real property, it can qualify as 1031 replacement property. That gives an exchanger a fractional deeded share of a larger asset that may be beyond the investor’s individual purchase capacity.

Each co-owner has rights in the property, including a vote on major decisions, and can generally sell or transfer the interest subject to the governing agreements. This direct title and voting right are the defining features that distinguish a TIC from a DST.

## How TICs Work in a 1031

In a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) into a TIC, exchange proceeds acquire a deeded fractional interest in property shared with other investors. Because that interest is treated as direct real-property ownership, it can satisfy the like-kind requirement. The owner receives a proportional share of rental income and tax attributes.

Typically, a sponsor acquires the property, puts the co-ownership program in place, and sells interests to 1031 investors. The TIC agreement governs co-owner rights, management, and the decision process. A professional manager commonly runs the property. The structure pools exchangers into one larger asset while preserving more direct ownership rights than a DST beneficial interest provides.

## TIC vs. DST: The Key Differences

Both TICs and DSTs create fractional ownership in larger property that can serve as 1031 replacement property. The structures differ in who holds title, who makes decisions, and how financing works.

A TIC gives each co-owner direct deeded title and a vote on major decisions. The lender has to be comfortable with multiple owners, and major actions can require unanimous or supermajority consent.

A DST is a trust that holds title. The sponsor makes the decisions and arranges non-recourse debt once at the trust level. Investors hold passive beneficial interests that are treated as direct real-property ownership for 1031 purposes. The DST is simpler, more passive, and generally easier to finance—but investors do not control the decisions.

The trade-off is deliberate. A TIC offers control and direct ownership but can create financing complexity and decision deadlock. A DST offers simplicity, passivity, and a clearer debt-replacement process but requires the investor to give up the vote. Most modern fractional exchanges use DSTs because that simplicity has value.

## Financing and Lender Issues

TIC financing is more involved because a lender must underwrite multiple co-owners and structure debt across them. Each co-owner may be on the loan, and the lender must be satisfied with the collective credit profile and the TIC arrangement itself.

That burden, combined with the 35-investor limit, made TIC financing cumbersome. A DST addresses the issue differently: the sponsor arranges non-recourse debt once at the trust level, with no individual investor qualification. That is a meaningful advantage for an exchanger trying to replace relinquished debt.

If a TIC is under consideration, the loan structure and the investor’s obligations under it deserve the same attention as the property. A deeded interest does not make the financing detail disappear.

## Decision-Making and Unanimous Consent

The TIC’s central benefit can also be its central challenge. Major decisions—selling the property, refinancing, substantial capital expenditures, or changing the manager—often require unanimous or supermajority consent under the TIC agreement.

That gives each owner a meaningful voice. It can also create deadlock. If co-owners disagree on a sale or refinancing, the decision can stall and the delay can affect everyone. Reaching agreement among as many as 35 investors is not effortless.

In a DST, the sponsor makes those decisions and the investors remain passive. The trade is plain: no vote, but no group-decision deadlock. An investor deciding between the structures needs to decide whether direct control is a benefit after the practical cost of exercising it is included.

### Key takeaways

- A TIC is direct, deeded fractional co-ownership of a larger property and can be 1031-eligible.
- TICs provide voting rights and control. DSTs provide a passive structure with pre-arranged debt.
- TIC financing and unanimous-consent risk helped drive the market toward DSTs.

## The History of TIC Exchanges

TIC exchanges became prominent in the early 2000s after Rev. Proc. 2002-22 clarified when a co-ownership arrangement would be treated as a TIC—direct real-property ownership eligible for a 1031 exchange—rather than a partnership, which is an entity interest that does not qualify. That clarification opened the door to sponsored TIC programs.

TIC syndications grew rapidly. But financing and decision-making challenges, together with difficulties some programs experienced in the 2008–2009 downturn, moved the market toward DSTs. The IRS had recognized DST interests for 1031 use in Rev. Rul. 2004-86.

Today, DSTs dominate the fractional 1031 market. TICs remain available, but generally as a smaller, specialized niche. The history explains why the default shifted without making the TIC structure irrelevant.

## Why DSTs Largely Replaced TICs

DST financing is simpler: the sponsor arranges non-recourse debt once at the trust level rather than asking a lender to underwrite up to 35 TIC co-owners. DST decision-making is also cleaner because the sponsor acts without a multi-owner vote. A DST has no practical investor limit comparable to the TIC’s 35-person limit, which can allow smaller minimum investments.

The investor experience is more passive, with the sponsor handling operations and decisions. DST beneficial interests are also generally easier to divide among multiple heirs. For a fractional exchanger whose main objective is a passive replacement property, those features often outweigh the attraction of a TIC vote.

TICs retain their place when direct title, voting rights, or a particular financing arrangement are important enough to justify the added complexity. For the usual fractional exchange, the DST has become the standard tool.

## When a TIC Fits

A TIC may fit an investor who specifically wants a deeded interest, a say in major decisions, or an ownership or financing arrangement a DST does not provide—and who accepts the extra complexity and deadlock risk. Larger exchangers and investors co-investing with known partners can be drawn to those direct rights. In some situations, a TIC may provide useful structuring flexibility.

For most investors looking for a passive, fast-closing, [diversified fractional replacement](building-a-diversified-dst-portfolio-by-asset-class.html), a DST is simpler. The choice also benefits from understanding [fee-simple versus fractional](fee-simple-vs-fractional-replacement-property.html) ownership. A TIC is the better tool only when its direct-title and voting features genuinely matter to the investor.

## TIC Risks and Considerations

TICs carry ordinary real-estate risk plus structure-specific risk. Co-owner disputes can arise. With shared financing, a co-owner’s financial problems can affect the group. Selling an individual TIC interest can be difficult because the secondary market is limited.

The program also depends on the sponsor and property manager. Sponsor quality matters, just as it does in a DST. Like DSTs, TICs are illiquid and are generally best held through the full investment period rather than treated as easy-to-exit holdings.

These points do not disqualify TICs. They do make thorough diligence essential: the property, sponsor, TIC agreement, financing arrangement, and co-owners all need review. For many investors, the simpler DST avoids several of the risks that come from co-owner governance.

## How a TIC Is Structured

A sponsor usually acquires a property, places financing on it, and sells deeded undivided interests to up to 35 investors. The TIC agreement governs co-owner rights, professional management, and decision-making.

Each co-owner holds title to a fractional share, receives proportional income and tax items such as depreciation and expenses, and can generally transfer the interest subject to the agreement and lender requirements. A master lease or management agreement often handles day-to-day operations so the owners do not manage the property individually.

The aim is to pool investors into an institutional asset while retaining direct real-property ownership. The TIC agreement and financing documents are therefore core diligence documents: they define the investor’s rights, obligations, transfer restrictions, and decision rules.

## Income, Taxes, and Reporting in a TIC

A TIC owner receives a proportionate share of rental income and reports a proportionate share of income, expenses, and depreciation, generally on Schedule E, much as an owner of a direct fraction of a building would. The treatment follows the direct-real-property nature of the interest.

The carryover basis from the relinquished property follows into the TIC interest, preserving deferred gain. The investor can continue to use depreciation on the share. Debt on the property is allocated proportionately, which is one way a TIC can help replace relinquished debt.

When the property is sold, the co-owner receives a share of proceeds and gain. A TIC interest can generally be exchanged again under Section 1031, continuing the deferral, much as any other investment real property could be exchanged.

## Diligencing a TIC Investment

Review the property’s quality, location, tenants, and projected income. Review the sponsor’s track record and fees as carefully as with a DST sponsor. Then add the TIC-specific work: decision rules and deadlock risk, financing and the investor’s obligations, management arrangements, transfer restrictions, and the alignment and financial quality of co-owners to the extent that information is available.

Because a TIC adds co-ownership and financing complexity to normal property risk, the work is more involved than for a DST. An adviser with experience in both structures can help compare a TIC against a DST alternative that may meet the same goals with less structural burden.

## Is a TIC Right for Your Exchange?

A TIC can be appropriate when direct deeded ownership and a vote are important, the investor is joining known partners or pursuing a particular structure, and the investor accepts the additional complexity and potential for deadlock. It gives more control than a DST, but that control has a cost.

It is generally less suitable for someone seeking maximum simplicity and passivity, straightforward debt replacement without co-owner underwriting, certainty that decisions will not deadlock, smaller minimums, or easier estate division. That description fits many exchangers.

TICs are now specialized enough that it is prudent to [work with an advisor who understands both TIC and DST structures](why-work-with-a-1031-exchange-advisor.html). For the typical fractional exchange, a DST is usually simpler. A TIC should be chosen because the direct ownership features answer a real need, not merely because they sound appealing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a tenants-in-common (TIC) 1031 exchange?

It is a 1031 exchange into direct, deeded fractional co-ownership of a larger property by up to 35 investors. Because the investor owns a direct real-property interest, it can qualify as like-kind replacement property and can provide a deeded share of an institutional-quality asset.

### What’s the difference between a TIC and a DST?

A TIC gives direct deeded title and voting rights, but lenders must underwrite multiple owners and major decisions can require unanimous consent, creating deadlock risk. A DST uses sponsor management and pre-arranged non-recourse debt. It is simpler and passive, but investors have no control. Most current fractional exchanges use DSTs.

### Can a TIC interest be 1031 replacement property?

Yes. A TIC interest is direct ownership of real property and can be like-kind replacement property. Rev. Proc. 2002-22 clarified when a co-ownership arrangement is a TIC rather than a partnership.

### Why did DSTs become more popular than TICs?

DSTs arrange debt once at the trust level, avoid TIC decision deadlock because the sponsor decides, have no 35-investor limit that restricts minimums, and offer a fully passive form that is generally easier to divide among heirs. Those advantages shifted the market to DSTs.

### How many investors can be in a TIC?

Up to 35 co-owners may participate for 1031 purposes. Each has an undivided deeded interest and a vote on major decisions. The limit, unlike a DST’s effectively unlimited investor capacity, contributed to higher TIC minimums and less flexibility.

### What is the decision-making risk in a TIC?

The TIC agreement often requires unanimous or supermajority consent for a sale, refinancing, or major capital expenditure. If owners disagree, the decision can deadlock. This is a principal TIC drawback and a reason DSTs, where the sponsor decides, became favored.

### Is TIC financing harder than DST financing?

Yes. Lenders need to underwrite multiple co-owners and debt across them. A DST generally has non-recourse debt arranged once at the trust level and requires no individual investor qualification. That makes it a simpler route for replacing leverage.

### Do I get a vote in a TIC?

Yes. A co-owner votes on major property decisions. That direct involvement can be valuable, but it comes with the risk that co-owners cannot agree. DST investors are passive and the sponsor makes decisions.

### Can I sell my TIC interest?

Generally, yes, subject to the TIC agreement and financing restrictions. But the secondary market for a single TIC interest is limited, so the interest is illiquid. Like a DST, it is generally best held through its full investment period.

### What is Rev. Proc. 2002-22?

It is IRS guidance that clarified when a co-ownership arrangement is treated as a TIC—direct real-property ownership eligible for 1031 treatment—rather than a partnership interest, which is generally not eligible. It helped enable sponsored TIC programs in the early 2000s.

### When does a TIC make more sense than a DST?

When an investor truly wants direct title, a vote, or a financing or ownership structure a DST does not offer and accepts the complexity and deadlock risk. Some larger exchangers and known co-investors prefer it. For most fractional exchanges, the DST is simpler.

### Are TICs still used today?

Yes, but in a smaller specialized niche. DSTs dominate for their simplicity, yet TICs still serve investors who value direct title, voting rights, or particular structures. The shift occurred after the 2008–2009 period, not because TICs disappeared.

### What are the risks of a TIC investment?

They include co-owner disputes and deadlock, financing complexity, co-owner financial problems affecting a group, limited liquidity, reliance on the sponsor and property manager, and ordinary real-estate risk. Those risks call for diligence of the property, sponsor, TIC agreement, financing, and co-owners.

### Can a TIC be divided among heirs?

A TIC interest can pass to heirs. But DST beneficial interests are generally easier to divide cleanly among multiple heirs, which is one reason investors often favor DSTs for passive, divisible fractional ownership in estate planning.

### Is a TIC right for my exchange?

It may be if direct deeded ownership and a vote, known co-investors, or a particular structure matter enough to accept complexity and deadlock risk. For most investors seeking passive, diversified fractional replacement property, a DST is usually the better fit.

### How is a TIC investment structured?

A sponsor acquires the property, places financing, and sells deeded undivided interests to up to 35 investors. A TIC agreement governs rights, professional management, and decision-making. Owners hold title to their shares and receive proportional income and tax items. The agreement and loan documents are key.

### How is TIC income taxed?

The investor generally reports proportional income, expenses, and depreciation on Schedule E, similar to direct property ownership. Carryover basis follows into the TIC interest, and the investor continues to use depreciation on the share.

### Can I do another 1031 on my TIC interest later?

Yes. Because it is direct real-property ownership, a TIC interest can generally be exchanged under Section 1031 on a sale or exit, continuing the gain deferral like any other qualifying investment real property.

### What documents govern a TIC?

The TIC agreement governs rights, management, major decisions, and transfer restrictions. Financing documents set obligations under shared debt. Property and offering materials also matter. Understanding decision rules and financing obligations is essential before investing.

### How do I diligence a TIC investment?

Evaluate the property, including quality, location, tenants, and income; the sponsor’s track record and fees; the financing and your obligations; and the co-ownership arrangement, including deadlock rules, transfer restrictions, and co-owner alignment. TIC diligence is more involved than DST diligence.

### Can a co-owner’s problems affect my TIC investment?

Potentially. With shared financing, a co-owner default or financial trouble can affect the group. Disputes can also cause deadlock. DST investors are passive and independent of one another, which avoids this particular form of co-owner interdependence.

### Does a TIC come with professional management?

Usually. Sponsor-assembled TICs commonly use a professional property manager, often through a master lease or management agreement. Co-owners are generally involved in major decisions, not routine operations.

### Is a TIC or DST better for replacing debt?

A DST is generally simpler because non-recourse debt is arranged once at the trust level with no individual qualification. A TIC can allocate debt proportionately to owners, but its lender must underwrite multiple co-owners. For straightforward debt replacement, the DST approach is usually easier.

### Can I refinance or sell a TIC interest?

An individual TIC interest can be sold subject to the agreement and lender requirements, but its secondary market is limited. Refinancing involves the owners and lender collectively. These features reinforce the view that TICs, like DSTs, are best held through their investment period rather than exited early.

### Do all 35 TIC co-owners have to agree on decisions?

The TIC agreement often requires unanimous or supermajority consent for major decisions. Day-to-day operations are normally handled by a professional manager. Owners are not generally voting on routine matters, but they may need to agree on matters such as a sale or refinancing.

### How is a TIC different from a partnership?

A TIC is direct co-ownership: each investor holds deeded title to a fraction of the building and is treated as owning that fraction directly. A partnership is an entity, and a partnership interest generally does not qualify for a 1031 exchange. Rev. Proc. 2002-22 addresses the distinction.

### Are TICs riskier than DSTs?

TICs add structural risks—deadlock, shared-finance interdependence, and co-owner problems—to the ordinary real-estate and sponsor risks both structures can have. DSTs avoid co-owner governance by making investors passive and independent. The question is whether direct ownership and voting rights justify the added risks in a particular case.

### Why are TICs limited to 35 investors?

Rev. Proc. 2002-22 set conditions, including a co-owner limit, for a co-ownership arrangement to be treated as a TIC rather than a partnership for 1031 purposes. The 35-owner cap, combined with financing and decision complexity, contributed to higher TIC minimums and the shift to DSTs, which have no comparable practical limit.

## Glossary

- **Tenants-in-Common (TIC):** Direct fractional co-ownership by multiple investors, up to 35 for 1031 purposes, with a deeded undivided interest and a vote.
- **Undivided Interest:** A fractional share of the whole property, not a specific physical portion.
- **TIC Agreement:** The contract governing co-owner rights, management, and decision-making.
- **Unanimous Consent:** A requirement for all co-owners to agree on a major decision, and a source of deadlock risk.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding title to property and issuing passive fractional interests; the more common modern fractional option.
- **Rev. Proc. 2002-22:** IRS guidance on when a co-ownership is a TIC rather than a partnership for 1031 purposes.
- **Rev. Rul. 2004-86:** IRS ruling treating a DST interest as direct real-property ownership for 1031 purposes.
- **Deadlock:** A stalled TIC decision when co-owners cannot reach the required consent, potentially harming all.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** Debt secured only by the property without personal liability, arranged at the trust level in a DST.
- **Secondary Market:** A limited and often illiquid market for reselling TIC or DST interests.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that assembles and manages a TIC or DST program.
- **Fractional Ownership:** Owning a share of a larger property through a TIC or DST.
