# How Non-Traded REITs Are Valued

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing a non-traded REIT's NAV quoted as though it were a stock price. It is not. A listed REIT has a market price that changes whenever buyers and sellers transact. A non-traded REIT has no exchange listing and no continuous market price. Its value must be calculated.

That calculation is an appraisal-based net asset value, or NAV. This guide explains why there is no market price, how the NAV process works, how often it is refreshed, where it can lag, and what an investor should examine before treating a reported value as dependable. First-order thinking asks whether the NAV on a statement went up, down, or stayed flat. Second-order thinking asks how that number was made, which assumptions sit beneath it, when the appraisals were performed, how much debt is present, and what a buyer might actually pay in a real transaction. The second question is where risk lives.

For background on the vehicle itself, see [our complete guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## Why There's No Market Price

A publicly traded REIT has a price because its shares trade on an exchange. Buyers and sellers meet continuously, and supply and demand establish a live, observable value. That value reflects investor sentiment, interest-rate expectations, and the market's view of the underlying real estate in real time.

None of that price discovery exists when a REIT is not exchange-listed. There is no continuous marketplace quoting a price. The value therefore cannot be observed in the way a listed share price can; it has to be estimated from what the REIT owns.

The basic calculation starts with real estate, usually the dominant asset, adds cash and other assets, subtracts liabilities, and divides the result across shares. The result is NAV per share. It is the price at which the REIT generally issues new shares and redeems existing shares. The lack of a market price is precisely why this appraisal-based process is necessary.

## The Appraisal-Based NAV Process

The sponsor periodically engages independent third-party valuation advisors to appraise the REIT's properties. Appraisals commonly use an income-based method: a property's [net operating income](same-store-noi-explained.html) is capitalized at an appropriate cap rate. Comparable-sales and replacement-cost analyses may support that work. These property values are the largest input into NAV.

Then comes the arithmetic. The REIT adds cash and other assets to the appraised real-estate value, subtracts liabilities including debt, and divides the remainder by the number of shares outstanding. That produces NAV per share, the operating price for issuances and redemptions.

Independent advisors are meant to provide a check on the sponsor's own view. That matters because a sponsor manages the assets and earns fees from them. Independence can reduce that conflict, but it does not turn an appraisal into a live market clearing price.

> NAV is not a market price. It is the appraised net worth of the REIT divided by its shares, and it is only as good as the appraisals and assumptions used to produce it.

## Valuation Frequency

Frequency tells you how current a reported NAV may be. Legacy non-traded REITs historically held an offering price fixed for an initial period and then updated NAV only about once a year. A value based on those old structures could reflect conditions from many months before.

Modern perpetual-life NAV REITs usually refresh the calculation more often—commonly monthly and, in some cases, daily. Their regularly updated NAV governs ongoing share sales and redemptions. More frequent valuation gives investors a more current picture and reduces staleness, although it cannot eliminate the problem.

So the range is broad: roughly annual valuations for older structures, and monthly or daily valuations for many modern NAV REITs. Frequency is not a promise of accuracy, but it is one useful measure of how fresh the reported value is.

## Limitations & Lags

NAV is a considered estimate, not a transaction price. It depends on appraisal judgment, especially the cap-rate assumptions used to value properties. Small changes in cap-rate assumptions can move appraised values meaningfully. That uncertainty is part of the number, even when independent advisors have been involved.

Appraisals also update gradually. Periodic valuation and incremental adjustments can create a smoothing effect: NAV tends to move more smoothly than the real market. In a fast-falling market, the reported NAV may be above what the properties would actually fetch. In a fast-rising market, it may be below. The stable-looking number can simply be behind the market.

Leverage increases the importance of that gap. Debt is included among liabilities, and it is relatively fixed while property values move. A given percentage change in property value can therefore create a larger percentage change in the equity value beneath it. Leverage magnifies both upside and downside, and it can make a later appraisal catch-up more consequential.

The first-order response to a smooth NAV is relief. The second-order response is to ask whether smoothness is a measurement feature rather than evidence of low volatility. Do not mistake a smooth NAV for the absence of risk.

## Key Takeaways
- Non-traded REITs are not exchange-listed, so their per-share value is calculated as appraisal-based NAV rather than discovered by a live market.
- Independent third-party advisors appraise properties; the REIT adds other assets, subtracts liabilities, and divides by shares outstanding.
- Legacy structures may update about annually, while modern perpetual-life NAV REITs may update monthly or daily. More frequent values are generally more current.
- NAV is sensitive to cap-rate assumptions, is subject to smoothing and lag, and can be materially affected by leverage. Review methodology, advisor independence, frequency, and debt.

## NAV vs. What Shares Are Really Worth

NAV is the price at which a non-traded REIT issues and redeems shares. It can be an independently supported estimate of net asset value, yet it can still differ from what the underlying real estate would command in an arm's-length transaction. This distinction matters most when markets move quickly and appraisals have not caught up. For more context, see [how REIT NAV premiums and discounts work](reit-nav-premiums-and-discounts-explained.html).

The difference becomes concrete at the edges. A shareholder using the redemption program receives a price based on NAV, subject to program caps and any early-redemption discount. That price may not match current market reality. If the REIT later has a liquidity event—a listing, merger, or wind-down—the price realized then will reflect actual conditions and buyer demand. It may be higher or lower than the last reported NAV.

The NAV on a statement is the price you can transact at under the program today. It is not a guaranteed realizable price in a sale. That is a modest distinction in a quiet market and a large one when liquidity is scarce or property values are moving quickly.

## What Investors Should Watch

Start with the valuation methodology. How are the properties valued? Is the process based on an income approach, and what cap-rate assumptions are being used? What other assets and liabilities enter the NAV calculation?

Next, assess the independence of the valuation advisors. A valuation process has more credibility when independent third parties, rather than only the sponsor, drive the appraisals. Independence does not erase estimation risk, but it is a meaningful check on the sponsor's incentive to value assets it manages and earns fees on.

Then look at frequency. A modern structure with monthly or daily NAV offers a fresher value than a legacy structure with roughly annual valuation. Finally, understand leverage. More debt makes NAV more sensitive to property-value and cap-rate movement. Throughout the review, keep the smoothing-and-lag limitation in view.

For a framework that places NAV alongside other REIT measures, see [how to evaluate a REIT using FFO, AFFO, and NAV](how-to-evaluate-a-reit-ffo-affo-and-nav.html). The useful question is not simply whether NAV looks stable. It is how much confidence the underlying process and current conditions justify.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How are non-traded REITs valued?

They use appraisal-based NAV because they are not listed on an exchange and have no continuous market price. Independent third-party valuation advisors periodically appraise the properties, primarily by capitalizing each property's NOI at an appropriate cap rate, with comparable-sales and replacement-cost support. The sponsor adds cash and other assets, subtracts liabilities and debt, and divides by shares outstanding. NAV becomes the price for issuing and redeeming shares. It is a periodically updated, cap-rate-sensitive estimate, not a market-discovered certainty.

### Why doesn't a non-traded REIT have a market price?

Its shares are not listed on a stock exchange. There is no continuous group of buyers and sellers, no live supply-and-demand price, and no continuously observable reaction to sentiment, interest-rate expectations, or perceived real-estate value. NAV is calculated from the assets because there is no market mechanism to discover a price.

### What is NAV per share?

NAV per share is the appraised value of real estate plus cash and other assets, less liabilities and debt, divided by shares outstanding. It is the appraised net worth of the REIT expressed per share and the price for issuances and redemptions. Because it depends on appraisals and cap-rate assumptions and updates periodically, it should be read as an estimate rather than a continuously market-tested figure.

### Who appraises a non-traded REIT's properties?

The sponsor generally engages independent third-party valuation advisors. They commonly use the income approach, comparable sales, and replacement cost. The advisors' role is to provide an objective check on the sponsor's valuation of assets it manages and earns fees on. Their work can support NAV, but it does not eliminate appraisal judgment or estimation risk.

### How often is a non-traded REIT's NAV updated?

It depends on the structure. Legacy non-traded REITs often held the offering price fixed initially and then updated NAV only about once a year. Modern perpetual-life NAV REITs commonly update monthly and sometimes daily, because the value governs ongoing share sales and redemptions. More frequent calculation reduces, but does not eliminate, staleness.

### Can a non-traded REIT's NAV be wrong or misleading?

It can mislead if treated as an exact, immediately realizable value. NAV depends on appraisal judgment and cap-rate assumptions, and small assumption changes can move it. Periodic appraisals can smooth the reported number and make it lag rapid market moves. Leverage can magnify the impact once values catch up. A smooth value is not a guarantee of stability.

### What is the smoothing effect in non-traded REIT NAV?

Smoothing is the tendency of reported NAV to change gradually and appear less volatile than the real estate it represents. Properties are appraised periodically, and appraisers usually adjust estimates incrementally rather than in sharp jumps. In a fast-rising or falling market, NAV can sit above or below what the properties would command until later appraisals reflect the change. Debt can compound the result.

### Does NAV reflect what I could sell my shares for?

Not exactly. NAV is the issue and redemption price under the REIT's program, but it is appraisal-based. A redemption can be limited by program caps or an early-redemption discount. A later listing, merger, or wind-down reflects actual buyer demand and conditions and can produce a different value. Treat NAV as an informed estimate, not a guaranteed sale price.

### What is a cap rate and why does it matter for NAV?

A cap rate, or capitalization rate, converts NOI into an estimated property value: value equals NOI divided by the cap rate. It represents the return a buyer may require for that property type in current conditions. The relationship is inverse: a higher cap rate implies a lower value, and a lower cap rate implies a higher value. Rising cap rates, often alongside rising interest rates, can reduce property values and NAV; cap-rate compression can increase them.

### How does leverage affect a non-traded REIT's NAV?

Leverage is the debt used to finance the properties. NAV equals assets less liabilities, including that debt. Because the debt is relatively fixed while property values change, a percentage movement in property value produces a larger percentage movement in the equity/NAV beneath it. Leverage increases upside and downside sensitivity. Review leverage in the offering and periodic disclosures.

### Are modern NAV REITs valued differently from legacy non-traded REITs?

The fundamental appraisal process is similar, but the structure and frequency differ. Legacy REITs often had an initial fixed price, roughly annual NAV updates, and a single terminal liquidity event years later. Modern perpetual-life NAV REITs commonly recalculate NAV monthly or daily for ongoing sales and redemption-based liquidity. The newer value may be fresher, but it remains an appraisal-based estimate.

### What should I watch when evaluating a non-traded REIT's valuation?

Focus on methodology, the cap-rate and other inputs, the independence of valuation advisors, valuation frequency, and leverage. Remember that a monthly or daily number is fresher than an annual one but still can lag. Those checks tell you how much confidence the reported NAV deserves.

### Is a smooth, stable NAV a sign of low risk?

No. A smooth NAV can reflect periodic appraisal rather than stable underlying real estate. Rents can fall, occupancy can drop, property values can decline, and cap rates can rise whether or not the number is continuously marked to market. Judge risk by the underlying real estate, leverage, and structure, not by the calm appearance of the reported NAV.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Net Asset Value (NAV) | The calculated per-share value at which a non-traded REIT transacts. |
| Appraisal-Based NAV | NAV derived from periodic property appraisals rather than a market price. |
| Independent Valuation Advisor | A third party engaged to appraise the REIT's properties objectively. |
| Cap Rate | The ratio converting NOI into an estimated property value. |
| Net Operating Income (NOI) | A property's income after operating expenses, before debt service. |
| Income Approach | Valuing a property by capitalizing its NOI. |
| Smoothing Effect | NAV's tendency to change gradually and look less volatile than the real market. |
| Valuation Lag | The delay before NAV reflects rapid real-market moves. |
| Valuation Frequency | How often NAV is recalculated—annual, monthly, or daily. |
| NAV REIT | A modern perpetual-life REIT with a regularly updated NAV. |
| Legacy Non-Traded REIT | An older structure valued infrequently, often about annually. |
| Leverage | Debt that amplifies how property-value changes affect NAV. |
| Shares Outstanding | The share count across which NAV is divided to get per-share value. |
| Liquidity Event | A listing, merger, or wind-down that realizes actual market value. |
| Redemption Program | The limited, NAV-priced way to exit a non-traded REIT. |
| Sponsor | The firm that manages the REIT and oversees the NAV process. |
