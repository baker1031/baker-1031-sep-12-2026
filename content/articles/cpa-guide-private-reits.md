# The CPA's 2026 Guide to Private & Non-Traded REITs

I keep seeing the same thing when a REIT reaches a CPA's desk: the client remembers the cash distribution, but not its tax character. That is understandable. A single Form 1099-DIV can carry four or five different treatments, and the headline yield says almost nothing about the return.

This guide is for the reporting questions tax advisors actually receive: dividend character, the Section 199A deduction, basis erosion from return of capital, NIIT, UBTI, and the point at which a client asks whether a REIT can be a 1031 replacement. [Our REITs guide](reits-guide.html) is the client-facing companion, and [our private-REIT dividend-tax guide](private-reit-dividends-tax.html) goes deeper on the distribution analysis.

First-order thinking treats the distribution as one ordinary-income number and the stated yield as the return. Second-order thinking separates current taxable income from cash received, tracks the basis that is being given up, and asks how liquidity, tax, and the eventual sale interact. That is where the useful work is.

## Key takeaways for advisors

- Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, not qualified dividends. Reconcile each 1099-DIV box rather than treating the distribution as one number.
- Qualified REIT dividends in Box 5 receive the **20%** Section 199A deduction, without W-2/UBIA or income-threshold limits. The 2025 law made the deduction permanent.
- Box 3 return of capital is not currently taxed, but it reduces basis. At zero basis, it becomes capital gain; reduced basis also raises a later gain on sale.
- Box 2a capital-gain distributions are long-term regardless of the shareholder's holding period. The Box 2b unrecaptured Section 1250 portion is taxed at up to **25%**.
- A REIT usually blocks UBTI for IRA and tax-exempt clients, unlike a leveraged partnership that can create UBTI/UDFI and a Form 990-T.
- REIT shares are securities, not like-kind property. They are never 1031 replacement property. The deferred route is DST first, then a 721 contribution; selling REIT shares is taxable.

## REIT taxation in brief

A REIT that satisfies its income, asset, and distribution tests generally is not taxed at the entity level on income it distributes. The test most people remember is the distribution rule: it must distribute at least **90% of taxable income** each year to retain REIT status. Income then moves through to the shareholder rather than remaining subject to corporate-level tax. That is why these vehicles can produce large, recurring distributions and why the reporting burden falls on the investor.

The wrapper changes the liquidity and suitability analysis, not the federal dividend character. A publicly traded REIT trades on an exchange and is marked to market each day. A non-traded REIT is SEC-registered but unlisted, so it is valued periodically and redemptions are limited. A private REIT is offered under Regulation D only to accredited investors and has the least liquidity and public disclosure. The same 1099-DIV framework still governs the federal treatment. For the structure and suitability questions, see [public versus non-traded REITs](public-vs-non-traded-reits.html) and [whether private REITs are safe](are-private-reits-safe.html).

## The 1099-DIV, box by box

The distribution a client quotes is rarely a single tax character. Read every box.

- **Box 1a — Total ordinary dividends.** This is the full ordinary portion, commonly the largest piece for a REIT. It is taxed at ordinary rates and flows to Schedule B and the dividend line of Form 1040.
- **Box 1b — Qualified dividends.** This is a subset of Box 1a taxed at long-term capital-gain rates. For REITs it is often small or zero because the underlying REIT income generally was not subject to corporate-level tax. Do not presume that a REIT dividend is qualified.
- **Box 2a — Total capital-gain distributions.** These are the REIT's net long-term property-sale gains passed to shareholders. They are long-term regardless of the client's holding period and go to Schedule D.
- **Box 2b — Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain.** This depreciation-related portion of Box 2a is a subset, not an addition. It may be taxed at a maximum **25%** rate and is carried through the Schedule D worksheet.
- **Box 3 — Nondividend distributions.** This is return of capital. It is not currently taxed, but it reduces basis.
- **Box 5 — Section 199A dividends.** This is the qualified-REIT-dividend amount eligible for the **20%** deduction. It generally sits within Box 1a and drives Form 8995 or Form 8995-A.

Two REITs can both report an **8%** headline yield and leave very different after-tax outcomes. The split across these boxes, not the yield alone, determines the current tax result.

## The Section 199A 20% deduction

Box 5 qualified REIT dividends qualify for the **20%** deduction under Section 199A, claimed on Form 8995 or Form 8995-A. A client in the **37%** bracket who receives qualified REIT dividends is effectively taxed at roughly **29.6%** on that slice after the deduction.

The difference from ordinary QBI matters. The REIT-dividend component is not subject to the W-2 wage or UBIA-of-property limits, and it is not phased out by the taxable-income thresholds that restrict QBI deductions for specified service businesses and high earners. A client above those thresholds still receives the full **20%** deduction on qualified REIT dividends; there is no wage or unadjusted-basis computation for that portion.

The deduction had been scheduled to expire after **2025**. The **2025** One Big Beautiful Bill Act made it permanent. That makes the qualified-REIT-dividend benefit a continuing planning feature, though preparers should still check the current form instructions and filing-year figures. See [how private-REIT dividends are taxed](private-reit-dividends-tax.html) for the client version of this discussion.

## Return of capital and basis tracking

Box 3 nondividend distributions are return of capital. They are not taxed in the year received; they reduce the shareholder's basis. Depreciation can shelter book earnings, allowing cash to leave the REIT faster than taxable income is recognized. So an advertised **8%** distribution can include a meaningful Box 3 component: part deferral and part return of the investor's own capital.

That makes a basis schedule necessary, not optional. Once basis reaches zero, additional return of capital becomes capital gain in that year. Before then, every dollar of Box 3 reduces basis and therefore increases the eventual gain, or reduces the eventual loss, on a sale or redemption. It is deferral, not free yield. The 1099-DIV gives the current year's Box 3 amount; it does not maintain the running basis for the client.

## Worked example: one distribution, five characters

The figures are illustrative only. Assume a client holds a non-traded REIT and receives **$10,000** of distributions for the year.

| 1099-DIV box | Illustrative amount | Treatment |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Box 1a — Ordinary dividends | **$6,000** | Ordinary rates; the qualified portion receives the Section 199A deduction |
| Box 1b — Qualified (subset of 1a) | **$200** | Long-term capital-gain rates; usually small for REITs |
| Box 2a — Capital-gain distribution | **$1,500** | Long-term regardless of holding period; Schedule D |
| Box 2b — Unrecaptured Section 1250 (subset of 2a) | **$600** | Up to **25%** maximum rate |
| Box 5 — Section 199A dividends (subset of 1a) | **$5,400** | Eligible for the **20%** deduction; no W-2/UBIA or income limit |
| Box 3 — Return of capital | **$2,500** | Not taxed now; reduces basis dollar for dollar |

Boxes 1b, 2b, and 5 are subsets, not additions. Actual splits change by REIT and by year. In this example, the **$10,000** headline distribution is not all currently taxable. The Section 199A slice receives its **20%** deduction, while **$2,500** of Box 3 is deferred today but reduces basis by **$2,500**. Put that amount on the basis schedule so the later sale is reported correctly.

## The 3.8% net investment income tax

REIT dividends are net investment income. The **3.8%** net investment income tax (NIIT) applies once modified AGI exceeds **$200,000** for a single taxpayer or **$250,000** for married filing jointly. Ordinary, qualified, and capital-gain components are NII. Box 3 return of capital is not current income, so it is not exposed to NIIT until it becomes recognized gain.

For a high-income client, NIIT sits on top of the ordinary or capital-gain rate. Section 199A reduces income tax on the qualified REIT-dividend slice; it does not reduce the NIIT base. Verify the filing-year threshold figures before finalizing the return.

## UBTI and retirement accounts

For an IRA, solo 401(k), or other tax-exempt account, a REIT has a structural advantage: it can act as a UBTI blocker. A REIT is a corporation for tax purposes, so its dividends generally are not unrelated business taxable income to the IRA or tax-exempt owner, even if the REIT carries substantial debt. The corporate wrapper contains the leverage.

That differs from a direct interest in a leveraged operating partnership or a typical real-estate syndication. Those can pass UBTI and UDFI—unrelated debt-financed income—through to an IRA, potentially requiring Form 990-T and tax inside the retirement account at trust rates. For a client seeking real-estate exposure in a retirement account, a private REIT can therefore be cleaner than a partnership. Compare the structures in [private REIT versus syndication](private-reit-vs-syndication.html).

The practical contrast is clear. Inside an IRA, private-REIT dividends are generally not UBTI, the leverage stays at the corporate level, and Form 990-T is typically unnecessary. A leveraged partnership can pass K-1 income and debt-financed income directly to the account; once UBTI exceeds **$1,000**, the account owes tax at trust rates and files Form 990-T. The details vary, so use the offering documents and a UBTI analysis.

## 1031 ineligibility and the 721 path

Raise this issue early: REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property. Public, non-traded, and private REIT shares can never be Section 1031 replacement property. Buying shares with real-estate sale proceeds does not defer the underlying sale; it leaves the sale fully taxable.

The deferred route into a REIT is a two-step path. First, the client completes a [1031 exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust](1031-exchange-into-dst.html), whose fractional real-property interest is 1031 eligible. Later, if the sponsor offers it, the DST property can be contributed to the REIT operating partnership in a Section 721 exchange for OP units. The contribution is tax-deferred, but the reporting changes: OP units are a partnership interest reported on a K-1, not a 1099-DIV. The mechanics are covered in the [CPA guide to 721 exchanges](cpa-guide-721-exchange.html), the [1031-to-721 walkthrough](1031-to-721-exchange.html), and [OP units explained](op-units-explained.html).

The 721 contribution is generally a one-way door. Conversion of OP units to REIT shares—or redemption by the REIT—is a taxable event that recognizes deferred gain. From there the investor owns a security, and selling it is taxable as with stock. “I will just 1031 into a REIT” is a misconception worth correcting before anyone relies on it.

## State tax and withholding notes

State treatment is not automatically federal treatment. States commonly start with federal taxable income, but their Section 199A conformity differs; some do not allow the **20%** deduction at the state level, leaving the benefit federal-only for a client. States also vary in their treatment of capital-gain distributions and return of capital. A client whose REIT holdings are tied to several property states can have filing or withholding exposure in those states.

Non-traded and private-REIT sponsors can also withhold or issue state-specific reporting for OP-unit holders after a 721. Check the resident state and each state in which the REIT holds property rather than assuming conformity.

## Client due-diligence checklist

1. Reconcile each 1099-DIV box: ordinary **1a**, qualified **1b**, capital gain **2a**, Section 1250 **2b**, Section 199A **5**, and return of capital **3**.
2. Claim the Box 5 Section 199A deduction on Form 8995 or 8995-A. It has no W-2/UBIA or income-threshold limit.
3. Maintain a basis schedule for every position, reduce it by every Box 3 amount, and flag a position before basis reaches zero.
4. Run the NIIT analysis near or above **$200k** single / **$250k** MFJ.
5. For IRA and tax-exempt clients, compare the REIT UBTI blocker with a partnership alternative that could produce Form 990-T.
6. Correct the 1031 premise: REIT shares are ineligible. The tax-deferred route is [DST](dst-guide.html) first, then 721.
7. For a private or non-traded REIT, verify accredited status and examine the PPM, redemption terms, valuation cadence, and fees. See [how to invest in a private REIT](how-to-invest-in-a-private-reit.html).

## Reporting workflow: what to give the CPA at filing

Request a specific annual package: the consolidated 1099-DIV from every REIT or brokerage, including corrected forms; the prior-year basis schedule for each position; purchase confirmations and redemption statements; and, if the client now owns OP units, the K-1 plus the original 721-exchange and DST-closing documents that establish carryover basis. Retain offering documents for private and non-traded REITs in case suitability or a redemption gate becomes relevant.

REITs often finalize box allocations after year-end, so amended 1099s are possible. Budget for a superseding or extended return. For broader context, pair this work with the [CPA guide to 1031 exchanges](cpa-guide-1031-exchange.html) and the [REITs guide](reits-guide.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How are REIT dividends taxed?

Mostly as ordinary income on Form 1099-DIV. The qualified REIT-dividend portion in Box 5 generally receives the **20%** Section 199A deduction. A distribution can also include Box 2a capital gain, Box 2b unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, and Box 3 return of capital.

### Does the Section 199A deduction apply to REIT dividends, and is it permanent?

Yes. Box 5 qualified REIT dividends receive the **20%** deduction on Form 8995 or Form 8995-A without W-2/UBIA limits or an income threshold. The **2025** One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the deduction permanent.

### How does return of capital from a REIT affect basis?

Box 3 is not currently taxed but lowers basis. Once basis reaches zero, later return of capital is capital gain; the lower basis also increases gain on a future sale. It is deferral, not free yield.

### Are REIT dividends subject to the 3.8% NIIT?

Yes. They are net investment income, so the **3.8%** NIIT applies once modified AGI exceeds **$200,000** single or **$250,000** married filing jointly. Section 199A lowers income tax, not the NIIT base.

### Are REITs good for IRA investors?

Often they are. The corporate REIT wrapper generally blocks UBTI for an IRA or tax-exempt owner, unlike a leveraged operating partnership that can produce UBTI/UDFI and a Form 990-T obligation.

### Can a client 1031 into a REIT?

No. REIT shares are securities rather than like-kind real property. A tax-deferred route is a 1031 exchange into a DST followed later by a 721 exchange into the REIT operating partnership for OP units.

### How is a 721 exchange into a REIT reported?

The 721 contribution is generally tax-deferred. The client then owns OP units, a partnership interest reported on Schedule K-1 rather than Form 1099-DIV. Converting OP units to REIT shares or having them redeemed recognizes the deferred gain.

### How does a DST report to the investor?

A DST is treated as a grantor trust under Rev. Rul. 2004-86. It reports on a 1099 plus a grantor letter rather than a K-1, preserving the direct-real-property treatment that allows the later 721 path.

## Glossary

- **Form 1099-DIV:** The form reporting ordinary **1a**, qualified **1b**, capital-gain **2a**, unrecaptured Section 1250 **2b**, Section 199A **5**, and return-of-capital **3** components.
- **Section 199A REIT Dividends:** Box 5 qualified REIT dividends eligible for the **20%** deduction without W-2/UBIA or income-threshold limits; made permanent by the 2025 law.
- **Return of Capital:** A Box 3 nondividend distribution that reduces basis dollar for dollar and becomes capital gain once basis reaches zero.
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain:** The depreciation-related portion of Box 2a, reported in Box 2b, and taxed at up to **25%**.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** The **3.8%** tax on net investment income, including REIT dividends, above modified AGI of **$200,000** single or **$250,000** MFJ.
- **UBTI Blocker:** A REIT's corporate ability to shield an IRA or tax-exempt owner from unrelated business taxable income.
- **721 Exchange:** A tax-deferred property contribution to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, generally after a 1031 exchange into a DST.
- **Form 8995 / 8995-A:** The forms used to calculate and claim the Section 199A deduction, including the qualified REIT-dividend component.

## A practical stance before filing or allocating capital

I would manage this by beginning with the boxes, not the yield: reconcile the Form 1099-DIV, maintain the basis schedule, run NIIT and UBTI checks where they apply, and make the 1031-versus-721 distinction before a client commits proceeds. The near-term distribution can look attractive while the more important result is sitting in basis, liquidity, and the future taxable exit.
