# How REITs Have Performed vs. the S&P 500

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing a long-term return chart used as if it settles the question. It rarely does. A chart can show that listed real estate and broad equities have both rewarded patient capital at different times, but it cannot tell an investor what will be rewarded next.

This is a balanced look at how REITs have performed versus the S&P 500: their long-run total returns, the large contribution from dividends, their moderate correlation with stocks, and the caveats that make any neat answer suspect. It is educational information, not a recommendation or a forecast.

The first-order view is simple: over long, multi-decade periods, REITs have historically delivered total returns that are broadly competitive with broad equities, while producing a large share of that return through dividends. The second-order view matters more: the result changes with the dates selected, REITs are volatile and rate-sensitive, and past performance does not guarantee future results. No historical comparison is a promise.

## Long-Run REIT Returns

Long-run REIT returns combine dividend income and price appreciation. The REIT structure requires the distribution of most taxable income, so income commonly carries more of the total-return load than it does for the broad stock market. Underlying properties can also appreciate as rents and property values grow. Taken together, those forces have produced meaningful and historically competitive total returns for the REIT sector over multi-decade spans.

That description should not be turned into a fixed return figure. An individual REIT can have a very different experience, and so can any short window. Rent growth, occupancy, development, the cost and availability of capital, and interest rates all change. Long-run averages smooth over strong years, weak years, and sharp drawdowns.

The useful reading of the record is competitive total returns from dividends plus appreciation, earned along a volatile, cycle-driven path. It is a story measured in decades, not quarters. No past average can be assumed to repeat.

## REITs vs. the S&P 500

The S&P 500 is the usual stand-in for U.S. large-cap stocks. Against that benchmark, REITs have been broadly competitive over long horizons. That matters because real estate is sometimes dismissed as merely an income play. The historical record says it has also been a total-return asset.

But there is no permanent winner. REITs have led in some stretches and clearly lagged in others. Their returns reflect real-estate fundamentals and interest rates, while the S&P 500 represents many industries. When growth-oriented stocks dominate, or when rising rates pressure REIT valuations, REITs can trail. In other economic and rate settings, they can lead.

So “competitive over the long run, but period-dependent” is more accurate than a claim that either asset always beats the other. Start and end dates can change the conclusion dramatically. A single “REITs beat stocks” or “stocks beat REITs” statement without dates is misleading.

## Correlation & Diversification

Return is only part of the decision. REITs have historically shown moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with the S&P 500. They are exchange-traded equities and do move with the broad market to some degree. Yet rents, occupancy, property values, and interest rates give them drivers that do not match the rest of the equity market exactly.

That imperfect co-movement is the case for diversification. Adding an asset that does not move in lockstep with an existing stock allocation can potentially smooth the path of a portfolio and add an income stream tied to real estate rather than the same influences driving other equities.

There is a limit worth stating plainly. In severe sell-offs, correlations across risk assets tend to rise. REITs can decline alongside stocks precisely when diversification is most wanted. Their diversification benefit is partial, not complete, and it is better judged over full cycles than in a single crisis.

> REITs do not need to promise higher returns to have a portfolio role. Less-than-perfect correlation can be useful, while still offering no protection from every market decline.

## Income Contribution

Dividends are central to REIT total return. A REIT generally must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income to keep its tax status. As a result, a meaningful and often substantial share of a REIT investor's long-run return arrives as income, generally a larger share than for the broad market, where price appreciation has historically supplied more of the return.

That changes the experience of ownership. Dividends can contribute even when prices are flat. Reinvesting them can compound over long horizons. And the income feature explains why REITs appeal to investors who value distributions.

It does not make the income fixed. Property income can fall and distributions can be cut. A high current yield can represent risk rather than reward. The income contribution is a defining strength of REITs, but it should always be evaluated for sustainability.

## Key Takeaways

- Over long, multi-decade periods, REITs have historically delivered total returns that are broadly competitive with broad equities such as the S&P 500.
- Dividends contribute a large share of REIT total return—generally more than for the broad stock market—so income is central to the asset class.
- Moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with the S&P 500 can support diversification, but the benefit is partial.
- Results vary sharply by period. REITs can be volatile and rate-sensitive, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Volatility and Rate Sensitivity

REITs are not bond-like instruments. They are listed equities, and their prices can move meaningfully. In sharp downturns they can fall as much as, or more than, the broad market even when underlying rents appear relatively stable. A low-looking long-run volatility figure can be misleading if it ignores the drawdowns that created it.

Interest rates are another important driver. Rising rates increase borrowing costs and raise the discount rates investors use for future cash flow, which can pressure REIT valuations. Falling rates often have the opposite effect. That rate sensitivity can make REITs underperform broad equities during rapid rate increases and outperform when rates ease.

This is part of the reason the comparison changes by period. Competitive long-run results have come with real ups and downs, including rate-cycle effects. An allocation should be sized with that path in mind, not only with the long-run endpoint in mind.

## Context and Caveats

Performance history is useful when it sets expectations. It is dangerous when it becomes a forecast. The first-order reaction to a strong historical comparison is to expect more of the same. The second-order question is what was priced in, what interest-rate and property cycle conditions produced the record, and whether an investor is mistaking a past average for a future contract.

Historical competitiveness, dividend contribution, and moderate correlation describe the past. Future results will reflect changing real-estate fundamentals, interest rates, valuations, and market conditions. REITs can be volatile and rate-sensitive, and the dates selected for a comparison can determine which asset looks better.

Use the record to understand the character of the asset class: income-oriented, equity-like, partially diversifying, and exposed to the rate and property cycles. Then use diversification and appropriate position sizing rather than a specific return target.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Have REITs outperformed the S&P 500?

Over long, multi-decade periods, REITs have historically delivered total returns broadly competitive with the S&P 500. Whether they have “outperformed” depends heavily on the particular period measured. They have led in some stretches and lagged in others; there is no rule that either one always wins. Real-estate fundamentals and interest rates affect REITs differently from the forces driving the rest of the equity market. The honest summary is competitive over the long run but period-dependent. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and no historical comparison should be assumed to repeat.

### Why do dividends matter so much for REIT returns?

REITs generally distribute at least **90%** of taxable income, so a large share of their total return arrives as income rather than price appreciation. Dividends have historically been a substantial, often majority, component of REIT total return and generally a larger contribution than for the broad stock market. They can provide return when prices are flat and can compound when reinvested. They are not guaranteed: property income can decline, distributions can be cut, and an unusually high yield can signal risk rather than reward.

### What is REITs' correlation with the stock market?

REITs have historically had moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with the S&P 500. They move with stocks to some degree because they are traded equities, but rents, occupancy, property values, and interest rates give them additional drivers. This can aid diversification by pairing assets that do not move exactly together. The benefit is incomplete. In severe market sell-offs, correlations tend to rise and REITs can fall alongside stocks.

### Are REITs more volatile than stocks?

REITs are genuinely volatile equities. Whether they are more or less volatile than the S&P 500 varies by period and by REIT type; mortgage REITs, for example, tend to be more volatile than many equity REITs. In sharp market declines, REITs can fall as much as or more than broad stocks despite stable rents and properties. Treat them as holdings with meaningful price swings and real drawdown risk, not as low-volatility substitutes for bonds.

### How do interest rates affect REIT performance vs. stocks?

Rising rates increase REIT borrowing costs and raise the discount rates applied to future cash flows, which can pressure valuations and share prices. Rising bond yields can also make REIT yields look less attractive. Falling rates often help. Mortgage REITs are especially sensitive because their economics depend on an interest-rate spread. The S&P 500 is also affected by rates, but REITs' real-estate and leverage profile can make them particularly responsive, so relative performance can change substantially from one rate setting to another.

### Do REITs improve portfolio diversification?

They can. Moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with broad stocks and bonds, plus exposure to income-producing real estate, can modestly improve diversification over full cycles. The risk-reduction benefit is not guaranteed. REITs are equities, they are rate-sensitive, and correlations often rise during severe sell-offs. A REIT allocation can complement a broader portfolio; it does not make the portfolio safe or insulate it from a broad crash.

### Why does the comparison vary so much by period?

REIT returns reflect real-estate fundamentals and interest rates, which do not move in step with the broad market. Growth-stock leadership can favor the S&P 500; settings favoring income and real assets, or falling rates, can favor REITs. Property cycles, including construction booms and busts and changing demand for office, industrial, or data-center space, also matter. The start and end dates can therefore change the verdict. A period must be stated before any comparison can be read responsibly.

### Should I expect REITs to match historical returns?

No. Competitive historical total returns, a large income component, and moderate correlation are context, not a forecast. Future results depend on real-estate fundamentals, interest rates, valuations, and market conditions that change over time. The appropriate use of history is to set modest expectations about an income-oriented, rate-sensitive, volatile equity asset class, then diversify and size the allocation appropriately. No specific return should be expected or relied upon.

### Are REIT total returns mostly income or growth?

REIT total return combines dividend income and price appreciation. Income has historically been an unusually large and often majority share compared with the broad market because REITs distribute most taxable income. Properties can also appreciate as rents and values rise. Both components are variable. Distributions can be cut and prices can decline, so neither should be treated as fixed.

### Do REITs fall during market crashes?

Yes. REITs are exchange-traded equities and can decline sharply with broad-market sentiment and risk appetite. During a severe sell-off, correlations across risk assets tend to rise, so REITs often fall with stocks even if underlying rents and properties have not changed as much. Leverage, refinancing, and tenant-default concerns can add pressure. The diversification case is best judged over a full cycle, not by expecting REITs to hold up in every crash.

### Is the S&P 500 a fair benchmark for REITs?

It is a common and reasonable benchmark because it represents broad U.S. large-cap equities competing for investor capital. It is not an apples-to-apples comparison. REITs are a single real-estate sector with particular sensitivity to property fundamentals and interest rates, while the S&P 500 spans many industries. A broad REIT index is a more direct sector measure. The comparison is most useful over long horizons with the period stated clearly.

### How should historical performance inform my REIT allocation?

Use it modestly. The record can help an investor expect competitive but bumpy returns, meaningful income, and partial diversification. It can help frame a REIT sleeve's role alongside—not instead of—a broader equity position. It should not be used to extrapolate a past average, chase a strong recent stretch, or set a concentrated allocation. Reinvest income when it is not needed, avoid concentration, size the sleeve according to goals and risk tolerance, and revisit it as conditions and circumstances change.

### Are non-traded REITs comparable to the S&P 500?

The comparison needs care. A non-traded REIT is periodically valued at net asset value (NAV), not continuously by the market. Its reported returns can look smoother and less volatile than a daily-marked index such as the S&P 500, but that smoothness largely reflects infrequent valuation rather than the absence of underlying risk. Property value can still decline between valuations, and NAV can lag market conditions. Historically higher fees and illiquidity also affect net returns. Evaluate a non-traded REIT's structure, fees, and real estate rather than treating its smoothed NAV series as directly comparable.

### Does adding REITs to stocks reduce risk?

It can modestly reduce overall portfolio risk over full cycles because assets that do not move exactly together can smooth the portfolio's path relative to the return earned. REITs add real-estate exposure and an income stream with drivers different from other equities. But the benefit is partial. REITs are volatile, rate-sensitive stocks that often fall with the broad market in severe sell-offs. They can support diversification, not guarantee lower risk.

## Glossary

### Total Return

The combined return from dividends plus price appreciation.

### S&P 500

The standard index of large-cap U.S. stocks.

### REIT Index

A benchmark tracking the performance of the REIT sector.

### Correlation

How closely two assets' returns move together.

### Moderate Correlation

Less-than-perfect co-movement that aids diversification.

### Diversification

Combining assets that do not move in lockstep to manage risk.

### Dividend Contribution

The share of total return coming from dividends.

### Dividend Reinvestment

Using distributions to buy more shares and compound.

### Volatility

The degree of variation in an asset's returns over time.

### Drawdown

A peak-to-trough decline in value during a downturn.

### Rate Sensitivity

How a REIT's value responds to interest-rate changes.

### Period Dependence

How results change based on the dates compared.

### Past Performance Caveat

The principle that history does not guarantee future results.

### 90% Distribution Rule

The payout requirement that drives REITs' large income share.

### Equity REIT

A REIT that owns property and earns income from rents.

### Mortgage REIT (mREIT)

A rate-sensitive REIT that finances real estate for a spread.

## Which should you choose?

This is less an either/or decision than a question of role. REITs have historically offered competitive long-run total returns, a larger income contribution, and less-than-perfect correlation with the broad market. That is why investors may hold them alongside an S&P 500 position, not instead of it.

An investor may weight toward REITs when income and diversification are priorities. The offset is greater rate sensitivity and potentially greater stress-period volatility. Past performance does not guarantee future results, so role and position size should matter more than an attempt to select a historical winner.
