# Recession-Resistant REIT Sectors

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I have been thinking about how quickly “defensive” becomes “safe” in an anxious market. It should not. A sector can have steadier demand in a recession and still face leverage, rate, supply, tenant, and valuation risk.

REIT property types react differently when growth weakens. Sectors serving needs-based, non-discretionary demand often have steadier occupancy and income than sectors tied to travel, corporate expansion, or discretionary consumer spending. This guide identifies the sectors commonly viewed as more resilient, explains lease length and diversification, and sets the limits of a defensive tilt.

First-order thinking is to buy the sector with the safest label. Second-order thinking asks what drives demand, how quickly revenue resets, whether tenants can pay, how much debt matures, what the price already reflects, and where concentration remains. Recession resistance is a degree of resilience, not an immunity claim.

For broader context, read our [2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## What Makes a Sector Defensive

A sector tends to be defensive when demand for its space is needs-based and non-discretionary: people and businesses use it through both strong and weak economies. When demand persists, occupancy is more stable, rents hold up better, and income can be steadier.

Several features help. Housing, healthcare, and storage associated with life events have demand less tied to the cycle than luxury travel or high-end shopping. A low expense relative to a tenant’s budget—such as a modest storage unit or a grocery-anchored center—can be easier to keep paying. Long, contracted leases can lock income in advance. No one trait is enough by itself, but needs-based demand, stable occupancy, manageable tenant cost, and contracted rent form a useful frame.

## Storage, Healthcare & Necessity Retail

Self-storage is often viewed as relatively defensive because demand follows life events: moving, downsizing, divorce, a death in the family, a new job, and business needs. Those events occur in every economy, and the monthly cost is often low relative to a household budget. Some events, such as downsizing or relocation, can even increase during a downturn.

Healthcare real estate, including medical office, senior housing, and related facilities, draws support from demographics and the ongoing need for medical care. It is needs-based, though operators, reimbursement conditions, staffing, and property-specific circumstances still matter.

Necessity retail means grocery-anchored centers and properties with pharmacies, discount stores, and essential-service tenants. Grocery shopping, prescriptions, and basic services tend to continue when consumers cut discretionary spending, supporting steadier traffic and occupancy than malls or centers focused on non-essential categories. [Residential real estate](best-reit-sectors-residential-industrial-healthcare-and-more.html) belongs in this group as well: people need housing, although oversupply, job losses, local rents, and interest rates can still hurt results.

## Lease Length as a Buffer

Lease structure matters beside sector selection. Long, contracted leases can protect near-term income from a weakening economy. Net-lease real estate often uses leases of **ten years or more**, with a tenant covering many property costs. The contracted rent can continue while the cycle weakens because the tenant is bound to pay under the lease.

Short-duration income resets faster. Hotels effectively re-lease rooms nightly. Storage often rents month to month. That can help in expansions and can expose revenue quickly in a downturn. A recession in the current year may not affect much of a long-lease REIT’s income until leases roll years later. Lease length is therefore an important source of buffer, not a guarantee of tenant performance or renewal.

## Sectors to Approach Cautiously

Hotels and lodging are highly cyclical. Business travel, conferences, and leisure trips can decline when budgets tighten, and nightly repricing makes weakness show up quickly. The same feature can create strong upside in expansions.

Office can also be vulnerable when companies shrink, freeze hiring, or [Office demand can soften when companies shrink, freeze hiring, or adopt remote and hybrid work](office-reits-in-a-hybrid-work-world.html). Long leases may defer visible weakness until a rollover or non-renewal. Discretionary retail, such as malls and centers dependent on apparel, electronics, dining, and other non-essential spending, can suffer when households cut those expenses first.

These sectors are not automatically uninvestable. They can perform well in the right cycle and at valuations that compensate for risk. The point is to recognize that hotels, office, and discretionary retail have more economically sensitive demand than needs-based property types.

## Key Takeaways

- A defensive sector has needs-based, non-discretionary demand, relatively stable occupancy, and often income buffered by long leases.
- Self-storage, healthcare, necessity retail, and residential are commonly viewed as more defensive because their demand persists through the cycle.
- Long, contracted leases such as net leases can buffer income, while short-duration sectors such as hotels reprice quickly with conditions.
- Hotels, office, and discretionary retail tend to be more cyclical. No sector is recession-proof, so diversify and size exposure carefully.

## Diversifying Within and Across Sectors

No defensive sector or individual REIT is immune. A REIT concentrated in one property type and a few markets can still be hurt by a regional downturn, a sector-specific event, an oversupply problem, or the weakness of one large tenant. Spread exposure across several demand drivers—for example, residential, healthcare, and necessity retail—so one problem does not control the entire result.

Diversification also applies across individual REITs and structures. A diversified REIT fund or ETF can supply breadth across sectors; a single traded or non-traded REIT concentrates exposure in that manager’s portfolio. Combining long-lease net-lease income with shorter-duration residential and healthcare can reduce the chance that one economic event dominates income. The durable defense is breadth, not one perfect sector.

## Building a Defensive Sleeve

A defensive sleeve tilts a REIT allocation toward needs-based, well-leased sectors: residential, healthcare, self-storage, necessity retail, and possibly contracted net-lease income. The aim is steadier income and shallower drawdowns in a downturn, while accepting that a tilt may also give up some upside in a strong expansion.

It remains part of a broader portfolio. Size it to goals, risk tolerance, and horizon; diversify across sectors, REITs, and structures rather than concentrating in one supposedly safe issuer. Defensive sectors can still face oversupply, rising costs, tenant distress, interest-rate pressure, reduced distributions, share-price movement, and NAV declines. A defensive tilt reduces risk; it does not eliminate it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What makes a REIT sector recession-resistant?

Needs-based, non-discretionary demand is the core. When people and businesses continue using space through a downturn, occupancy and income tend to be steadier. Low cost relative to the tenant budget and long, contracted leases can add resilience. That describes a tendency, not an assurance: property markets, tenants, debt, and valuations still matter.

### Which REIT sectors are considered most defensive?

Residential, healthcare, self-storage, and necessity retail are commonly cited. Housing is foundational; medical office, senior housing, and related facilities serve continuing care needs; storage follows life events; and grocery-anchored centers serve essential shopping. Their demand persists more than discretionary demand, but each remains exposed to sector-specific risks.

### How does lease length affect recession resistance?

Long, contractually locked leases can insulate income from near-term economic weakness. Net-lease properties often have a tenant on a lease of **ten years or more** and the tenant covers many costs. Hotels reprice nightly and storage often rents monthly, so short income can move quickly with conditions. Long leases buffer cash flow but do not ensure a tenant remains able to pay.

### Are hotels and office REITs riskier in a recession?

Generally they are more economically sensitive. Hotels lose business, conference, and leisure travel as budgets tighten and reprice immediately. Office demand can weaken with corporate contraction, hiring freezes, and remote or hybrid work; long rollovers can hide the impact until they arrive. Both can still have opportunity and expansion upside, but their recession risk is higher than needs-based sectors.

### Is any REIT sector truly recession-proof?

No. Residential can face oversupply or job-market weakness; healthcare can face reimbursement and operator stress; storage can face new construction; necessity retail can face tenant bankruptcies. Distributions can be cut and share prices or NAV can fall. “More resilient” does not mean immune.

### Why is self-storage considered defensive?

Its demand commonly arises from life events—moving, downsizing, divorce, a death in the family, marriage, a new job, or a business needing space—that happen in all economies. The cost is often low enough relative to a household budget that a monthly unit is not the first expense eliminated. Occupancy and rents can still suffer from new supply, local conditions, or weak demand, so it is not risk-free.

### How does healthcare real estate hold up in downturns?

Healthcare demand is tied to demographics and continuing medical need. Medical office, senior housing, and some related facilities can therefore have steadier occupancy and income through a cycle. Healthcare property still depends on operators, reimbursement, costs, tenants, and execution. Demographic demand is support, not a guarantee.

### Is residential a safe REIT sector in a recession?

Residential is relatively resilient because housing need is among the least discretionary. Apartments, single-family rentals, and manufactured housing can retain demand, and weaker home buying may push households toward renting. A weak job market, rent pressure, local oversupply, rising rates, and reduced rent growth can still hurt. Diversify across markets and size the allocation appropriately rather than treating housing as fully safe.

### What is necessity retail and why is it more resilient?

It is retail anchored by essential goods and services: grocery stores, pharmacies, discount retailers, and service tenants. Consumers keep buying groceries and prescriptions through the cycle, supporting traffic and occupancy more than discretionary retail, where apparel, electronics, and dining can be cut first. Necessity retail can still face bankruptcies, e-commerce competition, and local weakness.

### Should I avoid cyclical REIT sectors entirely?

Not necessarily. Hotels, office, and discretionary retail have more downturn risk, but can offer expansion upside and may trade at prices that reflect that risk. A defensive approach usually tilts toward needs-based, well-leased sectors rather than banning cyclicals. A modest, diversified cyclical allocation can add upside and breadth when it is understood and sized for the investor’s horizon and risk tolerance.

### How do interest rates affect defensive REIT sectors?

Rates affect all REITs. Higher borrowing costs, stronger competing bond yields, and higher capitalization rates can pressure values and share prices or NAV. That risk is separate from demand resilience in a recession. A needs-based sector with stable occupancy can still decline in a rising-rate environment. Long, fixed-income net leases can be especially rate-sensitive because rents do not reset quickly. Consider both economic-cycle risk and interest-rate risk.

### How many REIT sectors should I hold for diversification?

There is no fixed count. The goal is enough breadth across different demand drivers that one sector or REIT cannot dominate the result. A defensive-minded allocation might include residential, healthcare, necessity retail, self-storage, and some long-lease net-lease income. A diversified REIT fund or ETF can deliver broad exposure. No sector or manager is recession-proof, so overall sizing still matters.

### Do non-traded REITs offer recession-resistant sectors?

They can invest in the same needs-based sectors as traded REITs, including residential, healthcare, and necessity retail. Sector resilience applies regardless of trading format. The non-traded structure adds separate considerations: illiquidity, capped and suspendable redemption programs, historically higher fees, and periodic NAV rather than continuous market pricing. Evaluate the sector and the structure, and confirm suitability for accredited or otherwise suitable investors.

### Can a defensive REIT still cut its distribution in a recession?

Yes. Stable demand can reduce the severity of income stress but cannot promise payments. Occupancy and collection can weaken; leverage and debt maturities can create pressure; rising interest cost can squeeze cash flow; and management can conserve capital. Review balance sheet, payout ratio, lease structure, and tenant quality alongside sector exposure. Past distributions do not guarantee future ones.

## Glossary

- **Defensive Sector:** A REIT sector with needs-based demand that holds up better in downturns.
- **Needs-Based Demand:** Non-discretionary demand for space that persists through the cycle.
- **Cyclical Sector:** A sector whose demand rises and falls sharply with the economy.
- **Self-Storage:** A defensive sector driven by life events and low monthly cost.
- **Healthcare Real Estate:** Medical office and senior housing tied to demographic demand.
- **Necessity Retail:** Grocery-anchored, essential-service retail that holds up better.
- **Residential:** Housing real estate, with among the least discretionary demand.
- **Net Lease:** A long-term lease where the tenant covers many property costs.
- **Lease Length:** The contracted duration that buffers income against downturns.
- **Occupancy:** The share of leased space, a key driver of REIT income.
- **Hotels / Lodging:** A highly cyclical sector that reprices room demand nightly.
- **Discretionary Retail:** Retail dependent on non-essential spending consumers cut first.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across sectors and REITs to reduce risk.
- **Defensive Sleeve:** A portion tilted toward needs-based, well-leased sectors.
- **Interest-Rate Risk:** The pressure rising rates put on all REIT valuations.
- **Distribution Risk:** The risk that even a defensive REIT cuts its dividend.
