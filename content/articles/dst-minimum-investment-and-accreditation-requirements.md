# DST Minimum Investment & Accreditation Requirements


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the same early question: “What is the minimum?” It sounds like a price question. It is really an eligibility-and-allocation question.

First-order thinking treats a low minimum as a reason to invest. Second-order thinking asks what the current offering requires, whether you qualify, how the position fits your equity and debt replacement, and whether the illiquidity is sensible for you. Before investing in a Delaware Statutory Trust, those are the facts worth getting straight.

DSTs can provide fractional access to institutional-quality real estate at far lower minimums than buying a building outright, but they are not open to everyone or available at any amount. DST interests are securities, generally offered under Regulation D to accredited investors; the minimum differs by offering and can differ for a 1031 exchanger and a cash investor. ## Typical DST Minimum Investments

Rather than buying an entire building, an investor buys a fractional beneficial interest in a trust that owns the real estate. Typical DST minimums are often roughly $25,000 to $100,000, although sponsor and offering terms control and some thresholds are higher. That can give an individual access to real estate they likely could not acquire alone.

The floor has a practical purpose. A DST has a limited investor base and administering a large number of tiny interests is inefficient. The same fractional structure can make diversification possible: capital can be divided among several DSTs, sectors, and markets rather than put into one property. Confirm each minimum in the [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html) and plan commitments around the actual terms, not a common range.

## Accredited Investor Definition

DST interests offered under Regulation D are generally limited to [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html)s. The SEC’s familiar individual tests are income above $200,000 in each of the two most recent years, or $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, with a reasonable expectation of the same this year; or net worth above $1 million, individually or jointly, excluding the primary residence. Either test generally qualifies an investor.

There are other paths: specified professional licenses, including Series 7, Series 65, or Series 82; knowledgeable employees of certain funds; and entity categories meeting asset or ownership tests. The purpose is investor protection. Private, illiquid offerings do not have the full disclosure regime of public securities, and the standard is meant to identify people with the sophistication or financial cushion to bear the risks. It is a threshold, not a verdict on an investor’s intelligence or worthiness.

## Verifying Accreditation

Qualifying and proving qualification are separate issues. Many DSTs use Rule 506(b), under which the issuer may rely on an investor questionnaire self-certifying income or net worth when there is no reason for doubt. That lower-friction route requires a pre-existing relationship and prohibits general solicitation.

Rule 506(c) permits solicitation and advertising, but the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify that every investor is accredited. Self-certification alone is not enough. Verification can mean tax returns, W-2s, brokerage or bank statements, or a written confirmation from a CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser, broker-dealer, or third-party verification service. Expect documentation or a verification letter for a 506(c) offering.

## Minimums for 1031 vs. Cash Investors

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) investor places proceeds from a sold property into a DST to defer capital-gains tax; a cash investor invests available cash without an exchange. Both buy the same beneficial interests, but sizing and minimums can differ. An exchanger’s amount is often driven by the relinquished property’s value, equity, and debt rather than by a round-dollar minimum.

Some sponsors set a higher exchange minimum or accommodate exact exchange amounts. Cash investors can have a lower minimum and more sizing flexibility. Exchangers must also plan for equity and debt replacement, the 45- and 180-day deadlines, and a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html). Trust-level non-recourse debt can matter to mortgage replacement. Confirm the offering’s minimum and structure for your situation.

## Key Takeaways

- Typical DST minimums often run roughly $25,000 to $100,000, vary by offering, and provide fractional access to institutional real estate.
- Most DSTs require accredited investors: income above $200,000 single / $300,000 joint, or net worth above $1 million excluding the primary residence.
- Rule 506(b) generally uses self-certification; Rule 506(c) requires documented third-party verification.
- Exchange and cash minimums can differ. Exchange sizing also reflects equity and debt replacement.

## Planning Your Allocation

Low relative minimums can let an investor split an investment or exchange among multifamily, industrial, and net-lease retail DSTs instead of concentrating in one building. Diversification can reduce the effect of one weak property, tenant, or market. It does not eliminate risk.

Plan around total capital, each offering’s minimum, desired sectors and markets, sponsor track record, fees, debt, and hold period. A 1031 allocation must replace the appropriate equity and debt within the 45- and 180-day windows. It should also fit the investor’s time horizon, risk tolerance, and liquidity needs; DSTs are illiquid for the hold. A broker-dealer and CPA can help address the investment and tax sides.

## Getting Started and Confirming Eligibility

Start by confirming accredited status and gathering documentation, especially for a Rule 506(c) offering. Then determine whether the capital is exchange proceeds or cash. A 1031 exchanger should engage a qualified intermediary before selling the relinquished property and identify DSTs early.

Review suitable offerings with a broker-dealer. Confirm each minimum and structure in the PPM and complete a suitability review of financial situation, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. Plan one or more positions to diversify and, if exchanging, meet the equity-and-debt math within the 45- and 180-day windows. Distributions and returns are projections, not guarantees; your CPA and attorney address the tax and legal specifics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the typical minimum investment in a DST?

Often roughly $25,000 to $100,000, although each sponsor and offering sets its own figure and some are higher. The investment is a fractional beneficial interest, not the purchase of an entire building. Read the PPM to confirm the live minimum; it also determines how finely capital can be divided among DSTs.

### Do I have to be an accredited investor to invest in a DST?

Generally yes. Regulation D private placements typically limit DST interests to accredited investors. Common paths are the income and net-worth tests; Series 7, 65, or 82, knowledgeable-employee, and entity paths can also apply. The standard addresses the risks of private, illiquid investments that lack the full public-securities disclosure regime.

### What are the accredited investor income and net-worth thresholds?

Income must exceed $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly in each of the two most recent years, with a reasonable expectation of the same this year; or net worth must exceed $1 million, individually or jointly, excluding the primary residence. Primary-home value is excluded, as is mortgage debt up to fair market value; an underwater portion counts as a liability. The SEC can update its rules, so confirm the current test.

### How is accreditation verified for a DST?

Rule 506(b) commonly relies on a subscription-questionnaire representation, absent a reason to doubt it, and requires a pre-existing relationship without general solicitation. Rule 506(c) requires reasonable verification: tax returns, W-2s, brokerage or bank statements, or a CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser, broker-dealer, or verification-service letter. The verification protects both investor and issuer.

### What is the difference between Rule 506(b) and 506(c)?

Both are Regulation D exemptions. Rule 506(b) prohibits general solicitation, permits self-certification when appropriate, and can allow a limited number of sophisticated non-accredited investors, though most DSTs use accredited investors. Rule 506(c) can solicit broadly but requires every investor to be verified accredited. The practical difference is whether you self-certify or provide documents or a verification letter.

### Are DST minimums different for 1031 exchangers and cash investors?

They can be. Exchange investors must match equity and debt and meet the 45- and 180-day deadlines, so sponsors may use a higher minimum or tailor sizing to exchange proceeds. Cash investors can have a lower floor and more flexibility. Confirm the current minimum, non-recourse debt structure, and whether capital is exchange proceeds or cash.

### Why do DSTs require accredited investors?

They are private Regulation D securities. The accreditation standard is designed to match offerings with illiquidity, no investor control, sponsor and market risk, fees, and concentration risk to investors presumed better able to evaluate and bear a potential loss. It is a regulatory threshold based on income, net worth, licenses, or entity status.

### Can I spread one investment across multiple DSTs?

Yes. Minimums often make it possible to divide one investment or exchange among multifamily, industrial, and net-lease retail positions. This can reduce concentration in one property, tenant, or market and can help an exchanger deploy equity and debt. Each DST still has its own sponsor, fees, debt, and hold; diversification is a balance between reduced concentration and manageability.

### How should I plan my allocation across DSTs?

Begin with the total amount and each DST’s minimum. Consider the number of DSTs, sectors, markets, sponsors’ full-cycle histories, fees, debt, and hold periods. For an exchange, replace the appropriate equity and debt within 45 and 180 days. The allocation should fit goals, risk tolerance, and liquidity needs, with advice from the broker-dealer and CPA where appropriate.

### What documents do I need to verify accreditation?

A 506(b) investor generally completes a self-certification questionnaire. A 506(c) investor can expect tax returns, W-2s, or 1099s for income; bank, brokerage, and other account statements plus a credit report and liabilities for net worth; or a qualified third-party letter. Confirm the offering’s requirements before investing.

### Is the primary residence included in the net-worth test?

No. The primary residence is excluded from net worth, and related mortgage debt is generally excluded up to the home’s fair market value. Any underwater amount is a liability, and a look-back rule prevents inflating the calculation by borrowing against the home shortly before investing. Count investable and other assets, subtract liabilities, and confirm close cases with an advisor.

### Can a trust or business entity invest in a DST?

Yes. Corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and certain trusts can qualify with assets above $5 million when not formed solely to acquire securities; entities whose equity owners are all accredited and certain sophisticated-person trusts and family offices can also qualify. For a 1031 exchange, the same taxpayer that sold the relinquished property generally must acquire the replacement, so entity accreditation and titling require CPA, attorney, and broker-dealer coordination.

### Are DST minimums the same at every sponsor?

No. They vary by sponsor and offering and can differ by exchange versus cash capital. Typical ranges do not control a live deal. The PPM provides the actual term, which in turn affects how many positions an investor can use in an allocation.

### What if I am not an accredited investor?

Most DSTs will not be available. Confirm the calculation first, since income, net worth, license, and entity details matter. Publicly traded REITs and certain registered non-traded vehicles can be alternatives, though they differ materially from DSTs and REIT shares are not 1031-eligible. Discuss a fitting approach with a broker-dealer.

## Glossary

**Minimum Investment** — The smallest amount a DST offering accepts from an investor.

**Fractional Beneficial Interest** — An investor's share of a DST that holds the real estate.

**Accredited Investor** — An investor meeting SEC income, net-worth, or other tests.

**Income Test** — Income over $200,000 single or $300,000 joint for two years.

**Net-Worth Test** — Net worth over $1 million, excluding the primary residence.

**Regulation D** — The SEC rules under which DSTs are privately offered.

**Rule 506(b)** — A Reg D exemption allowing self-certification, no advertising.

**Rule 506(c)** — A Reg D exemption allowing advertising with verified accreditation.

**Self-Certification** — An investor's representation of accredited status via questionnaire.

**Third-Party Verification** — A CPA, attorney, or service confirming accredited status for 506(c).

**1031 Investor** — An exchanger placing sale proceeds into a DST to defer tax.

**Cash Investor** — An investor placing available cash, with no exchange involved.

**Debt Replacement** — Matching prior mortgage with a DST's non-recourse debt.

**Allocation** — How an investor spreads capital across multiple DSTs.

**Suitability Review** — Confirming a DST fits the investor before investing.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)** — 1031-eligible fractional real estate held in a trust.
