# DST vs. REIT: Key Differences for 1031 Exchange Investors

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › DST vs. REIT: Key Differences for 1031 Exchange Investors

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trust · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 22 min read**

I have been thinking about how quickly a liquid ticker can make an investment feel simpler than it is. A listed price solves one problem, but it does not answer the separate question of whether the investment can receive 1031 proceeds or how its risks behave through a cycle.

First-order thinking compares a DST and a REIT as two ways to collect passive real-estate income. Second-order thinking begins with the legal interest, the tax objective, the exit mechanism, the way income is taxed, and the point at which a convenient path becomes irreversible.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">DSTs and REITs are frequently mentioned in the same breath, and for good reason: both let an investor own real estate without managing it. But they are built on different legal foundations, and the difference matters enormously at the one moment many investors care about most — the moment they're trying to defer a capital gain. A DST can complete a 1031 exchange; a REIT, generally, cannot. That single fact reshapes everything else about the comparison, and understanding why is the key to choosing well.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>A DST interest is treated as direct real estate ownership and qualifies for a 1031 exchange; REIT shares are securities and generally do not.</li>
<li>REITs — especially public ones — offer liquidity; DSTs are illiquid with a finite life.</li>
<li>A DST holds specific, identified properties; a REIT holds a diversified, actively managed portfolio.</li>
<li>Income is taxed differently — DSTs pass through depreciation, REITs distribute dividends — and a 721 exchange can move you from a DST into a REIT while continuing to defer tax.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 id="why">Why the comparison is so common — and so misleading</h2>
<p>The instinct to compare DSTs and REITs is natural: both are ways to own real estate passively and at scale. But the comparison misleads when investors treat them as interchangeable substitutes, because they answer different questions. A REIT answers "how do I own diversified, liquid real estate?" A DST answers "how do I defer the tax on a property I'm selling while staying invested in real estate?" Confusing the two leads investors to reach for a REIT when they need 1031 eligibility, or to lock into a DST when liquidity was what they actually wanted. The rest of this memo draws the distinctions cleanly so you can match the tool to your real goal.</p>
<h2 id="what">What each one actually is</h2>
<p>A <strong>DST</strong> is a Delaware trust that holds one or more specific properties and sells fractional beneficial interests to investors, recognized as direct real-estate ownership for tax purposes under <a href="1031-exchange-into-dst.html">Revenue Ruling 2004-86</a>. You own a slice of identified buildings.</p>
<p>A <strong>REIT</strong> — real estate investment trust — is a company that owns and operates a portfolio of income-producing real estate and is required to distribute most of its taxable income to shareholders. REITs come in three flavors that matter here: <strong>publicly traded</strong> REITs, whose shares trade on exchanges like any stock; <strong>public non-traded</strong> REITs, registered with the SEC and sold through broker-dealers but without daily market liquidity; and <strong>private</strong> REITs, offered to accredited and institutional investors under Regulation D. You own shares in a company, not a deed to a building. Our <a href="reits-guide.html">REIT guide</a> covers the three types in depth.</p>
<h2 id="decisive">The decisive difference: 1031 eligibility</h2>
<p>This is the distinction that usually decides the question. Because a DST interest is treated as a direct interest in real estate, it can serve as 1031 <strong>replacement property</strong> — you can exchange into it to defer capital gains and depreciation recapture, and you can later exchange out of it into other real estate. A REIT share, by contrast, is a security, not a like-kind real-estate interest. Buying REIT shares does not qualify for a 1031 exchange, and selling them is a taxable event like selling any stock.</p>
<p>So if your starting point is "I'm selling an investment property and want to defer the tax," the DST is on the table and the REIT, directly, is not. The only way real estate gain reaches a REIT tax-deferred is through the 721 bridge described below — and even that runs through a structure, not a direct purchase.</p>
<h2 id="liquidity">Liquidity</h2>
<p>Liquidity is where REITs clearly win. A publicly traded REIT can be bought or sold any trading day at a transparent market price. Public non-traded REITs are less liquid but typically offer periodic redemption programs, subject to limits the sponsor can adjust or suspend. A DST offers essentially none: there is no public market and no dependable secondary market, and your capital is committed until the sponsor sells the property, often in five to ten years. If access to your capital matters, that difference alone may settle the choice — but remember that liquidity and 1031 eligibility tend to trade against each other here.</p>
<h2 id="control">Control and management</h2>
<p>On control, the two are similar: in both, you are a passive investor with no operational say. The difference is subtler. A REIT is an operating company with management actively buying, selling, and financing across a portfolio, adapting to markets over time. A DST is deliberately static — the <a href="dst-pros-and-cons.html">seven prohibitions</a> bar it from refinancing, raising capital, or re-leasing freely — so it holds and distributes rather than adapts. Neither hands you the wheel, but the REIT at least has a driver who can change course; the DST is closer to a fixed itinerary.</p>
<h2 id="income">Income and taxation</h2>
<p>Both aim to pay regular income, but it's taxed differently. A DST passes through your share of the property's income and, importantly, its <strong>depreciation</strong>, which can shelter part of the distribution from current tax — one of the quiet advantages of direct-style ownership. And because a DST is treated as real property, when it sells you can defer the gain again through another 1031 exchange, keeping the chain going. A REIT distributes <strong>dividends</strong>, most of which are taxed as ordinary income — the REIT itself paid no corporate tax — though a <strong>20% Section 199A deduction</strong> applies to qualified REIT dividends, and some distributions may be classified as return of capital or capital-gain distributions, each with its own treatment and reported on Form 1099-DIV. The practical point is that a headline yield from a DST and a REIT are not directly comparable until you account for how each is taxed in your hands.</p>
<h2 id="diversification">Diversification and risk profile</h2>
<p>A single DST concentrates risk in one property or a small set of them, with a single sponsor and business plan; you diversify by buying several DSTs. A REIT is diversified by construction, holding many properties across markets and often sectors, with professional capital allocation across the cycle. That makes a REIT's risk profile broader and, in the public case, also exposed to <strong>stock-market volatility</strong> — public REIT prices can swing with the market even when the underlying real estate hasn't changed. A DST's value isn't marked to a public market day to day, which feels steadier but simply means the risk shows up at sale rather than on a screen. Different shapes of the same underlying real-estate risk.</p>
<h2 id="lifespan">Lifespan and exit</h2>
<p>A DST is finite: the sponsor eventually sells, the trust dissolves, and you face a decision about what to do with the proceeds (see <a href="dst-full-cycle.html">DST full-cycle</a>). A REIT is ongoing — it can hold and recycle assets indefinitely, and you exit by selling your shares (easily for a public REIT, through redemption programs for a non-traded one). This is why some investors treat a DST as a stage rather than a destination, using it to defer tax now and transitioning later into a more permanent, diversified vehicle — which is exactly what the 721 bridge enables.</p>
<h2 id="estate">Estate planning and the step-up in basis</h2>
<p>Estate planning is where the two vehicles interact differently with the tax code, and it's often overlooked. A DST used in a 1031 exchange defers your capital-gains tax; if you hold the interest until death, your heirs generally receive a <strong>step-up in basis</strong> to fair market value, which can eliminate the deferred gain entirely. This "swap till you drop" pattern — defer the gain now, collect income for years, then pass the interest on with a stepped-up basis — makes DSTs and 1031 exchanges a powerful way to move real estate to the next generation tax-efficiently. A publicly traded REIT share also receives a step-up at death, like other appreciated securities, which can erase the capital-gains tax on the share's own appreciation. But because a REIT share isn't 1031-eligible, it can't defer a prior property-sale gain in the first place — so the REIT offers a step-up on the share, while only the DST combines <em>deferral of a property-sale gain</em> with the eventual step-up. For an investor planning to defer a gain and pass real estate to heirs, that combination is the DST's quiet superpower.</p>
<h2 id="bridge">The 721 bridge: how a DST becomes a REIT</h2>
<p>Although you can't 1031 directly into a REIT, you can often get there in two steps. Many DSTs are structured so that, at full-cycle, the property is contributed to a REIT's operating partnership and investors receive <strong>operating-partnership (OP) units</strong> through a <strong>721 exchange</strong> (an "UPREIT" transaction). This continues the tax deferral and moves you into a diversified REIT with potential liquidity. The catch is that it's a <strong>one-way door</strong>: once you hold OP units you generally cannot 1031 out again, and converting OP units into REIT shares down the road is itself a taxable event. The 721 route is an excellent destination for an investor ready to stop exchanging and accept a more diversified, semi-liquid position — and a trap for one who assumed deferral could continue forever. Our <a href="reits-guide.html">REIT and 721 guide</a> covers the mechanics.</p>
<h2 id="which">Which is right for you</h2>
<p>Reduce it to the goal. Choose a <strong>DST</strong> when you're completing a 1031 exchange, want to defer a real-estate gain, and can accept illiquidity for passive, diversified income. Choose a <strong>REIT</strong> when you want liquidity and broad diversification and you do <em>not</em> need 1031 eligibility — for instance, when investing new cash rather than exchange proceeds. And consider the <strong>721 path</strong> when you've used a DST to defer tax and are now ready to consolidate into a diversified, more liquid vehicle for the long term. The comparison below summarizes the trade-offs.</p>

<h2 id="twoinvestors">A tale of two investors</h2>
<p>The clearest way to see the distinction is through two illustrative investors with different starting points.</p>
<p><strong>The exchanger.</strong> She is selling a rental with a large embedded gain and wants to defer the tax. For her, the REIT is essentially off the table as a direct option — buying REIT shares wouldn't qualify as a 1031 exchange, so the gain would be fully taxable. The DST is the natural fit: she exchanges into one (or several), defers the entire tax, and stays invested in real estate. If she later wants the diversification of a REIT, she can pursue it through a 721 exchange at full-cycle.</p>
<p><strong>The cash investor.</strong> He has $200,000 of new savings — not exchange proceeds — and wants real-estate exposure with the option to sell if his plans change. He has no gain to defer, so 1031 eligibility is irrelevant to him, and the DST's illiquidity is a cost with no offsetting benefit. A REIT, especially a publicly traded one, fits him far better: diversified, liquid, and simple.</p>
<p>Same two products, opposite recommendations — driven entirely by whether there is a gain to defer and whether liquidity is needed. That is the comparison in miniature.</p>
<h2 id="myths">Common misconceptions</h2>
<p>A few persistent misunderstandings cause real mistakes:</p>
<ul class="bul">
<li><strong>"I can 1031 into a REIT."</strong> Not directly. Only the two-step DST-then-721 path reaches a REIT with deferral intact.</li>
<li><strong>"Non-traded REIT redemptions are guaranteed liquidity."</strong> They are not. Redemption programs have limits and can be reduced or suspended, especially when many investors want out at once.</li>
<li><strong>"A DST is always safer than a REIT because its price doesn't move."</strong> A DST simply isn't marked to a public market; the risk is still there, it just surfaces at sale rather than daily. Stability of quoted price is not the same as stability of value.</li>
<li><strong>"REIT dividends and DST distributions are comparable head-to-head."</strong> They're taxed differently, so compare them net of tax, not by headline yield.</li>
</ul>
<p>Clearing these up tends to resolve most DST-versus-REIT confusion on its own.</p>
<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq">
<details open="">
<summary>Can I 1031 exchange into a REIT?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Not directly — REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real estate. You can, however, 1031 into a DST and later use a 721 exchange to move into a REIT while continuing to defer tax.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What's the main difference between a DST and a REIT?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A DST is a fractional interest in specific properties that qualifies for a 1031 exchange; a REIT is a share in a diversified portfolio company that generally doesn't. REITs are more liquid; DSTs are 1031-eligible.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Are REIT dividends taxed like DST income?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>No. DSTs pass through income and depreciation, which can shelter part of the distribution. REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, though a deduction may apply to qualified REIT dividends and some payouts are return of capital or capital-gain distributions.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What is a 721 exchange and how does it relate to DSTs and REITs?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A 721 exchange contributes property into a REIT's operating partnership for OP units, deferring tax. Many DSTs use it at full-cycle to move investors into a REIT. It's a one-way move — you generally can't 1031 out afterward.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Are DSTs riskier than REITs?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>They carry different risks. DSTs are illiquid and concentrated in specific assets; public REITs are liquid but exposed to stock-market volatility. Both depend on management quality and real estate conditions.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Which is better for income, a DST or a REIT?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>It depends on the specific offering and on how each is taxed in your hands. A DST's depreciation can shelter income; a REIT's dividends are mostly ordinary, with a 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends. Compare them net of tax, not by headline yield.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Is a DST or a REIT more diversified?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A REIT is generally more diversified than a single DST. A REIT owns a large portfolio — often across many markets and sometimes multiple sectors — while a DST typically holds one or a few specific properties. That concentration is part of what makes a DST 1031-eligible (you're investing in identifiable real property), but it means less built-in diversification. Many 1031 investors address this by spreading exchange proceeds across several DSTs from different sponsors, sectors, and markets.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I move from a DST into a REIT later?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Yes — via the 721 bridge. After you've 1031-exchanged into a DST, the sponsor may have the DST's property acquired by a REIT through a 721 (UPREIT) exchange, converting your interest into operating-partnership (OP) units that can later convert to REIT shares. This preserves your deferral. The trade-off is that once you convert OP units to REIT shares and sell, you hold a security, not 1031-eligible real property, so the deferral chain ends there. Confirm the mechanics and timing with your tax advisor.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Why isn't a REIT share treated as real property for a 1031?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Because a REIT share is legally an interest in a corporation (or trust treated as one) — a security — not a direct interest in real estate. Section 1031 requires like-kind real property, and the tax law specifically excludes interests in entities (stocks, bonds, partnership and REIT interests) from like-kind treatment. A DST interest, by contrast, is structured under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 so that each investor is treated as owning a direct, undivided interest in the underlying real property. It's the legal character of the interest, not the fact that both involve real estate, that determines 1031 eligibility.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What happens to a DST at the end of its hold?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A DST has a defined life: the sponsor holds the property (commonly five to seven years) and then sells it, winding the trust down and distributing the proceeds. At that point you can take the cash (and pay any deferred tax then due), roll the proceeds into another 1031 exchange to keep deferring, or — if offered — take a 721/UPREIT path into a REIT. A REIT, by contrast, has no fixed end date; you hold the shares until you choose to sell. The DST's finite term is worth planning for in advance with your advisor and CPA.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Are DSTs and REITs available to all investors?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Availability differs. Publicly traded REITs are available to virtually any investor through an ordinary brokerage account. DSTs, and non-traded or private REITs, are offered through a broker-dealer, typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors, often carry investment minimums, and involve a suitability review before you invest — reflecting their illiquidity and complexity. If you simply want liquid REIT exposure with new capital, a publicly traded REIT is broadly accessible; for a DST or non-traded REIT, expect a suitability process and confirm you meet the requirements.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I hold both DSTs and REITs in my portfolio?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Yes — many investors do, because they serve different purposes. You might use a DST to defer the tax on a property sale (gaining passive, defined-hold income real estate with potential estate-planning benefits) while also holding REITs for liquid, diversified exposure with new capital. The vehicles aren't mutually exclusive — they address deferring an exchange gain versus deploying new capital liquidly — and via the 721 bridge a DST can eventually become a REIT interest, blurring the line further. Size each allocation appropriately and confirm suitability for the gated options.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Do both a DST and a REIT get a step-up in basis at death?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Both can receive a step-up in basis at death, like other appreciated assets, which can eliminate the capital-gains tax on the appreciation for heirs. The difference is what leads up to it: a DST used in a 1031 exchange defers a property-sale gain, so the step-up can erase that deferred tax entirely ("swap till you drop"). A REIT share gets a step-up on its own appreciation but, not being 1031-eligible, can't defer a prior property-sale gain — so only the DST combines deferral with the eventual step-up.</p></div>
</details>
</div>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>REIT</dt><dd>Real estate investment trust — a company owning a portfolio of income real estate, whose shares are generally not 1031-eligible.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Publicly Traded REIT</dt><dd>A REIT whose shares trade on a stock exchange with daily liquidity.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Non-Traded REIT</dt><dd>An SEC-registered REIT not listed on an exchange, with limited periodic liquidity.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>721 Exchange (UPREIT)</dt><dd>A contribution of property to a REIT's operating partnership for units, deferring tax.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>OP Units</dt><dd>Operating-partnership units received in a 721 exchange, convertible to REIT shares in a taxable event.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Pass-Through Depreciation</dt><dd>Depreciation from a DST's property that flows through to investors, potentially sheltering income.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Like-Kind Real Property</dt><dd>The real estate a 1031 exchange requires — a DST interest qualifies; a REIT share does not.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Revenue Ruling 2004-86</dt><dd>The IRS ruling treating a DST beneficial interest as a direct interest in real property for 1031 purposes.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Fractional Interest</dt><dd>A DST investor's undivided share of the specific underlying property.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Capital-Gains Deferral</dt><dd>Postponing the tax on a property-sale gain, as a DST does via a 1031 exchange.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Step-Up in Basis</dt><dd>The reset of an asset's cost basis to fair market value at death, which can erase a deferred gain for heirs.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Defined Hold</dt><dd>A DST's multi-year holding period, often about five to seven years, before the sponsor sells.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Section 199A Deduction</dt><dd>The 20% deduction available on qualified REIT dividends.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Suitability Review</dt><dd>The broker-dealer's assessment of whether a DST or non-traded REIT fits a given investor.</dd></div>
</dl>
