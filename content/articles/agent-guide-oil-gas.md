# The Agent & Broker's 2026 Guide to Oil & Gas and Mineral Rights

I keep seeing the same questions reach the real estate agent before they reach a mineral specialist: a seller who wants out of rentals without writing a six-figure check to the IRS, heirs holding a West Texas royalty check they do not understand, or a high earner asking how wealthy people reduce a tax bill. Mineral rights appear in estates, land sales, and high-net-worth portfolios more often than many agents expect. They can even be part of a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). The useful question is where the agent's role ends.

First-order thinking treats oil and gas as another product to mention in a client conversation. Second-order thinking starts with the interest itself, the securities line, the client’s risks, and the licensed professionals who must make the actual tax or investment call. An agent does not need to sell oil and gas to be useful. The agent needs to recognize the question, explain the basics, and make a clean handoff to a licensed specialist and the client's CPA.

## Key Takeaways for Agents & Brokers

- Mineral and royalty interests are real-property interests in most producing states and can often be like-kind property for a 1031 exchange.
- Most oil, gas, and mineral-fund investments are securities offered through a private placement memorandum (PPM) to accredited investors. Refer rather than sell unless you hold the required securities license.
- A royalty interest is passive income with no operating costs or operating liability. A working interest is active, bears drilling costs, and carries real liability.
- Potential fits include sellers seeking passive income or a 1031 backup and high earners seeking deductions through working interests and intangible drilling costs. The second group assumes more risk.
- Educate without giving tax or investment advice; pair each referral with a licensed specialist and the client's CPA.
- The main payoff is retention and trust. Referral fees exist only where the law and broker permit them and where they are documented.

## Why an agent should know this at all

The question is rarely labeled “oil and gas.” It arrives in the ordinary flow of a real estate relationship. The seller wants to stop handling tenants and toilets but has a low basis and a gain waiting. An heir owns a royalty interest. A former buyer who is a top-producing dentist asks about deductions. In each case, the agent may be the first call. A thoughtful next question builds trust; a careless answer can send the client elsewhere.

You do not have to become the specialist. You do need to separate a seller looking for passive income and tax deferral—possibly a [mineral-rights 1031](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html) or a [DST](dst-guide.html)—from an heir who may own a saleable real-property interest, and from a high earner who is asking about securities, working interests, and intangible drilling costs. Holding that distinction without bluffing is the practical skill.

There is also a defensive reason. Calling an oil and gas fund “basically just real estate,” or calling a working interest “safe passive income,” can turn education into unlicensed investment advice and may steer a client toward a loss. This guide is intended to provide enough accuracy to ask the right next question and route the matter cleanly.

## Mineral rights and royalty interests, in plain terms

One parcel can contain two estates. The surface estate is the land, buildings, and visible use. The mineral estate includes oil, gas, other minerals, and the right to extract them. In most states, those estates can be severed and sold separately. A rancher can keep minerals after selling the grazing land, or retain the ranch after selling minerals. After severance, the mineral estate is its own real-property interest with its own title chain. A buyer can acquire a beautiful 200 acres and own none of what lies beneath it.

Mineral ownership does not usually mean drilling. More commonly, the owner leases to an operator with rigs, crews, and capital, may receive an upfront bonus payment, and receives a royalty: a fixed share of production value, free of drilling and operating costs. A royalty fraction historically often ran around one-eighth, although current lease terms vary widely. The operator bears extraction cost and risk; the royalty owner receives a share from the top.

That creates two very different ownership positions. A royalty interest is a passive share of revenue with no obligation for drilling or operations. A working interest is an active stake in the operation that pays its share of costs and carries liability. They are not interchangeable. Treating them as interchangeable can be the costliest misunderstanding in the conversation.

## The securities line: refer, don't sell

Most products presented to an oil and gas investor are securities: mineral-rights funds, royalty funds, drilling partnerships, and sponsor-packaged working-interest programs. They are usually sold under a PPM and Regulation D to verified accredited investors. Selling, recommending, or receiving a commission on a security requires a securities license. A real estate license does not supply one.

For an individual, accredited investor status can be based on $200,000 of income in each of the last two years, $300,000 jointly with a spouse with a reasonable expectation of the same this year, or $1 million of net worth excluding the primary residence. A Series 7, 65, or 82 license also qualifies a person. A client who does not meet one of those tests generally cannot purchase these offerings.

The line is simple. You can explain what a royalty is, how a 1031 operates, and why a working interest is riskier than a royalty. You can introduce a licensed specialist and participate in a meeting. You cannot recommend a specific fund, say whether someone should invest, or accept a percentage of a securities sale. A useful script is: “That is a securities product, and I am not licensed to sell or advise on it. Let me connect you with someone who is, and loop in your CPA.”

A direct purchase of one mineral or royalty interest as real property can sometimes be on the real-estate side of the line, but treatment varies by state and specialized handling may be necessary. Verify locally before assuming the transaction is within your license.

## The mineral-rights 1031 angle

In most producing states, mineral and royalty interests are treated as real-property interests under state law. Because the 1031 like-kind standard for real property is broad, a mineral or royalty interest can often be like-kind to other real estate. An owner may potentially exchange an apartment building into a royalty interest or exchange a royalty interest into a net-lease building, deferring capital-gains tax in the same manner as a rental-to-rental exchange.

For the fully depreciated fourplex owner with a low basis, a royalty interest or a DST can be a replacement option beside net lease and other passive structures. That may offer a way out of active management without immediately recognizing the gain. But “often” is not “always.” Eligibility depends on the exact interest, structure, and state; fund-style mineral investments are generally securities and require a separate analysis. A qualified intermediary and the client's tax advisor must confirm the result. The agent raises the possibility and routes it to the QI and CPA. See the [mineral-rights 1031 guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html) and the broader [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) for the mechanics.

## Royalty interests as passive income

A royalty interest can be exceptionally hands-off real-property income. The owner pays no drilling or operating cost and receives a share of monthly production revenue for as long as wells produce. No tenants, repairs, or capital calls. For an owner seeking less operational involvement, that is often the profile they have been describing.

The depletion allowance is worth recognizing, not calculating for the client. The IRS treats a mineral deposit as a wasting asset and permits owners to deduct part of annual gross income to reflect its depletion. Percentage depletion is commonly cited at 15% of gross income for oil and gas, subject to limits and eligibility rules. It can shelter part of royalty income, but a CPA should apply the rules. The [depletion allowance guide](oil-gas-depletion-allowance.html) addresses the mechanics.

Passive is not the same as safe. Royalty income moves with commodity prices. When oil or gas prices fall, the check can decline sharply. Production also declines and eventually stops because every barrel produced is gone. A royalty interest is not a bond; it is a claim on a finite, price-sensitive income stream. The [risk guide](oil-gas-investment-risks.html) and [why mineral royalties can yield more](why-mineral-royalties-yield-more.html) provide further context.

## Working interest vs. royalty interest

A working interest is an ownership stake in the operation. It pays its proportional share of drilling and operating costs and carries lease-related liability. Cost overruns, dry holes, environmental matters, and wellsite accidents can all reach the owner. The possible upside and tax deductions can be greater, but so can losses, capital demands, and liability.

A royalty interest sits above operating costs. It has no operating liability and receives its revenue share. It can earn less in a gusher and lose less in a bust. The trade is active ownership and costs versus a clean revenue share.

| Factor | Royalty / Mineral Interest | Working Interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Share of production revenue; no operational stake | Direct operating stake in the well or program |
| Who pays costs | None; free of drilling and operating expense | Pays its proportional share of drilling and operating costs |
| Liability | No operating liability | Exposed to operational, environmental, and accident liability |
| Income type | Passive revenue share | Active income, net of costs and capital calls |
| Risk profile | Commodity-price and depletion risk; lower volatility | Higher: cost overruns, dry holes, liability, and capital calls |
| Typical investor | Income- and 1031-minded owner seeking passive exposure | High earner seeking larger deductions and accepting greater risk |
| 1031 eligibility | Often like-kind real property; verify by state | Treatment varies; many program forms are securities rather than real property |

Illustrative comparison for educational purposes. Treatment varies by interest, structure, and state. Confirm with a qualified intermediary, a licensed specialist, and the client's CPA. The [working interest vs. royalty interest guide](working-interest-vs-royalty-interest.html) provides the full comparison.

## Depletion and intangible drilling costs, at a glance

Two tax features explain much of the appeal. The depletion allowance lets a royalty owner deduct a portion of gross income for a wasting resource, commonly cited at 15% for oil and gas and subject to limits. It can reduce tax on passive royalty income.

Intangible drilling costs (IDCs) belong to working interests. They are non-salvageable drilling expenses—labor, fluids, and site preparation—with no resale value after they are spent. The tax code can allow working-interest investors to deduct a large share in the first year, creating a meaningful first-year deduction. That is also why a working interest must not be sold as a mere tax feature: the deduction comes with the costs and liability of a working interest. When a client mentions IDCs, hear “high earner, high risk, securities,” and bring a licensed specialist and CPA into the conversation.

## Which clients actually fit

Two broad profiles may fit:

- **The passive-income or 1031 seller.** This owner is tired of active management, seeks income or diversification away from one property type, or needs a backup replacement for an exchange. A royalty or mineral interest—or a DST—can be a possible fit. The discussion concerns income, deferral, and leaving operations.
- **The high earner seeking deductions.** A surgeon, founder after an exit, or partner with substantial W-2 or active income may ask about IDC and depletion benefits. This is the higher-risk path. Cost overruns, illiquidity, and loss of principal are real possibilities; the client needs a licensed specialist and CPA.

The non-accredited investor generally cannot buy these offerings. Neither is the retiree who needs every dollar liquid and stable, nor the buyer who hears “passive income” as “no risk.” A plain “this is not for you” can be the responsible answer. If another passive route fits, consider the [DST options](dst-guide.html) and the [agent's DST guide](agent-guide-dst.html).

## How to talk to clients without crossing the line

The pattern is educate, defer, refer. Explain the concept in ordinary language, do not give investment or tax advice, and involve the professional licensed to provide it.

For a seller considering a passive exit: “Mineral and royalty interests are treated as real property in most producing states, so they can sometimes qualify for a 1031 exchange as another rental can. I cannot tell you whether it is right for you, but I can explain the framework and bring in a specialist and your CPA.” For a high earner asking about tax breaks: “Working interests can create substantial first-year deductions, but they are securities and carry real risk, including the loss of money. I am not licensed to sell or advise on them. Let me connect you with a firm that specializes in them and include your CPA.”

Neither script says “you should,” names a fund, promises a yield, or promises a tax outcome. It explains the structure, identifies risk, and passes the decision to licensed hands. That is the compliance posture clients can trust.

## The referral relationship and what it's worth to you

The economic value of understanding this topic is usually not a referral fee. Compensation tied to a securities transaction is tightly restricted. Without the proper license, an agent generally cannot receive a cut of a securities sale. Even an ordinary referral fee must satisfy state real-estate rules, brokerage policy, and applicable securities law; if lawful, it needs written documentation and disclosure. Treat a casual “send them my way and I will pay you” proposal cautiously until compliance confirms it.

The durable return is retention and trust. A client who asks a question outside your usual area and sees a clear, honest response is more likely to return with the next property and refer others. A dependable bench—a licensed oil and gas specialist, a CPA, and an estate attorney—lets an agent serve a high-net-worth relationship end to end while remaining within the license.

## Compliance and disclosure for agents

Keep the discipline short and repeatable:

- **Educate, never advise.** Explain the structure, do not recommend an offering or express a tax outcome. Those calls belong to a licensed advisor and CPA.
- **Know what is a security.** Funds, drilling partnerships, and working-interest programs are almost always PPM offerings for accredited investors. Refer them. A direct mineral or royalty conveyance may be real estate, but confirm the state treatment first.
- **State the risk plainly.** These can be speculative, illiquid private placements that lose principal. Commodity prices can fall, reserves deplete, and no yield or return is guaranteed.
- **Document any referral.** If a referral fee is permitted, put it in writing, disclose it, and clear it with the broker and compliance professional. When uncertain, take no fee.
- **Bring in the right people.** Pair the discussion with a licensed specialist and the client's CPA. The edge is the handoff, not the close.

That discipline is not a reason to avoid the topic. It is what lets an agent remain useful with confidence: hold the map, point to the right door, and let the licensed professional lead the client through it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I, as a real-estate agent, sell oil & gas investments to my clients?

Generally no. Most oil, gas, and mineral-fund investments are securities offered under a PPM to accredited investors, and selling or advising on a security requires a securities license that a real-estate license does not provide. You can explain the concept and refer the client to a licensed specialist and CPA.

### Can mineral and royalty interests qualify for a 1031 exchange?

In many cases, yes. In most producing states they are real-property interests under state law and can be like-kind to other real estate. The exact interest, its structure, and the state matter, so confirm with a qualified intermediary and the client's tax advisor before relying on the outcome.

### What is the difference between a royalty interest and a working interest?

A royalty interest receives a share of production revenue without drilling or operating costs or operating liability. A working interest is an active operating stake that pays its share of costs and bears liability. Working interests offer potentially larger deductions and larger risk.

### What is the depletion allowance?

It is a deduction recognizing a mineral deposit as a wasting asset. Percentage depletion permits a royalty owner to deduct part of gross income each year, commonly cited at 15% for oil and gas and subject to limits. Some royalty income can therefore be shielded from tax, but the client's CPA should determine the exact result.

### Which of my clients are a fit for this?

Sellers who want passive income, diversification, or a 1031 backup may be candidates for a royalty or mineral interest. High earners seeking large deductions through working interests and IDCs may consider a drilling program, but they take more risk. Non-accredited investors generally cannot purchase the offerings.

### Can I earn a referral fee for sending a client to a specialist?

Sometimes, but the rules are restrictive. You generally cannot receive a cut of a securities transaction without a license. Any referral fee must comply with state real-estate rules, brokerage policy, and securities law, and must be documented and disclosed. Obtain compliance confirmation first; the primary value is retention and trust, not a fee.

### How do these investments lose money?

They can be speculative, illiquid private placements that lose principal. Royalty income can fall with commodity prices and decline as reserves deplete. Working interests add cost overruns, dry holes, capital calls, and liability. No yield is guaranteed, and income can fall or stop.

## Glossary

### Mineral Rights

Ownership of resources beneath a parcel and the right to extract them. Mineral rights can be severed from the surface estate and sold separately as a real-property interest.

### Royalty Interest

A passive right to production revenue, free of drilling and operating costs and operating liability. It is treated as real property in most producing states.

### Working Interest

An active ownership stake in an oil and gas operation that pays its share of costs and carries liability in return for larger potential income and deductions.

### Depletion Allowance

A deduction reflecting that a mineral deposit is a wasting asset. Percentage depletion is commonly cited at 15% of gross income for oil and gas, subject to limits.

### Intangible Drilling Costs

Non-salvageable expenses of drilling a well that working-interest investors can often deduct heavily in the first year; their treatment is the main draw for many high earners.

### Private Placement Memorandum

The disclosure document for a securities offering sold privately under Regulation D to accredited investors. Funds and drilling programs are usually offered this way.

### Like-Kind Property

The 1031 exchange standard. For real property, it is broad enough that mineral and royalty interests can often be like-kind to other real estate.

### Severance

The legal separation of the mineral estate from the surface estate, after which each can be owned, sold, and titled independently.
