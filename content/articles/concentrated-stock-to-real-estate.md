# Concentrated Stock to Real Estate: Using a Stock Gain to Buy Property

I keep seeing a familiar instinct: a large stock position has appreciated, real estate feels more tangible, and the holder wants to “1031” the stock into property. The wish is understandable. The mechanism is wrong.

A 1031 exchange is limited to like-kind real property. It cannot move stock into real estate. For a realized capital gain on appreciated stock, the tax-advantaged route worth examining is a Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF) under IRC §1400Z-2. A QOF is often a real-estate vehicle, and it can defer an eligible gain invested within 180 days. It may also permit tax-free appreciation on the QOF investment after a 10-year hold. QSBS and charitable structures can matter too.

First-order thinking says, “I want to avoid the tax and get into property.” Second-order thinking asks a more useful question: does the structure fit the gain, the holding period, the liquidity sacrifice, the investment risk, and the investor's actual objectives? A tax benefit can improve an investment. It cannot repair a weak one.

This is educational information, not tax advice. QOFs are securities offered to accredited investors following a suitability review. Confirm the facts and the tax treatment with a CPA.

## Why a 1031 Exchange Doesn't Apply to Stock

It is worth being direct. IRC §1031 defers gain only through an exchange of like-kind **real property** held for investment or business use. Stocks, bonds, and other securities are outside §1031. A concentrated position cannot be “1031'd” into an apartment building, rental property, or any other real estate on a tax-deferred basis.

[This comparison of 1031 exchanges versus selling and investing in stocks](1031-exchange-vs-selling-investing-stocks.html) explains the distinction. A 1031 moves real estate into real estate. For a stockholder, the real question is how to move a *stock gain* into real-estate exposure with tax advantages once 1031 is off the table.

## The Lead Strategy: A Qualified Opportunity Fund

Under IRC §1400Z-2, a person who realizes a capital gain from appreciated stock or another security generally has 180 days to invest that **gain** in a QOF and defer it. Many QOFs develop or improve property in designated Opportunity Zones. That makes a QOF a possible bridge from a stock gain to real-estate exposure.

Start with the mechanics in the [QOF overview](what-is-a-qualified-opportunity-fund-qof.html) and the [180-day rule guide](the-180-day-rule-for-investing-capital-gains-in-a-qof.html). The headline benefit comes later: after holding the QOF investment for at least 10 years, the appreciation on the **QOF itself** can be excluded from tax. That excludes new gain created by the fund investment, not the original deferred stock gain. See [how the deferral and 10-year exclusion work](opportunity-zone-tax-benefits-deferral-and-10-year-exclusion.html).

Two details do most of the work here. Only the realized gain, not the full sale proceeds, must be reinvested to seek QOF treatment. And the original deferred gain is recognized later on a schedule set by law. The program was made permanent under 2025 legislation often called “OZ 2.0,” with a rolling deferral structure, but the exact recognition timing depends on current rules. [Crypto and other security gains may qualify as well](investing-crypto-and-stock-gains-in-an-opportunity-zone.html).

> A 1031 moves real estate into real estate. A Qualified Opportunity Fund moves a gain—from stock, crypto, or a business—into Opportunity Zone real estate. That is the bridge an equity holder actually needs.

## QSBS: If Your Stock Is Qualifying Small-Business Stock

There may be a different answer if the position is qualified small business stock, or QSBS. QSBS is original-issue C-corporation stock that satisfies the requirements of IRC §1202. Subject to holding-period and per-issuer limits, part or all of its gain may be excluded from federal tax.

That is not a real-estate strategy by itself. It can still change the decision. If a gain is QSBS-eligible, an investor may be able to exclude it rather than defer it through a QOF, then deploy the after-tax proceeds into real estate without the QOF structure. Eligibility is technical and fact-specific; a CPA should confirm it before anyone plans around it.

## Charitable Options for Appreciated Stock

When charitable intent is real, direct donation of appreciated stock to a charity or donor-advised fund can avoid capital-gains tax on the donated shares and provide a fair-market-value charitable deduction, subject to AGI limits. A charitable remainder trust (CRT) can sell appreciated stock inside the trust without immediate tax, pay an income stream, and leave the remainder to charity.

Neither option is a route to personally owned real estate. They are legitimate choices when philanthropy is part of the plan, and a CRT can hold or invest in real estate. [DSTs and charitable remainder trusts](dsts-and-charitable-remainder-trusts.html) covers the trust mechanics. Confirm deduction limits and structure with a CPA and attorney.

## Key Takeaways

- A 1031 exchange does **not** apply to stock. A stock position cannot be exchanged into real estate under §1031.
- A QOF under IRC §1400Z-2 is the leading tax-advantaged route: reinvest the realized gain within 180 days, often in Opportunity Zone real estate, and seek potential tax-free QOF appreciation after 10 years.
- Reinvest the gain, not necessarily the entire proceeds. The original deferred gain is recognized later on the statutory schedule.
- QSBS under IRC §1202 may provide an outright exclusion for qualifying small-business-stock gain. A donor-advised fund (DAF) or CRT may fit when charitable intent is present.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use a 1031 exchange to move stock into real estate?

No. IRC §1031 applies to like-kind real property held for investment or business use, not stocks, bonds, or other securities. A concentrated appreciated stock position cannot be exchanged into an apartment, rental, or other property tax-deferred under 1031. A QOF under IRC §1400Z-2 may let an investor reinvest realized capital gain within 180 days; QSBS under IRC §1202 may offer an exclusion if the shares qualify. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice, so confirm the path with a CPA.

### How does a Qualified Opportunity Fund let me move a stock gain into real estate?

After a sale of appreciated stock creates a capital gain, IRC §1400Z-2 permits investment of that gain in a QOF within 180 days to defer tax. Because many QOFs develop or improve Opportunity Zone real estate, the investment can provide the desired exposure. Only the gain is reinvested, unlike a full-deferral 1031 exchange, which generally requires all net equity and debt replacement. A 10-year QOF hold may exclude appreciation on the QOF itself. The original deferred gain is recognized later under the statutory schedule; the program's details were updated by 2025 legislation often called OZ 2.0, so confirm current timing and eligibility with a CPA.

### What is QSBS and how does it differ from a QOF?

QSBS is original-issue stock in a qualifying C corporation. Under IRC §1202, it can exclude a portion or potentially all of a gain, subject to holding-period tests and per-issuer dollar limits. That is an exclusion, not a deferral. A QOF can defer eligible gain from many sources, including non-QSBS stock, and may exclude appreciation after a 10-year hold. If QSBS applies, an investor may exclude the gain and invest after-tax cash freely; if the position is regular appreciated stock, a QOF may be the tax-advantaged route to deferred gain and real-estate exposure. Neither tool is a 1031 for stock.

### Do I have to reinvest all the stock-sale proceeds, or just the gain?

For a QOF, reinvest the realized capital **gain**, not the full proceeds or original basis. For example, a sale for $1,000,000 with a $400,000 gain can support a $400,000 QOF investment within 180 days, while the remaining $600,000 is retained. That differs from a 1031, which does not apply to stock and generally requires all net equity plus debt replacement for full deferral. For a direct stock sale, the 180-day clock generally starts on the sale date; confirm timing and amounts with a CPA because the rules can vary with the source and type of gain.

### Are charitable strategies a good way to handle appreciated stock?

They can be when the investor has charitable intent. Direct donation to charity or a DAF may avoid capital-gains tax on donated shares and provide a fair-market-value deduction subject to AGI limits. A CRT can sell appreciated stock without immediate tax, pay income for a term or life, and leave the remainder to charity; it can also hold or invest in real estate. These structures are about charitable giving, not personally owning real estate. Work with a CPA and estate attorney before acting.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF):** A fund investing in Opportunity Zones and eligible for OZ tax benefits.
- **IRC §1400Z-2:** The Opportunity Zone statute allowing capital-gain deferral and a 10-year exclusion.
- **180-Day Rule:** The window to invest a realized gain into a QOF.
- **10-Year Exclusion:** Tax-free appreciation on a QOF held at least 10 years.
- **1031 Exchange:** A real-property-only tax-deferred swap; it does not apply to stock.
- **QSBS:** Qualified small business stock with potential gain exclusion under IRC §1202.
- **Concentrated Stock:** A large single-position holding, often highly appreciated.
- **Donor-Advised Fund (DAF):** A charitable account that can receive appreciated stock.
- **Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT):** A trust that sells appreciated assets tax-deferred and pays income.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds under SEC Rule 501.
