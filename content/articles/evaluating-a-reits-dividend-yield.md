# Evaluating a REIT’s Dividend Yield

I have been thinking about how quickly a yield screen can turn a falling share price into something that looks like opportunity. The number at the top gets attention. The cash flow supporting it deserves the attention first.

The first-order view is that the highest dividend yield produces the most income. The second-order view asks why the yield is high, whether adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) supports the dividend, how the result compares with sector peers, and what the position contributes to total return. That is the difference between durable income and a yield trap.

## Why a High Yield Can Be a Trap

Dividend yield is annual dividend divided by share price. It can rise because the dividend increases, which may be good. It can also rise because the price falls, which often is not. If a REIT faces declining occupancy, a stretched balance sheet, or a struggling sector, investors may sell. A lower price mechanically produces a higher yield even before the dividend changes.

That is the yield trap. An investor sees a double-digit yield, buys for income, then discovers that [the REIT cuts its dividend because the payout was unsustainable](why-reits-cut-dividends.html). Income falls and the price often falls again. The elevated yield was the market’s warning about a possible cut, not free income.

Not every high yield is a trap. Some sectors properly carry higher yields. Still, a yield that is unusually high relative to peers, or that has climbed because the price fell, should bring suspicion before excitement. The payout must be tested.

> Yield is dividend divided by price. The most eye-catching yield can be the market’s warning that a dividend cut is coming, not an invitation to easy income.

## Yield vs. Payout Sustainability

The relevant question is not simply the yield. It is whether the payout is supported by the cash the REIT produces. AFFO is generally a more useful approximation of distributable cash than net income or even FFO. A REIT must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income as dividends, as explained in [the 90 percent distribution rule](how-reits-pay-dividends-the-90-percent-distribution-rule.html). What matters to an income investor is the cushion behind that required payout.

The AFFO payout ratio is dividends divided by AFFO. A ratio comfortably below **100%** of AFFO suggests coverage with room to spare. A ratio near or above **100%** suggests that the dividend is stretched. The trend matters too: a rising payout ratio can signal declining coverage.

Funding also matters. A high yield backed by a low, stable AFFO payout ratio and operating cash flow can be a sound income opportunity. A high yield backed by declining coverage, debt, asset sales, or return of capital can be fragile even if the headline number looks attractive. Two REITs can show the same yield and have entirely different risk profiles.

## Comparing Yields by Sector

Yield has meaning only in context, and sector is the most useful context. Mortgage REITs (mREITs) tend to pay more because they are leveraged and rate-sensitive. Net-lease REITs also often pay higher yields, but for another reason: stable, bond-like cash flows and high payouts. Growth-oriented sectors, including certain industrial or data-center REITs, often yield less because they retain cash for expansion and investors expect growth rather than large current income.

A **4%** yield may be high for a fast-growing data-center REIT but low for a mortgage REIT. An **8%** yield may be routine for an mREIT and a conspicuous warning for an apartment REIT. Cross-sector comparisons can falsely reassure or falsely alarm. The better question is how a yield compares with its own peer group. A yield well above the sector norm deserves the most scrutiny because it may reflect additional risk or a coming cut.

> An 8% yield can be unremarkable for a mortgage REIT and a flashing red light for an apartment REIT. A yield only means something inside its own sector.

## Yield Plus Growth

Chasing the highest current yield can sacrifice total return. Total return is dividend income plus appreciation, and appreciation is often tied to growth in FFO/AFFO and the dividend over time. A lower-yielding REIT that consistently grows cash flow and raises its dividend can produce a higher total return and a rising income stream than a higher-yielding REIT with a static payout or a payout at risk of a cut.

For example, a **4%** yield growing at a healthy pace can, over years, exceed a flat **7%** yield in total income collected and ending value, often with less cut risk. High-growth REITs in strong sectors may pair modest yields with durable dividend growth. The highest static yields often offer little growth and more risk.

The better income question is not only “what is the yield today?” It is “what is the yield, and how sustainably can the dividend grow?” The aim is to balance starting yield and sustainable growth rather than maximize the first number on the screen.

## Building a Yield Watchlist

A yield watchlist turns the analysis into a repeatable process. It is a curated list of REITs worth considering for income, screened and monitored rather than selected merely because they show the highest yield. That same discipline supports [income-focused REIT distribution strategies](income-reit-distribution-strategies.html).

For each candidate, record the current yield alongside:

- AFFO payout coverage and the trend in that coverage;
- balance-sheet strength, including leverage, interest coverage, and debt maturities;
- dividend-growth history; and
- yield relative to sector peers.

The list helps an investor wait for the right kind of yield increase. A quality REIT marked down in a broad market sell-off or rate scare may offer more income at a better price. A yield increase caused by a company-specific problem may instead deserve rejection. If the candidate has already passed the coverage and quality checks, a high yield leads back to the notes rather than to an impulse purchase.

### Key Takeaways

- Yield is dividend divided by price, so a high yield can reflect a falling price and an at-risk dividend rather than a gift.
- Test yield against AFFO payout coverage and funding, not in isolation.
- Compare a REIT with its own sector. Mortgage and net-lease REITs tend to yield more; growth sectors often yield less.
- Combine yield and sustainable growth for total return, and use a watchlist rather than chasing the highest number.

## Yield and the Rate Environment

Rates influence both the level of REIT yields and what those yields are worth. When rates rise, REIT prices often fall. Their dividends compete with higher-yielding bonds, higher borrowing costs can pressure cash flow, and higher cap rates can lower property values. All of that can mechanically push REIT yields up.

Higher yields during a rising-rate period can signal genuine value—quality REITs sold off because of macro concern—or real strain on an issuer whose balance sheet and cash flow are under pressure. Coverage and balance-sheet work separates those cases.

The rate backdrop also changes opportunity cost. A **5%** REIT yield looks different when safe bonds yield **2%** than when safe bonds yield **5%**. In the latter case, the REIT needs to offer stronger growth, quality, or more income to compensate for its added risk. When rates fall, REIT prices can rise and yields can compress. A yield should therefore be judged against bonds, cash, peers, and the prevailing rate environment—not in a vacuum.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a REIT’s dividend yield?

It is the annual dividend divided by the current share price, expressed as a percentage. A REIT paying **$2** annually at a **$40** share price has a **5%** yield. That shows the current-income return at today’s price. It is a helpful starting point, but the arithmetic cuts both ways: yield rises if the dividend rises and also if the price falls. A high yield can therefore show a growing dividend or a marked-down price that may warn of a cut. Test sustainability, sector peers, and likely growth before treating it as income quality.

### Why can a high REIT yield be a trap?

Yield can spike because the share price falls, not because the dividend becomes more generous. Declining occupancy, a stretched balance sheet, a weak sector, or looming debt maturities can bring selling. If the payout was already unsustainable, the REIT may then cut it, reducing income while the price falls further. That is the classic yield trap. Some sectors legitimately yield more, but an unusually high or price-driven yield should be investigated rather than grabbed.

### How do I tell if a REIT’s dividend is sustainable?

Start with AFFO coverage. Calculate or find the AFFO payout ratio: dividends divided by AFFO. A ratio comfortably below **100%** can show cushion; a ratio near or above **100%** can show strain. Also review its trend. Rising payout ratios mean coverage is weakening. Check the funding source as well. Heavy return of capital, asset sales, or borrowing can make a dividend less dependable than one supported by operating cash flow. Verify current figures from the REIT’s reporting, because AFFO includes estimation.

### Should I compare REIT yields across different sectors?

No. Different sectors have different economic structures. mREITs have higher yields because of leverage and rate sensitivity. Net-lease REITs can support higher payouts through stable, bond-like cash flow. Growth-oriented industrial and data-center REITs may retain more cash and yield less. A **4%** yield can be high for a fast-growing data-center REIT but low for a mortgage REIT, while an **8%** yield can be normal for an mREIT and alarming for an apartment REIT. Compare within the sector, then perform the sustainability test.

### What is a yield trap?

A yield trap is a stock or REIT with a tempting high dividend yield but an at-risk payout likely to be cut. As price falls on weak fundamentals, balance-sheet strain, or a stretched payout, the yield rises mechanically. An investor who buys only for that yield can face a reduced dividend and a further share-price loss. Defenses are AFFO coverage and its trend, sector-peer comparison, and checking whether the distribution is funded by operations rather than by fragile sources.

### Is a lower-yielding REIT ever a better investment than a higher-yielding one?

Yes, often. Total return and the durability and growth of income matter more than the starting yield alone. A lower-yielding REIT that grows FFO/AFFO and raises its dividend can produce more cumulative income and ending value than a high-yielding REIT with a flat or threatened payout. A **4%** yield that grows steadily can surpass a flat **7%** yield over time, generally with less cut risk. The highest current yield is rarely the best long-term outcome.

### What does yield plus growth mean for REITs?

It means evaluating current dividend income together with the appreciation that can follow growth in FFO/AFFO and the dividend. A REIT that expands cash flow and raises its distribution may offer an increasing income stream and capital appreciation, rather than a static payout. That is why a lower starting yield that grows can outperform a higher static yield. Ask not only what the yield is, but how sustainably the dividend can grow.

### How do I build a REIT yield watchlist?

Create a cross-sector roster of quality REITs you would consider for income. For each, review AFFO payout coverage and the trend, leverage, interest coverage, debt maturities, fixed-versus-floating debt mix, dividend-growth history, and sector-relative yield. Record the yield and its sustainability profile, then monitor the names. A broad market sell-off or rate scare may create an attractive entry for a quality REIT; a company-specific decline may not. The point is to make a high yield trigger a review of prepared notes, not a reflexive purchase.

### Why does yield rise when a REIT’s price falls?

The arithmetic is direct. A **$2** dividend on a **$40** price is a **5%** yield. If the dividend remains **$2** but the price falls to **$25**, the yield becomes **8%**. The same dollar payment is a larger percentage of a smaller price. The issue is why the price fell: the market may be anticipating weak fundamentals, a stretched payout, or balance-sheet pressure and a future cut. Determine whether the yield rose because the dividend grew or because the price declined.

### Do mortgage REITs have higher yields than equity REITs?

Generally, yes, but the higher yield comes with different and often greater risk. Mortgage REITs do not own properties directly. They finance real estate by holding mortgages and mortgage-backed securities and earn the interest-rate spread between those assets and their borrowing costs. Significant leverage and rate/spread sensitivity can support high yields while making income, book value, and dividends more volatile. Equity REITs own and operate income-producing property and usually have more moderate rent-supported yields that vary by sector. A mortgage REIT’s yield should be compared with the mREIT peer group, not assumed superior to a more stable equity REIT.

### Can a high yield ever be safe?

Yes, if it is verified rather than assumed. A stable net-lease REIT with long leases and a high but covered payout may legitimately offer an elevated yield. The better signs are a low and stable AFFO payout ratio, sound or improving coverage, operating-cash-flow funding, a sound balance sheet, and a yield in line with peers. The warning signs are a stretched ratio, worsening coverage, return-of-capital-heavy funding, and a yield far above the sector norm. A high yield is not automatically unsafe; it is unverified until those checks are complete.

### How does interest rate environment affect REIT yields?

Higher rates often lower REIT share prices because dividends compete with higher-yielding bonds, borrowing costs increase, and property values can be pressured. The resulting price decline raises yield mechanically. Those yields can represent quality REITs discounted by macro fear or issuers facing genuine cash-flow strain, especially rate-sensitive mREITs or REITs with heavy floating-rate debt. When rates fall, prices can rise and yields compress. In a rising-rate environment, coverage and balance-sheet checks are especially important.

### Should income investors hold a diversified set of REITs?

Generally, yes. Diversification across REITs and sectors—such as residential, industrial, healthcare, and net lease—can reduce the damage from one dividend cut, sector decline, or company-specific error. It does not eliminate risk: REITs can fall together during a downturn or rate shock. But it can smooth the income stream and reduce the effect of one mistake. Pair diversification with individual sustainability work, sensible position sizes, and an overall REIT allocation that fits the broader plan and risk tolerance.

### How are REIT dividends taxed, and does that affect yield?

Tax affects the income an investor keeps, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice. Most REIT ordinary dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at qualified-dividend rates because the REIT did not pay corporate tax. A **20%** Section 199A deduction applies to qualified REIT dividends, lowering the effective top federal rate; the source notes that the deduction was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. Some distributions are return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than being currently taxed, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the mix on Form 1099-DIV. Tax-advantaged accounts can change the calculation, so confirm individual treatment with a tax advisor.

## Glossary

- **Dividend Yield:** Annual dividend divided by share price, expressed as a percentage.
- **Yield Trap:** A deceptively high yield backed by an at-risk dividend likely to be cut.
- **AFFO Payout Ratio:** Dividends divided by AFFO; a primary sustainability gauge.
- **Payout Sustainability:** Whether the dividend is covered by distributable cash flow.
- **Adjusted FFO (AFFO):** FFO less recurring capex, an approximation of distributable cash.
- **Dividend Coverage:** The cushion between distributable cash and the dividend paid.
- **Total Return:** Dividend income plus appreciation over a holding period.
- **Dividend Growth:** Increases in a distribution over time that can drive appreciation.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A leveraged, rate-sensitive REIT that often has a high yield.
- **Net-Lease REIT:** A stable-cash-flow REIT that often pays a high but covered yield.
- **Growth Sector:** A REIT type with lower yields because cash is retained for expansion.
- **Return of Capital (ROC):** A distribution that returns invested capital rather than earnings.
- **Yield Watchlist:** A curated, monitored list of quality REITs vetted for sustainability.
- **Sector Norm:** The typical yield range for REITs in a given property sector.
- **Dividend Cut:** A reduction in the distribution, the risk a high yield may flag.
- **Funds From Operations (FFO):** Net income plus real-estate depreciation, minus gains on sales.
