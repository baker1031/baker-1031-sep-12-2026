# Form 8824: Reporting Your 1031 Exchange

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I have been thinking about the point at which investors start calling an exchange “done.” It is often the replacement closing. That is an understandable milestone, but it is not the last one. The tax position still has to be reported.

Form 8824 is the IRS form that documents a like-kind exchange and computes deferred gain, recognized gain, taxable boot, and the new basis. It is where the transaction's economics become the figures the IRS uses to confirm the deferral and track the carried-over gain.

First-order thinking is that a fully deferred exchange produces no current tax, so the paperwork is routine. Second-order thinking recognizes that the form establishes the basis that will influence depreciation and a later sale, while mismatched figures can create questions even if the transaction itself was valid. Form 8824 is not a form to treat casually.

## What Form 8824 reports

Form 8824, Like-Kind Exchanges, is used to report a 1031 exchange and claim the deferral. It documents that an exchange occurred, identifies the properties involved, records the timeline, and computes the tax result: how much gain is deferred, how much is recognized as taxable [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), and what basis the replacement property will have.

The form is filed with the federal income tax return for the year the relinquished property was transferred—the year of the sale. That is true even when a replacement closing occurs in the following year, as it can within the 180-day period. Form 8824 is part of the return, not a separate filing. Generally, taxpayers complete one form for each exchange, with special handling when an exchange involves multiple properties or assets.

The form is required even if the exchange fully defers gain and produces no tax currently due. Deferral is a tax position claimed by reporting the exchange; it is not automatic just because the transaction closed. Leaving out the form, or completing it incorrectly, can leave the IRS without a record of the deferral or lead it to question an otherwise valid exchange.

## Key parts and lines of the form

Form 8824 is divided into parts, each addressing a separate part of the exchange.

Part I records the descriptive and timeline information: the like-kind property given up and received, the date the relinquished property was transferred, and the dates the replacement property was identified and received. It is the portion that documents compliance with the 45-day and 180-day deadlines.

Part III contains the financial calculations. It determines realized gain, recognized gain—the taxable boot—and the replacement property's basis. Sale price, adjusted basis, boot, and additional investment flow through the lines in sequence. Those figures determine both current tax, if any, and the basis that carries forward.

Part II applies to related-party exchanges. It captures information about the related party and the two-year holding period required under those special rules. Part IV applies to certain deferrals involving conflict-of-interest property sales, a narrow situation. For most investors, Parts I and III do the practical work; Part II is added when a related party is involved.

> Part I establishes the like-kind property and timeline. Part III computes realized gain, taxable boot, and the new basis. Together, they are the heart of the form.

## Reporting boot and basis

Boot and basis are the most consequential—and often the most error-prone—Form 8824 calculations. Boot is cash or non-like-kind value received, including unreplaced debt. It creates recognized gain that is taxable up to realized gain. The recognized gain is the lesser of total boot or realized gain. If no boot is received and the exchange is fully deferred, recognized gain is zero. If cash is retained or debt is reduced, the recognized gain reflects that boot.

The basis calculation establishes the basis in the replacement property. It carries deferred gain forward and affects future depreciation. In a fully deferred exchange, replacement basis is essentially the old adjusted, carryover basis, adjusted for additional cash invested or boot received. It is commonly lower than the replacement purchase price; the difference is the deferred gain continuing in the replacement property.

That lower basis affects depreciation deductions and the gain on a later sale. The calculations also interact. Boot received increases recognized gain and changes basis. Additional cash invested or debt assumed affects basis and can offset boot. [Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) can enter the result as well, because recognized gain from boot may include recapture taxed at higher rates.

The arithmetic is mechanical but intricate. Since one input affects another, this is the part of an exchange where a CPA's work can prevent a tax amount or basis from being misstated.

## Filing timing

Form 8824 is filed with the federal return for the year the relinquished property was transferred. An exchange can span two tax years because a replacement can close within 180 days, but a late-year sale followed by an early-next-year purchase is still reported with the return for the sale year.

There is an important timing connection. The replacement-closing deadline is the earlier of 180 days or the due date of the sale-year tax return. A late-year sale can therefore have a shorter practical closing window if that return date arrives before day 180.

An extension for the sale-year return preserves the full 180 days and gives the taxpayer time to complete the exchange before reporting it. That is why late-year exchanges often call for an extension coordinated with a CPA. The filing deadline and exchange deadline should be planned at the beginning of the exchange, not discovered at tax time.

## Working with your CPA

Many investors work through Form 8824 with a CPA because realized gain, boot, recognized gain, carryover basis, and depreciation recapture interact in ways that are easy to get wrong. A CPA familiar with like-kind exchanges draws on the closing statements, basis records, boot details, and depreciation history to prepare the form as part of the return.

The CPA's role goes beyond filling out a form. The CPA reconciles the Form 8824 figures with the full return, applies tax to recognized boot, including recapture at the right rates, establishes the replacement property's basis and depreciation schedule, and checks consistency with the exchange documents. For partial exchanges, related-party transactions, multiple properties, or a working-interest equipment carve-out, that technical work becomes even more important.

Early involvement matters. The filing timetable, extension question, carryover basis, boot calculation, and records all benefit from advance planning. When the CPA is involved from the start, the transaction can be documented so reporting is straightforward. Waiting until the return is due often means reconstructing facts after the event and missing planning opportunities. The CPA who prepares Form 8824 should be part of the exchange team from the beginning.

## Key Takeaways

- Every 1031 exchange is reported on Form 8824 with the return for the year the relinquished property was sold, even when gain is fully deferred.
- Part I covers property descriptions and the timeline; Part III computes realized gain, recognized boot, and replacement basis.
- Boot is recognized gain up to realized gain. The replacement basis carries over and is commonly lower than the purchase price, preserving deferred gain.
- A late-year exchange often needs an extension to preserve the 180-day window. Involve a CPA from the beginning.

## Common reporting errors to avoid

The first recurring error is simply not filing Form 8824. Investors sometimes assume no tax means no reporting. It does not. A fully deferred exchange still has to claim its deferral through the form.

Another error is miscalculating boot or basis. Understating boot can underreport tax. Getting carryover basis wrong can distort future depreciation and gain on a later sale. Those errors often begin with missed debt boot, mishandled additional cash, or a failure to account for depreciation recapture. Because the figures interact, one wrong entry can affect several others.

The third error is inconsistency. Form 8824 figures should reconcile with the closing statements, exchange documents, and the rest of the return. A valid exchange can invite scrutiny if its reporting does not match its records. Reporting on the wrong year's return or leaving out related-party Part II are other avoidable problems. Complete records and careful CPA-supported preparation are the remedy.

## The records you'll need to complete it

The reporting starts with records. For the relinquished property, gather the sale closing statement showing sale price, selling costs, and debt paid off. Also gather basis records: original cost, improvements, accumulated depreciation, and depreciation history. Those facts establish realized gain and feed Part III.

For the replacement property, retain the purchase closing statement showing price, debt assumed, and any additional cash invested. Keep the dates of identification and receipt for Part I. If cash was retained or debt was not replaced, preserve the boot details. The [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) records and accounting for the exchange funds help connect both legs.

The supporting file should be built before tax time, not recreated under deadline pressure. Keep the exchange agreement, identification notice, both closing statements, basis records, and depreciation records together. The CPA uses them to compute realized gain, boot, recognized gain, and carryover basis. The form can only be as accurate as the records behind it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Form 8824?

Form 8824 is the IRS Like-Kind Exchanges form used to report a 1031 exchange and claim the deferral. It documents the properties and timeline, calculates realized gain and recognized gain from taxable boot, and establishes the replacement property's basis. It is filed with the tax return for the year the relinquished property was sold.

### Do I have to file Form 8824 if I owe no tax?

Yes. A fully deferred exchange must still be reported. The deferral is claimed by reporting the exchange; it is not granted automatically. Leaving the form off can leave the IRS without a record of the position and create problems later, even if the underlying exchange was valid.

### When do I file Form 8824?

File it with the federal income tax return for the year the relinquished property was transferred, even if the replacement closed in the following year. It is part of the return, not a separate filing. Late-year exchanges may need an extension to preserve the full 180-day closing window and complete the exchange before reporting.

### What does each part of the form cover?

Part I covers property descriptions and the transfer, identification, and receipt dates. Part III computes realized gain, recognized gain from boot, and replacement basis. Part II is for related-party exchanges, and Part IV covers a narrow conflict-of-interest situation. Most investors focus on Parts I and III.

### How is boot reported on Form 8824?

Boot is cash or non-like-kind value received, including unreplaced debt. It is recognized gain taxable up to realized gain, and the form uses the lesser of total boot or realized gain. No boot produces zero recognized gain in a fully deferred exchange. Cash retained or reduced debt creates boot and may include depreciation recapture at higher rates.

### How is my replacement property's basis calculated?

In a fully deferred exchange, it is essentially the old adjusted, carryover basis, adjusted for additional cash invested or boot received. It is commonly lower than the purchase price because deferred gain carries forward. That basis affects future depreciation and gain when the replacement is later sold.

### Why is the basis lower than what I paid for the replacement?

A 1031 exchange carries the old basis forward instead of resetting it to a fresh purchase price. The gap between replacement value and carryover basis is the deferred gain continuing in the new property. It can mean smaller depreciation deductions than a new purchase would have allowed, which the CPA accounts for.

### Can I complete Form 8824 myself?

You can, but most investors use a CPA because realized gain, boot, recognized gain, carryover basis, and recapture calculations are intricate and connected. A CPA familiar with exchanges uses the closing statements and basis records to report the deferral and basis accurately.

### What happens if I file Form 8824 incorrectly?

The result can be an incorrect current tax amount, including understated boot, or an incorrect basis that affects future depreciation and gain. Inconsistency with closing statements or the return can invite scrutiny. Frequent mistakes include leaving out the form, miscalculating boot or basis, overlooking debt boot or recapture, and using the wrong year. Careful CPA-backed preparation helps avoid them.

### Do I report a related-party exchange differently?

Yes. A related-party exchange requires Part II, which records the related party and the two-year holding period. These exchanges have special rules, so work with a CPA to ensure Part II is completed correctly.

### What if my exchange straddles two tax years?

Report it with the return for the year the relinquished property was sold, even if the replacement closes the next year. The same fact raises the deadline issue: the closing deadline is the earlier of 180 days or the sale-year return due date. A late-year sale often needs an extension to preserve the full window.

### Should my CPA be involved before tax time?

Yes. Involve the CPA at the beginning. Filing timing, the extension issue, basis carryover, boot calculations, and documentation are easier to plan in advance than to reconstruct when the return is due.

### What records do I need to complete Form 8824?

For the relinquished property, retain the sale closing statement, basis records, and depreciation history. For the replacement, retain the purchase closing statement and the identification and receipt dates. Add boot details and the qualified intermediary's accounting of exchange funds. Together, they support realized gain, recognized gain, and carryover basis.

### Where do the figures on Form 8824 come from?

The closing statements supply sale price, selling costs, replacement price, debt, and additional cash. Basis and depreciation records supply adjusted basis and recapture. The qualified intermediary's accounting documents boot. The CPA brings those records together to calculate realized gain, boot, recognized gain, and carryover basis.

### Do I report one Form 8824 per exchange?

Generally, yes: one Form 8824 for each like-kind exchange. Exchanges involving multiple properties or asset groups receive special treatment. If more than one exchange was completed in a year, each is reported. A CPA can determine the correct approach for multi-property facts.

### How long should I keep my exchange records?

Keep them well beyond filing. Retain them while the replacement property is held and through the period when a later sale may be examined, because carryover basis affects gain whenever the replacement is sold. Keep the exchange agreement, identification notice, both closing statements, basis and depreciation records, and filed Form 8824 in an organized long-term file.

## Glossary

**Form 8824:** The IRS form, Like-Kind Exchanges, used to report a 1031 exchange and claim the deferral.

**Realized Gain:** Total gain on the relinquished property—sale price minus adjusted basis and selling costs.

**Recognized Gain:** The portion of gain actually taxed: the lesser of total boot or realized gain.

**Deferred Gain:** The portion of gain not recognized, carried forward in the replacement property's basis.

**Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received, including unreplaced debt, recognized as taxable gain.

**Carryover Basis:** The relinquished property's adjusted basis transferred to the replacement, preserving deferred gain.

**Adjusted Basis:** Original cost reduced by depreciation and other adjustments; it is used to compute realized gain.

**Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, at higher rates, which can be part of recognized boot.

**Part I (Form 8824):** The section reporting property descriptions and the exchange timeline.

**Part II (Form 8824):** The section for related-party exchange information and the two-year holding period.

**Part III (Form 8824):** The section computing realized gain, recognized gain, and the replacement basis.

**45-Day Identification Period:** The window to identify replacement property, confirmed on the form's timeline.

**180-Day Exchange Period:** The window to close on the replacement, confirmed on the form's timeline.

**Tax-Return-Date Rule:** The closing deadline is the earlier of 180 days or the year-of-sale return's due date.

**Extension:** Filing for more time on the year-of-sale return to preserve the 180-day window for a late-year exchange.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party whose closing statements and records support the Form 8824 figures.
