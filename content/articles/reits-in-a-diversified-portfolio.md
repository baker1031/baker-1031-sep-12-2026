# REITs in a Diversified Portfolio

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how easily “diversification” becomes a label rather than a discipline. A portfolio can own many securities and still be making the same bet. Real estate accessed through REITs is a distinct asset class, and that can matter—but only when the allocation, the correlations, the liquidity, and the existing exposures are considered together.

First-order thinking says REITs add income and real estate to a stock-and-bond portfolio. Second-order thinking asks whether the allocation duplicates direct property already owned, whether the observed correlation will hold in stress, how rate sensitivity changes the result, and whether the position has grown beyond its intended role. This is general education, not personalized investment advice. The appropriate allocation depends on the individual; consult an advisor, and remember that past performance does not guarantee future results.

REITs provide liquid, professionally managed exposure to income-producing real estate. They have historically shown moderate, or less-than-perfect, correlation with stocks and bonds, so they can bring a different return pattern alongside income and an inflation consideration. This guide covers the reasons investors use them, commonly cited allocation ranges of roughly **5–15%**, rebalancing, and the qualifications that accompany each. For the full range of vehicles, including private and non-traded structures, see the [2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## Why add REITs to a portfolio

Investors usually cite four reasons.

First, diversification. REITs represent real estate, a distinct asset class from stocks and bonds, so they spread capital across more types of exposure. Second, income. REITs distribute most of their taxable income as dividends under the **90%** rule, which can provide meaningful portfolio cash flow.

Third, inflation consideration. Rents and property values may rise with inflation over time. Fourth, accessibility. REITs can provide liquid, low-minimum real-estate exposure without the capital commitment and management responsibilities of direct ownership.

Those are reasons to examine REITs, not reasons to add them automatically. The role should fit the whole portfolio.

## Correlation with stocks and bonds

Correlation is the mechanism behind the diversification argument. Historically, REITs have shown [moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with the broad stock market](how-reits-have-performed-vs-the-sp-500.html) and with bonds. They do not move in lockstep with either, so adding them to a stock-and-bond portfolio can introduce a somewhat different return pattern rather than simply duplicating the existing risk.

Combining assets that are not perfectly correlated can reduce overall portfolio volatility relative to return. That is the statistical point, and it is more useful than calling any asset a stand-alone diversifier.

Correlations are not static. REITs can move with stocks in short-term market stress, and their relationships vary over time. Their longer-horizon, historically moderate correlation is why they have been used as a diversifier; it is not a promise that they will offset every decline.

## Recommended allocation ranges

[Commonly cited REIT allocation ranges](how-much-of-your-portfolio-should-be-reits.html) fall roughly in the **5–15%** area. Many portfolio frameworks use a broad real-estate allocation in that range to add the asset class meaningfully without overconcentrating. Treat it as general education, not a prescription.

The right allocation depends on goals, time horizon, income needs, risk tolerance, existing real-estate holdings, and the overall plan. Someone with substantial direct property may want less REIT exposure. Someone without direct property may want more. Neither conclusion follows from the range alone.

An allocation is a decision about the portfolio that already exists, not a vote on whether REITs are “good.” Set the actual allocation with an advisor.

## REITs as an inflation consideration

REITs have two inflation channels, not one. Rents and property values may rise with inflation as leases reset and replacement costs increase. That can support income and value over time.

The counterforce is rate sensitivity. REITs are income-producing and often use debt. Rising rates, which frequently accompany inflation-fighting policy, can pressure REIT prices through higher borrowing costs and competition from higher bond yields. The rent-and-value tailwind can be offset by the rate headwind.

REITs are therefore an inflation consideration, not a guaranteed inflation hedge. The lease structure, financing, sector, and rate environment all matter.

## Key Takeaways

- REITs add a distinct real-estate asset class, with income and accessible exposure, to a portfolio.
- Their historically moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with stocks and bonds is the basis of their diversification benefit.
- Roughly **5–15%** is a commonly cited general-education range, not an individualized allocation rule.
- Rents and values may respond to inflation, while rate sensitivity can work the other way. Rebalancing keeps the intended real-estate exposure on plan.

## The income role of REITs

REITs distribute at least **90%** of taxable income to shareholders, a rule connected to their corporate-tax treatment. The result is relatively high dividends and a potential source of cash flow for retirees and other income-focused investors. See [REITs for retirement income](reits-for-retirement-income.html) for that use case.

Income complements diversification. It can support spending or be reinvested to compound. But tax character matters: REIT dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income and reported on a **1099-DIV**. Qualified REIT dividends may receive the **20%** Section **199A** deduction, made permanent by the **2025** OBBBA. A CPA should confirm the actual tax treatment.

That gives REITs a dual role—diversification and income—without turning them into a guarantee of either income or a particular return.

## Rebalancing with REITs

Rebalancing is the discipline of periodically restoring target allocations. Because REITs have their own price movements, the allocation drifts: it rises when REITs outperform and falls when they lag. Rebalancing means trimming a position that has grown beyond target and adding to one that has fallen below it.

Traded REITs make this relatively straightforward because they can be bought or sold on any trading day, without the friction of direct property. A disciplined rebalancing process can create a buy-low, sell-high tendency: trim after gains and add after declines. It is not a trading prediction.

Common methods are calendar-based reviews, such as annual or semi-annual checks, and threshold rebalancing, such as acting when a position is plus or minus a few percentage points from target. Rebalancing too often can create costs and taxes in taxable accounts; doing it too rarely lets the allocation drift too far. Choose a policy that is practical and apply it consistently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why should I add REITs to my portfolio?

REITs can add a distinct real-estate asset class alongside stocks and bonds, providing diversification, income from the **90%** distribution rule, an inflation consideration because rents and values may rise, and liquid, low-minimum exposure without direct-property management. Rate sensitivity is a counterforce to the inflation case. Whether to add them, and in what amount, depends on the individual portfolio. This is general education; consult an advisor.

### How correlated are REITs with stocks and bonds?

Historically, REITs have had moderate, less-than-perfect correlation with the broad stock market and bonds. They do not move in lockstep, so combining them with other assets can reduce overall portfolio volatility relative to return. The relationship is not fixed: REITs can move more closely with stocks in short-term stress, and past correlations do not guarantee future ones. The relevant question is their relation to the rest of a particular portfolio.

### How much of my portfolio should be in REITs?

Roughly **5–15%** is a commonly cited general-education range. Actual sizing depends on goals, horizon, income needs, risk tolerance, existing direct real estate, and the full portfolio. Direct-property owners may need less REIT exposure; investors without it may choose more. There is no universal number. Use the range as a discussion starting point with an advisor, not a fixed rule.

### Are REITs a good inflation hedge?

They are better described as a nuanced inflation consideration than a guaranteed hedge. Inflation can support rents and property values through lease resets and replacement costs. At the same time, higher rates can increase borrowing costs and make higher bond yields more competitive, pressuring REIT prices. The outcome is not purely positive or negative, and it should be assessed with an advisor in the context of rate sensitivity.

### How do REITs provide income in a portfolio?

Because REITs must distribute at least **90%** of taxable income, they tend to pay relatively high dividends. The cash flow can support spending or be reinvested. REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income reported on a **1099-DIV**, with a **20%** Section **199A** deduction on qualified REIT dividends, made permanent by the **2025** OBBBA. Ask a CPA about the actual tax treatment; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### What does it mean to rebalance with REITs?

It means restoring a target allocation after the REIT position drifts. Trim when it grows beyond target and add when it falls below target. With traded REITs, the trading-day liquidity makes adjustment more direct than with property. The process can enforce a buy-low, sell-high tendency, but it is a discipline for maintaining intended exposure, not a market call.

### Do REITs replace direct real estate in a portfolio?

Not necessarily. REITs offer passive, liquid, diversified real-estate exposure. Direct ownership can offer control, leverage, direct depreciation, and **1031** eligibility. Some investors use REITs as their full real-estate allocation for simplicity and liquidity; others combine them with direct property to diversify across markets or property types. The right choice depends on capital, goals, and situation. Consult an advisor about how the two should fit together.

### Are REITs too volatile to be a diversifier?

Traded REITs can be volatile in the short term. Their share prices move with the market and can sometimes move more than the underlying property value. That does not disqualify them as diversifiers, because diversification depends on how an asset moves relative to the rest of the portfolio, not just its standalone volatility. Still, REITs are equity-like, not steady bond-like holdings. Their volatility supports a sensible, not oversized, allocation and a long-term perspective.

### Can REITs help with retirement income?

They can contribute through relatively high dividends driven by the **90%** rule, adding cash flow and real-estate diversification to a retirement portfolio. Dividends are mostly ordinary income on a **1099-DIV**, partly softened by the **20%** Section **199A** deduction. Tax-advantaged account treatment can change the calculation, so consult a CPA. REITs have market and rate risk and should not be the sole income source; they are one component of a balanced retirement income plan sized to risk tolerance and needs.

### Does adding REITs increase or decrease portfolio risk?

Either outcome is possible. A reasonable REIT allocation may improve diversification in a stock-and-bond portfolio because of moderate correlation. An oversized allocation, or one added to an already real-estate-heavy portfolio, can increase concentration and risk. The effect depends on the starting portfolio and the allocation size. Consult an advisor about whether and how much exposure would diversify the actual holdings.

### How often should I rebalance my REIT allocation?

There is no single right frequency. Calendar approaches include annual or semi-annual reviews. Threshold approaches act after drift beyond a band, such as plus or minus a few percentage points from target. Traded REITs make either approach practical on a trading day. Keep costs and taxes in taxable accounts in mind: too much activity can be costly, while too little can allow material drift. An advisor can help set a frequency and bands for the portfolio and accounts.

### Are non-traded REITs suitable for a diversified portfolio?

They can play a role for some investors. They provide REIT-style diversification and income, and NAV pricing does not swing with daily market sentiment. The trade-offs are limited redemption, illiquidity, higher fees, and suitability requirements. They are generally for accredited or otherwise-suitable investors after review, not capital that may be needed. The SEC has published an investor bulletin on non-traded REITs that addresses illiquidity, fees, and distribution sources. Review those factors with an advisor before committing capital.

### Should my REIT allocation change as I get older?

It might, as the overall allocation evolves, but there is no automatic age rule. Investors nearing or in retirement may value income more, or they may choose to reduce equity-like volatility, including REIT exposure, in favor of more stable assets. The appropriate direction depends on income needs, risk tolerance, other holdings, goals, and time horizon. Review the allocation as circumstances change rather than applying a fixed age-based formula.

### Do REITs belong in taxable or tax-advantaged accounts?

Account location can matter. REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income reported on a **1099-DIV**, so some investors favor tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs or **401(k)s**, where the income is not taxed currently. In taxable accounts, the **20%** Section **199A** deduction on qualified REIT dividends, made permanent by the **2025** OBBBA, can soften the ordinary-income treatment. The best location depends on accounts, tax bracket, and the overall plan. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; ask a CPA to weigh ordinary-income treatment against the deduction.

## Glossary

### Diversification

Spreading capital across asset classes that do not all move together.

### Asset Class

A category of investments, such as stocks, bonds, or real estate/REITs.

### Correlation

How closely two assets’ returns move together.

### Moderate Correlation

Less-than-perfect co-movement, the basis of the REIT diversification argument.

### Allocation

The portion of a portfolio assigned to an asset class.

### 5-15% Range

A commonly cited REIT allocation range for general education.

### 90% Rule

The requirement that REITs distribute **90%+** of taxable income, supporting high dividends.

### Income

Cash flow from REIT dividends, a potential portfolio benefit.

### Inflation Consideration

The nuanced relationship between REITs and inflation.

### Rate Sensitivity

Price pressure on REITs from rising interest rates.

### Rebalancing

Periodically restoring target allocations.

### Threshold Rebalancing

Rebalancing when an allocation drifts beyond a specified band.

### Volatility

The degree of price fluctuation in an asset.

### Section 199A Deduction

The **20%** deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

### 1099-DIV

The form reporting REIT dividend income.

### Risk Tolerance

An investor’s capacity and willingness to bear risk.
