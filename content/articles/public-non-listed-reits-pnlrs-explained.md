# Public Non-Listed REITs (PNLRs) Explained

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the word “public” treated as if it automatically means an investor can get out whenever they want. With a public non-listed REIT, or PNLR, that is the wrong inference. The vehicle may provide the paperwork and oversight of a public company, but it does not provide a public market for the shares.

A PNLR sits between exchange-listed REITs and private REITs. It is registered with the SEC and files the same recurring reports a public company files, including Forms 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K. That gives an investor far more information and oversight than a private REIT generally provides. But PNLR shares are not listed on a stock exchange. They do not trade every day. Liquidity usually comes through periodic redemption programs that can be capped.

The first-order view is simple: public reporting sounds safer and a smooth NAV statement sounds steadier. The second-order question is more useful: what happens to the exit when many owners want cash at the same time? That question, along with fees, time horizon, and suitability, determines whether the structure belongs in a portfolio. Non-traded and private REITs typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors and are offered through a broker-dealer after a suitability review; confirm the current requirements with your advisor.

## What Is a PNLR?

A public non-listed REIT is a [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html) registered with the SEC but not listed on a stock exchange. Like every REIT, it owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate. It follows REIT qualification rules, including distributing at least 90% of taxable income, and pays dividends to shareholders.

“Public” describes registration and disclosure. “Non-listed” describes trading. A PNLR files a registration statement and prospectus with the SEC, then makes annual Form 10-K filings, quarterly Form 10-Q filings, and current Form 8-K filings available to investors and the public. But because there is no exchange listing, an owner cannot buy or sell shares on a market throughout the day.

Instead, the REIT commonly sells shares in a continuous or periodic offering at a price tied to net asset value, or NAV, and offers limited liquidity through a [redemption program](non-traded-reit-redemption-programs-explained.html). That is why PNLRs are often called non-traded REITs. They are public in disclosure, not in daily tradability: a transparent but illiquid middle category in the REIT family.

## Registration & Reporting Standards

SEC registration is the defining feature. To raise capital from the public, a PNLR files a registration statement and an SEC-reviewed prospectus describing its investment strategy, fees, risks, management, and financial condition. The registration makes the REIT public even when its shares never list.

The reporting obligations continue after the offering. A Form 10-K carries audited financial statements and a comprehensive business review. A Form 10-Q provides unaudited interim financials. A Form 8-K reports material events. The REIT is also subject to SEC rules concerning proxy statements, internal controls, and ongoing disclosure. Investors can review standardized public information on properties, income, debt, fees, and NAV much as they would for a listed company.

That transparency is meaningful. It does not, however, create daily liquidity. A PNLR can offer a listed company’s paperwork without offering a listed company’s ability to sell on a Tuesday afternoon.

## PNLR vs. Private REIT

A PNLR and a private REIT are easy to confuse because both are unlisted and illiquid. Their regulatory footing is very different.

A PNLR is SEC-registered, files full public-company reports, and generally is available to a broad base of suitable investors after a suitability review. A private REIT is not registered with the SEC. It generally relies on a private-placement exemption, is sold only to accredited and institutional investors, and supplies much less public disclosure.

That difference shapes the information available to the investor. A private REIT does not publicly file 10-Ks and 10-Qs or follow the same public reporting and proxy rules. Investors rely much more heavily on the sponsor’s private reporting. A PNLR’s audited public filings give an investor more standardized material to evaluate and more regulatory oversight.

The private structure can, by limiting its investor base and disclosure, sometimes pursue strategies or fee arrangements with fewer constraints. The trade is less transparency and less protection through public reporting. Both forms are illiquid. Only the PNLR is publicly reporting.

## Liquidity Mechanisms

Public registration does not make a PNLR liquid. Since shares are not exchange-listed, there is no continuous market of buyers. The primary exit route is a share redemption program, also called a repurchase plan, in which the REIT periodically buys shares from owners who request redemption.

The terms matter. A program can be capped by a percentage of outstanding shares or NAV per quarter or per year—often around 5% annually, sometimes with monthly or quarterly sub-limits. If requests exceed the limit, not every request can be filled in full. The board may reduce, suspend, or terminate the program, particularly when market stress produces a wave of redemption requests.

Redemptions normally use the most recent NAV and may carry an early-redemption discount during a stated holding period. That makes liquidity real but constrained and discretionary. An investor can ask for a redemption. They cannot count on a sale on demand or on a price they control. PNLR capital should be treated as long-term, committed capital.

### Key Takeaways

- A PNLR is SEC-registered and files full 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K reports like a listed company, but its shares are not exchange-listed.
- Registration and reporting provide public-company transparency and oversight, far more than an unregistered, lightly disclosed private REIT generally offers.
- Liquidity comes only through a capped, suspendable, NAV-priced redemption program, so the structure is illiquid and intended for the long term.
- PNLRs tend to suit longer-term, income-focused, suitable or accredited investors who do not need liquidity and value transparency over daily tradability.

## PNLR vs. Listed REIT

A PNLR and a listed REIT share SEC registration and full reporting. The dividing fact is the listing. Listed REIT shares can be bought and sold on an exchange on any trading day at a continuously updated market price. That offers daily liquidity and real-time price discovery.

It also makes every change in market mood visible. A listed REIT can move with broad market swings, sector sentiment, and interest rates even when its underlying properties are stable. The listed owner receives daily access in exchange for daily volatility.

A PNLR does not list its shares. It uses periodic NAV rather than continuous market pricing and relies on the redemption program for exits. The resulting statement value may look smoother, but that reflects infrequent appraisal-based valuation, not the absence of underlying risk. A PNLR has also historically carried higher upfront fees and sales loads than a listed REIT, although newer structures have worked to reduce some costs.

This is the central trade: daily liquidity and market pricing on one side; periodic NAV and a longer-term, less-frequently-marked holding on the other. Listed and non-listed public REITs read from the same regulatory rulebook. The PNLR simply skips the chapter that lets an owner sell whenever they want.

## Investor Suitability

A PNLR generally fits a longer-term, income-focused investor who values public SEC filings, wants real-estate exposure that is not marked to market every day, and does not need the invested principal for an extended period. The income orientation follows the REIT requirement to distribute most taxable income. This is usually a multi-year holding, not a vehicle for quick trading gains.

Access reflects the commitment. PNLRs are offered through a broker-dealer, can have investment minimums, and typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors. A suitability review considers financial situation, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. The redemption limits mean the allocation should be money the investor can leave invested, not funds needed in the near term.

Fees require equal attention. Upfront selling costs and ongoing management fees can reduce net returns. An investor should weigh those costs, the illiquidity, and the value of public reporting before committing. For background on eligibility, see [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a PNLR?

A PNLR is a public non-listed REIT: a Real Estate Investment Trust registered with the SEC and subject to full public-company reporting, including Forms 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K, but not listed on a stock exchange. It owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate, follows the REIT rules including the at-least-90% distribution requirement, and pays dividends. It is public in disclosure, not exchange trading. Shares are priced periodically at NAV and have limited redemption liquidity, which is why the structure is commonly called a non-traded REIT and generally suits a longer-term, suitable investor.

### How is a PNLR different from a publicly traded REIT?

Both structures are SEC-registered and fully reporting. Listed REIT shares trade on an exchange at a continuously updated market price, offering daily liquidity and real-time price discovery, along with visible volatility. A PNLR has no listing, uses periodic NAV, and offers only a capped, suspendable redemption program. PNLRs also have historically carried higher upfront fees. The transparency is shared; the price mechanism and ability to trade are not.

### How is a PNLR different from a private REIT?

Both are unlisted and illiquid. A PNLR is SEC-registered, publicly reports through 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K filings, and generally is available to suitable investors after review. A private REIT is unregistered, generally relies on a private-placement exemption, is limited to accredited and institutional investors, and has minimal public disclosure. It does not make public 10-Ks and 10-Qs or follow the same reporting and proxy rules, so there is less standardized information to evaluate.

### Is a PNLR liquid?

No. There is no continuous exchange market. An owner requests a redemption through the share-repurchase program, which is commonly capped around 5% of shares or NAV each year and may have quarterly sub-limits. The board can reduce, suspend, or terminate the program, especially in stress when many investors want to redeem. Redemptions generally use the most recent NAV and may have an early-redemption discount. Treat the capital as committed for the long term.

### What reports does a PNLR file?

It first files a registration statement and SEC-reviewed prospectus. After the offering, it files an annual Form 10-K with audited financial statements and a comprehensive business review, a quarterly Form 10-Q with unaudited interim financials, and a current Form 8-K for material events. SEC rules also cover proxy statements, internal controls, and ongoing disclosure. The reports give public information about properties, income, debt, fees, and NAV.

### Who should invest in a PNLR?

A PNLR may suit a longer-term, income-focused investor who values public filings, does not need access to principal for years, and prefers not to see a daily market mark. It is not appropriate for near-term cash needs. Broker-dealer access, minimum investment requirements, accredited-or-suitable status, and a review of finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance all matter. Historically higher upfront and ongoing fees can reduce net returns.

### How are PNLRs priced?

PNLRs use periodic NAV rather than a continuously quoted market price. NAV is an estimate of the per-share value of real estate and other assets, usually calculated on a set schedule—often monthly or quarterly—using appraisals and valuation methods. It prices share sales and redemptions. The smoother number can lag actual conditions; it does not eliminate property risk. A redemption generally uses the most recent NAV and can include an early-redemption discount.

### Do PNLRs have higher fees than listed REITs?

Historically, yes. PNLRs have often included selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, organizational and offering expenses, and asset-management fees. Those costs can reduce the amount initially deployed in real estate and the investor’s net return. Listed REIT shares can often be bought through a brokerage account without a commission, while REIT funds or ETFs may have a modest expense ratio and no large upfront load. Newer non-traded structures have sought to reduce some costs, but the all-in fee load still deserves comparison with a lower-cost listed alternative.

### Are PNLR dividends taxed differently?

No. A PNLR is a REIT under the tax code, so its dividend character does not depend on being listed, non-listed, or private. Ordinary REIT dividends are generally taxed as ordinary income rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rate. Qualified REIT dividends generally receive the 20% Section 199A deduction, which can lower the effective top federal rate to roughly 29.6%; the deduction was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation.

A distribution can also include return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than being currently taxed, or capital-gain distributions taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the character on Form 1099-DIV. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; confirm current rules and your own treatment with a tax advisor.

### Can the PNLR redemption program be suspended?

Yes. The board can reduce, suspend, or terminate the program. A redemption program is the main liquidity route for an unlisted REIT, but it is not a guarantee. Programs are often capped around 5% of shares per year, sometimes with quarterly sub-limits, and stress can be the very time when more requests arrive than the program can meet. Review the offering documents for the caps, pricing, holding periods, and the board’s suspension rights before investing.

### Is a PNLR the same as a non-traded REIT?

Generally, yes. Both terms describe a REIT that is SEC-registered and fully reporting but not exchange-listed. “Non-traded” emphasizes no daily exchange liquidity; “public non-listed” emphasizes public registration and reporting. Both use periodic NAV and capped, suspendable redemptions. They should not be confused with a private REIT, which is unregistered, exemption-based, restricted to accredited and institutional investors, and far less transparent. Confirm the particular offering’s terms.

### What kinds of real estate do PNLRs own?

They can own much the same range as listed REITs. Equity REITs may own or operate residential property, including apartments and single-family rentals; industrial and logistics assets; net-lease retail; healthcare and medical office; office; self-storage; and data centers. A program may diversify across sectors or focus on one strategy. Non-traded mortgage REITs can hold mortgages and mortgage-backed securities.

The non-listed status does not define the real estate. The sponsor’s strategy does. A PNLR’s 10-K and prospectus can show what it owns, where it is located, how it is financed, and how it earns income. Portfolio mix, sector exposure, leverage, and income sources should be reviewed before investing.

### Are PNLRs less volatile than listed REITs?

They may appear less volatile, but that does not necessarily mean less underlying risk. A listed REIT price reflects investor sentiment, interest-rate expectations, and broad market swings continuously and can decline sharply during a sell-off while the properties remain stable. A PNLR’s periodic NAV moves less often and may look smoother because it is appraisal based. The real estate can still lose value, rents can fall, occupancy can decline, and NAV can lag the change. The two structures report risk on different schedules.

### How do I invest in a PNLR?

You invest through a broker-dealer rather than an exchange. The REIT typically offers shares continuously or periodically at a price tied to NAV. The process includes reviewing the prospectus and offering documents, then a suitability review of finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. PNLRs are illiquid, longer-term holdings that often require accredited or otherwise suitable investors and may have minimum investments.

Once suitability is confirmed, the investor subscribes and capital is deployed into the REIT. Before committing, examine the public filings for strategy, fees, redemption terms, NAV methodology, leverage, and underlying real estate. The public reports are one of the structure’s advantages; use them.

## Glossary

**PNLR:** A public non-listed REIT—SEC-registered and fully reporting, but not exchange-listed.

**Non-Traded REIT:** Another name for a PNLR: unlisted, illiquid, and NAV-priced.

**Private REIT:** An unregistered, exemption-based REIT for accredited and institutional investors.

**Listed REIT:** An exchange-traded, liquid, market-priced public REIT.

**SEC Registration:** Filing an offering with the SEC, triggering public reporting duties.

**Form 10-K:** A REIT’s audited annual report filed with the SEC.

**Form 10-Q:** A REIT’s quarterly interim financial report filed with the SEC.

**Form 8-K:** A current report disclosing material events to the SEC.

**Net Asset Value (NAV):** The periodic per-share value used to price a PNLR.

**Redemption Program:** A PNLR’s limited, capped, suspendable share-repurchase feature.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether an illiquid PNLR fits the investor.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for certain offerings.

**Prospectus:** The SEC-reviewed offering document disclosing strategy, fees, and risks.

**Sales Load:** Upfront selling and offering costs historically higher on PNLRs.

**90% Distribution Rule:** The REIT requirement a PNLR must meet to qualify.

**Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends, including PNLRs.
