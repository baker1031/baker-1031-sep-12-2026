## Phases of the CRE Cycle

Commercial real estate cycles turn on the balance between demand—occupancy and rents—and supply—new construction. In **recovery**, a prior downturn has left occupancy low, but it begins to stabilize and improve as the economy heals. Rents remain soft and little new construction is under way because the earlier oversupply is still being absorbed. Demand begins to outpace the limited new supply.

In **expansion**, occupancy rises above its long-term average and rent growth strengthens. Higher rents and values eventually justify construction, so the supply pipeline starts building. In **hyper-supply**, that construction outpaces demand: supply grows faster than absorption, occupancy peaks and softens, and rent growth slows even as projects started in expansion continue to deliver.

In **recession**, supply clearly exceeds demand. Vacancy rises, rents fall, construction halts, and values decline until the excess stock is absorbed and recovery begins again. The sequence is recurring, but its timing and severity vary across markets and sectors.

## How REITs Behave in Each Phase

REIT performance broadly follows the cycle, although not with a precise or mechanical schedule. Recovery can present opportunity: property values and REIT prices may still be depressed after the prior decline, while stabilizing occupancy, limited new supply, and the prospect of rent growth improve the foundation for income and value. Recognizing the turn can be valuable, but timing the bottom is difficult.

Expansion is usually the strongest phase. Rising occupancy and rents can lift property income and values, support distribution growth, and produce appreciation. In hyper-supply, new inventory pressures occupancy and slows rent growth; performance tends to flatten or weaken as the market anticipates a downturn. In the [recession phase, REITs typically underperform](reit-bear-markets-history-and-lessons.html): falling rents, higher vacancy, and declining values pressure income and prices, and distributions may be cut, especially at overleveraged or cyclically exposed REITs.

The broad pattern is opportunity in recovery, strength in expansion, a plateau in hyper-supply, and weakness in recession. It is a framework for expectations, not a trading signal.

## Leading Indicators to Watch

The cycle develops gradually, so several indicators can help place the market, even though none predicts perfectly. Occupancy and vacancy describe demand: rising occupancy generally signals recovery or expansion; rising vacancy can indicate hyper-supply or recession. Rent growth is closely related. Accelerating rents suggest a healthier market, while slowing or falling rents warn of oversupply or contraction.

Construction starts and the supply pipeline matter because today's building becomes tomorrow's available space. A surge in starts during expansion can foreshadow hyper-supply when projects deliver into a softer market. Cap rates—the ratio of net operating income to property value—show how the market prices property and risk. Rising cap rates often correspond with falling values or higher risk premiums; compressing cap rates can correspond with rising values.

Credit availability is another important signal. Easy credit can finance construction and acquisitions, extending an expansion and sometimes adding to oversupply. Tight credit can slow activity and make a downturn worse. Read occupancy, rents, construction, cap rates, and credit together rather than treating any single one as decisive.

## Defensive vs. Offensive Sectors

Sectors do not behave alike. Defensive, needs-based REIT sectors generally have less discretionary demand and may hold up relatively well across the cycle. Healthcare real estate, including medical office and senior housing, is supported by demographic need. Residential REITs, including apartments and manufactured housing, serve the persistent need for housing. Net-lease properties with long leases and creditworthy tenants can provide contractual income that is more insulated from short-term moves.

Offensive, or cyclical, sectors have more upside in good times and more downside in difficult ones. Hotel income is repriced nightly and is closely tied to economic conditions and travel demand; it can rise sharply in expansion and fall sharply in recession. Office has both economic-cycle exposure and sensitivity to changing work patterns. Some retail depends heavily on consumer spending. These sectors can outperform in expansion but are more vulnerable in a downturn.

A defensive tilt can add resilience; an offensive tilt adds more cyclical exposure. Diversifying across both can balance steadier income against participation in economic growth. No sector is immune, and the balance sheet, leverage, asset quality, and management of the specific REIT still matter.

## Key Takeaways

- The CRE cycle has four phases—recovery, expansion, hyper-supply, and recession—driven by the balance between demand (occupancy and rents) and supply (construction).
- REIT performance broadly tracks the cycle: recovery can offer opportunity, expansion is generally strongest, hyper-supply can plateau, and recession is usually weakest.
- Watch occupancy, rent growth, construction starts, cap rates, and credit availability; together they inform a view, but none predicts perfectly.
- Defensive, needs-based sectors such as healthcare, residential, and net-lease generally hold up better through cycles than hotels, office, and some retail. Long-term investing generally beats precise market-timing.

## How Interest Rates Shape the Cycle

Interest rates influence the cost and availability of credit, which can fuel or constrain construction and acquisitions. Easy, inexpensive credit can accelerate expansion and oversupply. Costly, tight credit can slow activity and make a downturn arrive sooner. Rates also affect cap rates and values: all else equal, higher rates often lead investors to demand higher yields, pushing cap rates up and values down; lower rates can allow cap rates to compress and values to rise.

For REITs, [rising rates raise borrowing and refinancing costs](reits-and-rising-rates-a-sector-playbook.html), which can pressure distributions, especially at leveraged companies. Higher fixed-income yields can also make REIT income less attractive and weigh on share prices. A downturn combined with high rates and tight credit is more punishing than one occurring in an easier refinancing environment.

Rates are both a cycle driver and a direct REIT influence. Their relationship with values, credit, and the broader economy is complicated and not perfectly predictable, so they belong alongside—not instead of—the supply-and-demand indicators.

## Long-Term Investing Through Cycles

The cycle is real, but it does not create an easy switching strategy. Turning points are visible in hindsight and difficult to trade in real time. Selling in a recession can lock in a decline; waiting for absolute certainty in recovery can mean missing part of a rebound. The most reliable discipline is usually a long-term one: own quality REITs with sound balance sheets and durable income, diversify across sectors and geographies, include defensive exposure where appropriate, reinvest distributions when that suits the plan, and stay invested through normal downturns.

That does not mean ignoring valuations or risk. It means using the cycle to form realistic expectations, assess leverage and distribution coverage, and decide whether the portfolio has become too concentrated—not to make an oversized bet on the next turn. REIT prices and distributions can fall. A measured, diversified allocation remains necessary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the commercial real estate cycle?

The commercial real estate, or CRE, cycle is the recurring movement of property markets through recovery, expansion, hyper-supply, and recession. It is driven by changes in demand—seen in occupancy and rents—and supply—seen in new construction. The cycle is not random, but it does not run on a fixed timetable. Its pace and severity vary by market and sector, which is why the framework should guide expectations rather than be treated as a precise forecast.

### What are the four phases of the real estate cycle?

Recovery follows a downturn: occupancy is low but stabilizing, rents are soft, and little construction is under way. Expansion brings higher occupancy and rents, which eventually support new building. Hyper-supply arrives when construction exceeds absorption; occupancy peaks and rent growth slows. Recession follows when supply exceeds demand, vacancy rises, rents fall, construction stops, and values decline until excess supply is absorbed. The cycle then returns to recovery.

### How do REITs perform in each phase of the cycle?

REITs often offer opportunity in recovery because prices and values may still reflect the prior decline while operating fundamentals improve. Expansion is usually the most supportive phase for income, distributions, and appreciation. Hyper-supply can flatten or weaken performance as new supply pressures occupancy and rents. Recession generally brings the greatest pressure on income and prices, with distribution cuts possible for leveraged or cyclical REITs. The relationship is broad, not precise, and sector selection and company quality matter.

### What leading indicators signal where the cycle stands?

Watch occupancy and vacancy, rent growth, construction starts and the supply pipeline, cap rates, and credit availability. Rising occupancy and rents can suggest recovery or expansion; rising vacancy and slower rents can signal hyper-supply or recession. Construction starts become future supply. Cap-rate movements show how the market is pricing income and risk. Easy credit can fund expansion, while tight credit can restrain it. No indicator predicts perfectly, so use the group of signals rather than one measure.

### What are defensive REIT sectors?

Defensive REIT sectors have needs-based demand that tends to be less discretionary. Healthcare, including medical office and senior housing, is tied to demographic demand. Residential REITs provide housing, and net-lease REITs can have long contracts with creditworthy tenants. These sectors may offer steadier income through a downturn than more cyclical sectors, though they still carry property, tenant, leverage, valuation, and market risks.

### What are offensive or cyclical REIT sectors?

Offensive sectors tend to move more with the economy. Hotels are highly sensitive because income is effectively repriced nightly. Office is exposed to the cycle and to changing work patterns. Some retail relies on consumer spending. These sectors can have more upside during expansion but often have more downside in recession. A higher potential return does not remove the need to examine leverage, lease structure, asset quality, and current valuation.

### Should I try to time the real estate cycle with REITs?

Precise cycle timing is difficult. The bottom of a recession and the start of recovery are clear only after the fact, and frequent trading can miss recoveries or lock in declines. A more durable approach is to hold quality REITs with sustainable income and sound balance sheets, diversify by sector and geography, keep the allocation in line with the plan, and use cycle knowledge to set expectations. That is not a guarantee; it is an effort to avoid letting a confident forecast become an oversized risk.

### How do interest rates affect the real estate cycle?

Rates affect the cost and availability of credit that funds acquisitions and construction. Easy credit can accelerate expansion and oversupply; tight credit can slow activity and hasten a downturn. Rates also influence cap rates and property values: higher rates can raise required yields and cap rates, putting pressure on values. For REITs, rising rates can increase borrowing and refinancing costs and can make fixed-income yields more competitive. The rate backdrop can amplify or soften a cycle phase, but its effect is not perfectly predictable.

### Are REITs a good long-term investment despite cycles?

They can be, for investors with realistic expectations and a through-the-cycle approach. Real estate has historically provided income and appreciation over long periods, and REITs are income-oriented because of their distribution requirements, including the general 90% taxable-income distribution rule. But REITs also have real risks: distributions can be cut, values fluctuate, and rates and cycles can hurt returns. Quality, diversification, appropriate sizing, and a long horizon are more durable than trying to trade every phase. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### What is a cap rate, and why does it matter for the cycle?

A capitalization rate, or cap rate, is net operating income divided by a property's value or price—roughly its unlevered income yield. When cap rates compress, investors pay more for each dollar of income and values generally rise. When cap rates expand, investors demand more income per dollar invested and values generally fall. Cap rates reflect risk premiums and are closely tied to interest rates, which makes their direction useful for reading valuation and the cycle. Review cap rates in the relevant REIT sectors and markets, not just in the aggregate.

### Which REIT sectors are most resilient in a downturn?

Residential, healthcare, and quality net-lease sectors often hold up relatively well because housing, demographic care needs, and long leases with creditworthy tenants produce less discretionary demand. Hotels, office, and some retail generally have more downside in a recession. No sector is immune. The specific REIT's balance sheet, leverage, management, asset quality, and tenant credit can matter as much as the sector label. A defensive tilt can soften the impact, while diversification remains prudent.

### How does the cycle affect REIT distributions?

Distributions ultimately come from property income. Recovery and expansion can improve occupancy and rents, supporting stable or growing distributions. Hyper-supply can flatten income growth and stall dividend increases. Recession can bring falling rents, rising vacancy, and tenant defaults, putting distributions at risk, especially at overleveraged companies or those with poorly covered payouts. Assess FFO and AFFO coverage and balance-sheet strength. Well-covered distributions at conservatively financed REITs are more likely to withstand stress than fragile high payouts, but there is no assurance.

### Can REITs help diversify against the economic cycle?

REITs can add real-estate exposure with supply-and-demand drivers that differ somewhat from stocks and bonds, and defensive sectors can add a measure of resilience. They are not a hedge that reliably moves opposite the economy. Publicly traded REITs can fall in broad market sell-offs, and recessions raise vacancies and pressure rents and values. The diversification value comes from exposure to real estate and to a range of sectors, not from immunity to a downturn. Combine REITs with other asset classes and diversify within REIT exposure.

## Glossary

- **CRE Cycle:** The recurring rhythm of commercial real estate markets.
- **Recovery Phase:** Low but stabilizing occupancy, soft rents, little construction.
- **Expansion Phase:** Rising occupancy and rents, with new construction ramping up.
- **Hyper-Supply Phase:** Construction outpacing demand as occupancy peaks and softens.
- **Recession Phase:** Supply exceeding demand, with rising vacancy and falling rents.
- **Occupancy Rate:** The share of space leased, signaling demand strength.
- **Absorption:** The rate at which available space is leased up.
- **Construction Starts:** New building activity that becomes future supply.
- **Cap Rate:** Net operating income divided by property value.
- **Credit Availability:** The ease of financing that fuels or constrains the cycle.
- **Defensive Sector:** Needs-based property (healthcare, residential, net-lease).
- **Offensive Sector:** Cyclical property (hotels, office, some retail).
- **Net-Lease:** A long lease where the tenant pays most operating costs.
- **Leading Indicator:** A signal that foreshadows the cycle's direction.
- **Time in the Market:** Long-term holding versus trying to time the cycle.
- **Interest-Rate Risk:** The risk rising rates pressure values and REIT prices.
