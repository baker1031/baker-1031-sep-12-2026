# Office DST Investing and Its Risks

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › Office DST Investing and Its Risks

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often a higher projected yield is mistaken for a sufficient investment thesis. In office today, the yield may be compensating the buyer for a real and durable demand problem, not presenting a free return.

First-order thinking sees “office” and assumes one property sector. Second-order thinking asks which building, in which submarket, with which tenants, which lease expirations, and what replacement demand. Hybrid and remote work have made the gap between a well-located Class A, medical, or government-leased asset and older commodity suburban space much wider. This is a candid guide to that demand headwind, quality differences, lease terms, tenant credit, and whether office should have a role in a 1031 exchange. ## The Office DST Investment Setting

An office DST holds office real estate leased to business tenants. Investors own fractional beneficial interests and receive their share of rental income, as in any DST. The category spans Class A towers in central business districts, trophy assets leased to major corporations, suburban office parks, medical office buildings, and government-leased space. Treating all of that as one thing obscures more than it explains.

The common backdrop is difficult. Hybrid and remote work have reduced demand for office space, elevated vacancy in many markets, softened rents, and reduced the value of weaker assets. This is a structural headwind, not a short-lived dip. The difference between a well-located, well-leased, high-quality building and a commodity building in a soft submarket can be the difference between durable income and a struggling investment.

Office is the most challenged major property sector. That does not make every office asset identical or uninvestable, but it does make careful selection essential.

## Remote Work and Demand Risk

The defining risk is a sustained reduction in the space companies need. When employees work from home some or all of the time, tenants may renew for smaller footprints or not renew at all. Higher vacancy and lower rents directly pressure the income an office property can produce.

The long-term balance of office demand is still settling, but hybrid work appears durable. That gives tenants more leverage to seek concessions, including free rent and tenant-improvement allowances. It also affects eventual sale value: weak demand and higher cap rates can reduce property value. Income and sale value are both exposed.

There is no useful way to soft-pedal this. Hybrid and remote work have structurally reduced office demand, raising vacancy and pressuring rents. It is the core risk an investor must assess before an office DST enters an exchange.

## Class A vs. Suburban Office

The divide between high-quality and commodity office has widened. Class A and trophy buildings are modern, well-amenitized properties in prime locations. In a softer market, tenants that still lease space have increasingly concentrated demand in the best assets—a flight to quality. Companies use better space to attract employees back, so the best buildings have retained tenants and pricing power more effectively.

Commodity suburban office and older, lower-quality buildings have carried much more of the downturn. They may lack the amenities, location, and appeal that help tenants compete for employees. The result can be higher vacancy, weaker rents, and steeper declines in value.

Specialized office can be more resilient. Medical office is tied to in-person health care, and government-leased office can be backed by stable, creditworthy tenants on long leases. Quality, location, and property type create a large spread in resilience and risk. The most capable assets have held up much better than older and commodity office.

## Lease Terms & Tenant Quality

In a challenged sector, tenant credit and lease structure matter even more. A property leased to strong-credit, stable tenants on long-term leases has income visibility that can cushion the broader weakness—at least for the lease term. A tenant’s financial strength and need for physical space are central underwriting questions.

Lease-rollover risk is the specific danger. When a lease expires into a soft market, the tenant may take less space, ask for free rent or tenant-improvement allowances, accept a lower rent, or leave entirely. Re-leasing vacant space can be slow and costly, with no income until it is filled. A weak tenant may also default.

Read the rent roll and lease-expiration schedule closely. A building with strong-credit tenants and long remaining lease terms is better positioned than one facing major expirations in a weak market. In office, the investor is underwriting the tenants and leases as much as the building.

## Key Takeaways

- Office is the most challenged major property sector, with a durable hybrid- and remote-work headwind, elevated vacancy, and softer rents.
- Quality and location matter: Class A, trophy, medical, and government office have generally held up better than commodity suburban and older office.
- Strong-credit tenants and long leases can support income visibility; lease rollover into a soft market is a major risk.
- Office requires more selectivity and caution than other sectors. It can fit some exchanges, but only as a well-chosen, appropriately sized asset with strong tenants and durable leases.

## Is Office Right for Your Exchange?

The answer should be cautious and selective. Given the demand headwind, office is not a casual allocation and should not generally be the centerpiece of an exchange. A high-quality, well-located office DST with strong-credit tenants, long-term leases, and a resilient niche such as medical or government office can play a role in a diversified replacement portfolio. It should ordinarily be measured, not dominant.

The reason is straightforward. Office faces income risk from vacancy and soft rents and value risk from weak demand and higher cap rates. Scrutinize asset quality, location, tenant credit, lease terms, rent roll, and the rollover schedule rather than relying on the sector label. Sectors with stronger fundamentals may fit the core of an exchange better.

Office is not off the table, but it is no longer a default. If included, it deserves careful asset-level due diligence and a size that recognizes its elevated sector risk.

## Office in a Diversified Exchange

If office has a place in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), it is usually a limited part of a diversified replacement portfolio rather than a standalone bet. Relatively low DST minimums can let an exchanger spread proceeds across several trusts, anchor the exchange in sectors with stronger fundamentals—[grocery-anchored necessity retail](grocery-anchored-retail-dsts.html), industrial, multifamily, or medical office—and keep a carefully selected office DST as a limited component.

Diversification can limit the portfolio effect if office demand remains weak. Within the office allocation, more resilient niches can be preferable: medical office, [government-leased space backed by creditworthy GSA tenants](government-leased-gsa-dsts.html), and well-located Class A office have generally fared better than commodity suburban space.

The discipline is sizing. Do not allow office’s sometimes higher projected yields to tempt an outsized allocation to the most challenged property type. A deliberate, limited slice selected for quality and lease durability is the more defensible role, if office is included at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an office DST?

An office DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust holding office buildings leased to business tenants. Investors own fractional beneficial interests and receive a share of rental income. Office can include Class A and trophy towers, suburban parks, medical office, and government-leased space. Under [Revenue Ruling 2004-86](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rr-04-86.pdf), a DST interest can be treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. Office works mechanically like another DST, but it carries elevated demand risk and requires selective evaluation of the asset, location, tenants, and lease terms.

### Why is office considered the most challenged property sector?

Hybrid and remote work have structurally reduced the space companies need. As leases expire, tenants may take smaller footprints or leave, raising vacancy, weakening rents, and reducing the value of weaker assets. The demand balance is still settling, but the headwind appears durable rather than a passing cycle. Industrial, necessity retail, and multifamily do not face the same pressure to the same degree, which is why office needs more caution.

### How has remote work affected office DSTs?

Less office demand can weaken both income and property value. Higher vacancy makes it harder to keep a building leased, lower demand can push rents down, and tenants gain leverage for free rent or tenant-improvement allowances. At expiry they may renew for less space, renew at lower rents, or leave; refilling vacant space can be slow and costly. High-quality, well-located, well-leased assets have generally held up better than commodity office, but remote work remains the central risk.

### Is all office space equally risky?

No. The risk spread is large. Class A and trophy buildings in prime locations can benefit from a flight to quality and retain tenants and pricing power more effectively. Commodity suburban and older buildings can face higher vacancy, weaker rents, and steeper value declines. Medical office, tied to in-person care, and government-leased office with stable, creditworthy tenants on long leases can be more resilient. Selection matters more than in almost any other property type.

### What is the difference between Class A and commodity office?

Class A office is high-quality, modern, well-amenitized space in prime locations, frequently with strong tenant rosters; trophy assets are the very best of that group. Commodity office is generally older or suburban space that lacks comparable location, amenities, or appeal and competes mainly on price. The flight to quality has allowed the best assets to retain tenants, occupancy, and pricing power better, while commodity office has faced higher vacancy, weaker rents, and steeper value declines.

### What is lease-rollover risk in an office DST?

It is the risk that expiring lease income cannot be replaced on favorable terms. A tenant in a weak market may renew for less space, demand concessions such as free rent or tenant-improvement allowances, accept a lower rent, or leave. The resulting vacancy can be slow and costly to re-lease. Review remaining lease length, the rent roll, and the lease-expiration schedule. Long remaining terms with strong-credit tenants provide more income visibility than major leases rolling in a downturn.

### Can I still use an office DST in a 1031 exchange?

Yes. An office DST can be like-kind replacement property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. The usual 1031 mechanics remain: a 45-day identification window, a 180-day closing window, replacement of equity and typically debt, and avoidance of boot. The more relevant question is whether an office DST is suitable given its sector risk. If it is included, inspect quality, location, tenant credit, and lease rollover, and consider a measured allocation rather than a centerpiece.

### What types of office are more resilient?

Class A and trophy office in prime locations have generally held up better through flight to quality. Medical office can be more resilient because care is delivered in person, tenants need physical space, and specialized build-outs can make them less likely to relocate. Government-leased office can benefit from stable, creditworthy tenants on long leases. Well-located office in supply-constrained or growing markets has generally fared better than office in oversupplied or declining ones. These categories do not guarantee an individual property’s performance; evaluate its tenants, location, and leases.

### Why does tenant quality matter so much in office DSTs?

In office, strong-credit, stable tenants on long-term leases can provide income visibility and cushion sector weakness for the term of the lease. Weaker tenants may default, and leases expiring soon may not renew or may renew on poorer terms. Tenant credit and lease duration therefore largely determine whether income is durable or vulnerable. The building alone is not enough; the tenants and their leases require close underwriting.

### Should office be the centerpiece of my 1031 exchange?

Generally no. Office’s structural demand headwind exposes both income and value, so a concentrated office position puts an exchange in the most challenged sector. A carefully chosen high-quality asset with strong-credit tenants and long leases may have a place in a diversified portfolio, especially in medical or government office, but it should usually be measured. Necessity retail, industrial, multifamily, or medical office may be better anchors for the core. Decide with advisors based on goals and risk tolerance.

### What are the main risks of an office DST?

Demand risk from hybrid and remote work raises vacancy and pressures rents. Lease-rollover risk can lead to smaller renewals, lower rents, costly concessions, or vacancies. Empty space can be slow and expensive to fill. Weak demand and higher cap rates can reduce eventual sale value, and a tenant default is harder to backfill in a challenged sector. Standard DST risks also apply: illiquidity through a multi-year cycle, fees, sponsor execution, debt and interest-rate exposure, and distributions that are projections rather than guarantees. Quality, location, tenant credit, and lease terms can manage but do not eliminate these risks. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How do I evaluate whether an office DST is worth the risk?

Evaluate the individual asset, not merely the office label. Ask whether it is well-located Class A, a resilient medical or government niche, or commodity suburban office. Assess tenant credit, stability, need for physical space, remaining lease length, rent roll, and expiry schedule. Review submarket health and supply, sponsor track record, debt, fees, and portfolio diversification. Consider whether more resilient sectors better serve the exchange’s core. Demand and return considerations are general and non-promissory, and no distribution is guaranteed. Make the decision with advisors after rigorous asset-level review.

### Is medical office a safer alternative to traditional office?

Medical office is generally more resilient than commodity office because patients need in-person visits to doctors, dentists, imaging centers, and clinics. Medical tenants can invest heavily in specialized space such as exam rooms and equipment, which may make them less likely to move; health-care use can also be supported by an aging population. “Safer” is relative, not absolute. Tenant credit, location, lease terms, and all standard DST risks still apply, and a resilient category does not guarantee a particular property’s outcome.

### Should office be a measured or a large part of my exchange?

Office should generally be a measured, limited allocation rather than a large or concentrated one. Its vacancy, rent, and value risks are greater than those of more resilient sectors. Low DST minimums can allow proceeds to be spread across several trusts, with office kept a small component. Favoring medical, government, or well-located Class A can limit risk further. Resist the temptation to treat a higher projected yield as a reason for an outsized allocation; set any weighting with advisors based on your goals and risk tolerance.

## Glossary

**Office DST:** A DST holding income-producing office real estate.

**Class A Office:** The highest-quality, well-located, amenitized office buildings.

**Trophy Asset:** A premier, best-in-class office property in a prime location.

**Suburban Office:** Office in suburban locations, often more exposed in the downturn.

**Commodity Office:** Lower-quality office competing mainly on price.

**Medical Office:** Specialized, in-person healthcare office, relatively resilient.

**Government-Leased Office:** Office leased to stable, creditworthy government tenants.

**Flight to Quality:** Tenant demand concentrating in the best office buildings.

**Remote Work Headwind:** The structural demand decline from hybrid and remote work.

**Vacancy:** Empty office space producing no income until re-leased.

**Lease Rollover:** The risk of leases expiring into a soft demand market.

**Rent Roll:** The schedule of tenants, rents, and lease expirations.

**Tenant Credit:** The financial strength of an office tenant paying rent.

**Concessions:** Free rent or improvement allowances given to attract tenants.

**Cap Rate:** A yield measure affecting an office property's value.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust owning real estate with fractional, 1031-eligible interests.
