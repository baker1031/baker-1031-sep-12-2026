# REIT Dividends and the 20% QBI Deduction

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep finding that investors remember the headline REIT yield and forget to ask what portion they actually keep after tax. Qualified REIT dividends can receive a 20% deduction under Section 199A. That can lower the effective federal rate on the ordinary portion of REIT income, but it is not a universal answer for every account or return.

First-order thinking says “REIT dividends are ordinary income, so put them in an IRA.” Second-order thinking sees the trade-off: the deduction lives in taxable accounts, is subject to an overall return-level cap, and must be considered beside bracket, account mix, return of capital, and capital gains. This is educational material, not tax advice.

## Key Takeaways

- The 20% deduction applies to the ordinary qualified-REIT-dividend portion of a distribution—the portion taxed at ordinary rates.
- At the 37% top federal rate, it lowers the effective rate from 37% to roughly 29.6%; the proportional benefit applies at lower brackets.
- Unlike the business side of Section 199A, qualified REIT dividends have no wage, property, or income-phase-out limit.
- The deduction works only in taxable accounts, is capped overall at 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain, and the 2025 OBBBA made it permanent.

[REIT dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income](reit-taxation-how-reit-dividends-are-taxed.html) because a REIT generally pays no corporate tax and passes earnings through to shareholders. That can feel tax-heavy compared with the qualified dividends a typical stock pays. Section 199A provides a distinct 20% deduction for the qualified ordinary REIT-dividend portion. The deduction is one tax feature among several; our [guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html) explains why their dividend structures matter. Verify current rules and individual treatment with a tax advisor.

## What the QBI Deduction Is

Section 199A created the qualified business income, or QBI, deduction. Its business component can allow owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships, S corporations, and other pass-through businesses to deduct up to 20% of qualifying business income. That business component can be subject to wage and property (UBIA) limits and income-based phase-outs for certain service businesses. Congress designed the provision to give pass-through owners a benefit roughly comparable to the lower corporate tax rate, so pass-through income would not be taxed far more heavily than C-corporation income.

The REIT-dividend component is separate. It allows a 20% deduction for qualified REIT dividends, including amounts passed through from qualifying publicly traded partnership income. For this REIT portion, the wage, property, and income-phase-out limits that constrain the business component do not apply. The overall taxable-income cap still matters.

## How It Applies to REIT Dividends

The REIT or brokerage reports a distribution’s components on Form 1099-DIV. Most REIT dividends are ordinary, non-qualified income. The qualified REIT-dividend portion is eligible for the 20% Section 199A deduction because it is ordinary income passed through from a REIT.

The deduction does not apply to every distribution component. Capital-gain distributions are taxed at capital-gains rates; return of capital is not currently taxed but reduces cost basis; and a qualified-dividend portion is already subject to capital-gains treatment. The 1099-DIV identifies the Section 199A-eligible figure. Confirm the classification with a CPA.

## Calculating the Benefit

Multiply qualified REIT dividends on Form 1099-DIV by 20%. The result is a deduction from taxable income, not a tax credit. For example, $10,000 of qualified REIT dividends creates a $2,000 deduction, leaving $8,000 of that income subject to tax. At a 37% federal rate, 37% times 80% (or 37% × 0.8) produces an effective rate of roughly 29.6%; at a 32% rate, the effective rate would be about 25.6%.

The total Section 199A deduction—business and REIT pieces combined—cannot exceed 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain. The cap is often not binding, but it can limit the benefit in a year with little other taxable income or substantial capital gains. Illustrative figures are not a projection; have a CPA calculate the return-level result.

## Account-Type Considerations

The deduction is available only in a taxable account. In a traditional IRA, Roth IRA, or 401(k), REIT dividends are not currently taxed as received, so there is no current taxable income for the deduction to reduce. Traditional-IRA withdrawals are later taxed as ordinary income without a REIT-dividend deduction; qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free regardless of Section 199A.

This creates a genuine placement trade-off. A tax-advantaged account shelters mostly ordinary REIT income, but it forfeits the 20% taxable-account deduction. A taxable account can claim the deduction but exposes the ordinary portion to current tax. Account location depends on bracket, allocation, account mix, return-of-capital and capital-gain components, and planning goals. See [traditional IRA, Roth IRA, or 401(k)](investing-in-reits-with-a-self-directed-ira.html) and [where to hold REITs](reits-in-taxable-vs-tax-advantaged-accounts.html) for related context. A CPA can model the alternatives.

## Coordinating With Your CPA

The rule is comparatively clean; a tax return is not. The 1099-DIV provides the components, the QBI forms calculate the deduction, and the return-level cap can interact with business QBI, capital gains, and account decisions. A CPA can confirm the qualified figure, apply the cap, reconcile business-side QBI, and model taxable versus IRA placement as rules and income change. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the QBI deduction on REIT dividends?

It is a federal Section 199A deduction equal to 20% of qualified REIT dividends. It applies to the ordinary qualified-REIT-dividend portion reported on Form 1099-DIV. Since REIT income is mostly ordinary income, it can otherwise feel tax-heavy compared with the qualified dividends a typical stock pays. At a 37% top bracket it lowers the effective rate to roughly 29.6%. The 2025 OBBBA made the deduction permanent, but verify current rules with a tax advisor.

### What is Section 199A?

It is the tax-code provision creating the QBI deduction. One component applies to pass-through business income and can have wage, UBIA property, and income-phase-out constraints. A separate component applies to qualified REIT dividends and qualified publicly traded partnership income without those REIT-specific limits. Its purpose was to give pass-through income a benefit roughly comparable to the lower corporate tax rate.

### How much does the QBI deduction lower my tax on REIT dividends?

It deducts 20% of qualified dividends from taxable income. A $10,000 eligible amount produces a $2,000 deduction; at the 37% rate, the effective rate is about 29.6%, and at 32% it is about 25.6%. It is a deduction, not a credit, so the dollar benefit varies by bracket and may be limited by the overall cap.

### Does the QBI deduction on REIT dividends have an income limit?

No income-based phase-out applies to the REIT-dividend component. The business component has wage, property, and service-business income limits; qualified REIT dividends do not. The combined QBI deduction still cannot exceed 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain.

### Which part of a REIT dividend qualifies for the 20% deduction?

Only the ordinary qualified-REIT-dividend component. It does not apply to capital-gain distributions, return of capital, or the smaller qualified-dividend component. Form 1099-DIV separates the components and reports the eligible Section 199A figure.

### Is the QBI deduction on REIT dividends permanent?

As of June 2026, the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the Section 199A deduction permanent, removing its scheduled end-of-2025 sunset. Tax law can change, so permanent does not mean frozen; verify current treatment before planning.

### Do I get the QBI deduction on REIT dividends inside an IRA?

No. Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, and 401(k)s do not tax dividends currently, leaving no current taxable income for the deduction. Traditional-IRA withdrawals are ordinary income later and qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free. Holding REITs in those accounts shelters ordinary income but gives up the taxable-account deduction.

### Should I hold REITs in a taxable account to get the deduction?

Not automatically. A taxable account is the only place to claim it, but ordinary REIT income may also be tax-inefficient. The better placement depends on bracket, REIT allocation, account mix, and the distribution’s return-of-capital and capital-gain elements. Model it with a CPA; this is educational, not advice.

### Is there a cap on the QBI deduction?

Yes. Business and REIT deductions together cannot exceed 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain. The cap can bind in a low-income year or where large capital gains reduce the calculation base. A CPA applies it in the context of the complete return.

### Do I have to itemize to claim the QBI deduction?

No. It is a separate below-the-line deduction available whether the taxpayer uses the standard deduction or itemizes. The Form 1099-DIV amount flows to the relevant QBI forms and remains subject to the overall cap.

### How do I find the qualifying REIT-dividend amount?

Use Form 1099-DIV. It reports ordinary, qualified, capital-gain, nondividend return-of-capital, and the specific Section 199A dividend amounts. Funds and ETFs holding REITs can also pass through an eligible amount. A CPA can confirm which box and component qualify.

### Does the deduction apply to REIT capital-gain distributions?

No. Capital-gain distributions arise when a REIT realizes long-term gains on property sales and are already taxed at the lower long-term capital-gains rates, so they are not part of the ordinary qualified-REIT-dividend slice. Return of capital and qualified dividends are likewise not Section 199A eligible.

### How does the deduction interact with return of capital?

They apply to different components. Return of capital is not currently taxable and reduces basis, deferring tax to sale. It is not an ordinary qualified REIT dividend. A single distribution can contain both Section 199A-eligible ordinary income and return of capital; track basis and apply the deduction separately.

### Does the QBI deduction apply to REIT funds and ETFs?

Generally, qualified REIT dividends passed through by a fund or ETF are eligible in a taxable account, subject to the overall cap. The fund reports the eligible amount on Form 1099-DIV. The benefit does not apply inside tax-advantaged accounts.

## Glossary

### QBI Deduction

The Section 199A deduction of up to 20% of qualifying pass-through income.

### Section 199A

The tax-code provision creating the QBI deduction.

### Qualified REIT Dividend

The ordinary REIT-dividend portion eligible for the 20% deduction.

### Ordinary Dividend

The REIT-dividend portion taxed at ordinary income rates.

### Effective Rate

The real federal rate after the deduction, about 29.6% at the top bracket.

### Wage/UBIA Limit

Business-side limits that do not apply to REIT dividends.

### Income Phase-Out

A high-earner limit on the business side, absent for REIT dividends.

### Taxable-Income Cap

The overall ceiling of 20% of taxable income minus net capital gain.

### Form 1099-DIV

The form reporting REIT distribution components, including Section 199A dividends.

### Below-the-Line Deduction

A deduction available whether or not you itemize.

### OBBBA

The 2025 law that made the Section 199A deduction permanent.

### Taxable Account

The only account type where the QBI deduction benefits REIT dividends.

### Tax-Advantaged Account

An IRA or 401(k) where the QBI deduction provides no benefit.

### Capital-Gain Distribution

A REIT payout taxed at capital-gains rates, not QBI-eligible.

### Return of Capital

A distribution that reduces basis, not QBI-eligible.

### Pass-Through Income

Business income taxed at the owner level, the basis for Section 199A.
