# Illustrative 1031 Case Studies

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep seeing the same mistake in how people discuss a 1031 exchange: they treat it as a tax switch, when it is really a decision about what the next ownership chapter should look like. The first-order view is simple—defer tax. The second-order question is what the deferral lets an owner change: work, concentration, timing, cash needs, or the eventual transfer of wealth.

These are hypothetical composite illustrations, not actual client stories or promises of results. They show a tired landlord moving to passive DSTs, a buyer using a reverse exchange under deadline pressure, an investor diversifying across sectors, an estate-focused owner using a long-horizon strategy, and an owner taking some planned cash. Individual results vary. Some exchanges do not go as planned, and DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.

The mechanics become clearer in situations with real objectives. Deferral, deadlines, replacement choices, and estate planning can fit together in different ways. The practical lesson is to begin with the goal, then choose the structure that can support it.

## Case study: tired landlord to passive DSTs

*Hypothetical composite illustration; not an actual client result; individual results will vary; DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.*

After thirty years and four single-family rentals, a landlord in her sixties no longer found the tenant calls, repairs, and vacancies worth the work. The properties had appreciated substantially. An outright sale could trigger a four-layer tax stack—capital gains, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax—that might claim a third or more of the gain. She wanted reliable retirement income without giving up a third of her wealth to tax.

Through a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html), she exchanged the rental-sale proceeds into a diversified group of [Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html): passive, professionally managed interests in institutional apartments, industrial properties, and medical office buildings across several markets. The exchange deferred the gain, leaving the full value of the rentals invested rather than only an after-tax remainder. Tenant calls and repairs gave way to quarterly distributions and no day-to-day management while she remained invested in real estate.

That trade has limits. DSTs are illiquid securities sold only to accredited investors through a private placement memorandum. They carry fees and a risk of loss of principal. Their distributions are not guaranteed and can be reduced or suspended.

Still, this common tired-landlord exchange pursued three goals at once: leave active management, diversify several local rentals into institutional real estate across markets, and defer a large tax. DST depreciation can partly shelter new income. Holding DST interests until the eventual [step-up at death](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) could erase deferred gain entirely. The exchange can convert a management-intensive, concentrated, heavily taxed position into passive, diversified, tax-deferred retirement income; it does not make the investment risk-free.

## Case study: reverse exchange under deadline

*Hypothetical composite illustration; not an actual client result; individual results will vary; DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.*

Another investor found an exceptional, well-located commercial replacement property before selling the property already owned. It rarely came to market. A standard forward exchange—sell first, then buy—would not work because the replacement would not wait. Buying it outright and trying to label it a 1031 later would not work either. The investor risked losing the ideal replacement or losing the deferral unless buying first was possible.

The solution was a [reverse 1031 exchange](reverse-1031-exchange.html). An exchange accommodation titleholder parked the replacement under the Revenue Procedure 2000-37 safe harbor while the investor arranged the existing-property sale. The replacement was acquired through the parking arrangement, funded with bridge financing; the existing property then sold within the required window. The investor preserved the deferral and secured a replacement that otherwise would have been lost.

The first-order appeal is obvious: do not lose the property. The second-order cost is a more complex structure, bridge financing, added expense, and pressure to sell the relinquished property in time. If that sale does not occur by the deadline, the structure can unwind, the bridge financing still must be repaid, and the intended deferral can be lost. A reverse exchange is useful when buying first is truly necessary, but it needs experienced professionals and a realistic sale plan.

> The reverse exchange secured a replacement that forward-exchange timing would have foreclosed, at the cost of more complexity, financing, and deadline pressure.

## Case study: diversifying into multiple sectors

*Hypothetical composite illustration; not an actual client result; individual results will vary; DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.*

A different investor held one large commercial property that represented most of net worth. One property type, one market, and one tenant base carried too much of the load. Appreciation made selling expensive, but concentration created its own risk: if that property or market faltered, much of the investor's wealth would be exposed.

The exchange became a portfolio-construction tool. The investor sold the single property and placed the value across several DSTs spanning residential, industrial, and net-lease retail, plus different geographies and perhaps a royalty-pool DST for energy exposure. By using the [identification rules to name multiple replacements](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html), the investor shifted from one concentrated asset to a spread across sectors, markets, and asset classes while deferring the gain.

The first-order story is diversification. The second-order reality is that diversification lowers concentration risk; it does not remove market risk. The DSTs can still lose value and their distributions are not guaranteed. Yet depreciation across the DSTs may shelter income, no single asset's underperformance determines the whole result, and the deferral keeps the full value working. For an owner whose wealth is dangerously concentrated, this is tax-deferred rebalancing as much as it is tax deferral.

## Case study: estate-focused swap-till-you-drop

*Hypothetical composite illustration; not an actual client result; individual results will vary; DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.*

A fourth investor was thinking beyond one exchange. After years of owning and exchanging real estate, deferring gain and trading up, the focus had moved to passing accumulated wealth to children as tax-efficiently as possible while reducing management work.

The strategy combined several tools. Management-intensive properties were exchanged into passive DSTs. At a DST's full cycle, the investor might move into a REIT through a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) for further diversification and liquidity. That kept the deferral intact and the chain unbroken while reducing hands-on work. The investor held the passive interests for life and drew income while deferred gain continued to accumulate.

At death, heirs receive a stepped-up basis that can erase the deferred gain. This swap-till-you-drop approach compounds on the full pre-tax base, uses DST and 721 off-ramps without breaking the chain, and can leave heirs a diversified portfolio without embedded income-tax liability from the deferrals. The step-up depends on current law and on holding until death, so an estate attorney needs to be part of the planning.

## Lessons across the stories

A 1031 exchange can serve more than an abstract tax objective. It can help an owner move toward passivity, seize an opportunity, diversify a concentrated position, or build a legacy. Deferral is the common engine, but it enables different outcomes depending on the objective.

Preparation and the right structure decided each illustration. The tired landlord planned DST replacements. The reverse exchange required bridge financing and experienced professionals. The diversified portfolio required multiple-property identification. The estate strategy joined DSTs, 721s, and estate planning over decades. None of these outcomes came from improvisation.

DSTs recur because they can be passive, diversified, fast-closing, and qualifying replacement property. They are also illiquid, fee-bearing, and subject to loss of value; their distributions are not guaranteed. The durable lesson is straightforward: know the goal, choose the structure that fits it, and prepare early with the right guidance.

### Key takeaways

- A 1031 exchange can support management exit, opportunity, diversification, and multigenerational wealth—not only tax deferral.
- Every case needed the right structure and sound execution, not improvisation.
- DSTs can serve as a passive replacement, diversifier, and estate off-ramp, but they are illiquid and distributions are not guaranteed.
- Deliberate planning, not luck, produced the illustrated outcomes. These are illustrations, not promises.

## Applying the lessons to your exchange

Start by deciding which goal most resembles yours. Are you a tired landlord seeking passivity? An owner with a concentrated position? Someone who has found an opportunity before selling? Or someone focused on the estate finish line? Real exchanges often blend goals—diversifying while going passive, or trading up while planning for an estate—but the primary goal should orient the work.

That goal suggests both structure and team. Passivity can point toward DST replacements. A property that must be bought before the sale can point toward a reverse exchange. Diversification can call for multiple replacements under the identification rules. A legacy plan can involve swap-till-you-drop thinking and estate coordination. A qualified intermediary, CPA, advisor, and estate attorney may each have a distinct role.

These outcomes are available but not automatic. Individual facts, market conditions, and execution determine the result. The cases show a process: a clear goal, an appropriate structure, experienced guidance, and preparation applied to the owner's facts.

## Case study: cashing out part while deferring the rest

*Hypothetical composite illustration; not an actual client result; individual results will vary; DST distributions and outcomes are not guaranteed.*

A fifth investor wanted to exchange but also needed meaningful cash—perhaps for a child's education, other obligations, or some realization after years of appreciation. A full exchange would defer all gain but leave no cash. A full sale would produce cash but trigger the entire four-layer tax. Neither extreme fit.

A [partial exchange](partial-1031-exchange-taking-cash-out.html) offered a middle path. The investor reinvested most proceeds into replacement property, deferring gain on that portion, while deliberately taking a planned amount of cash as [boot](1031-exchange-boot.html) and paying tax only on that portion. On a roughly $1 million gain, the illustration takes $150,000 in cash at closing, pays tax on that $150,000 of boot, and defers the remaining $850,000. Those amounts were modeled with the CPA before listing so the taxable amount was the intended target.

The 1031 is not all-or-nothing. A planned partial exchange can provide cash for a genuine need while deferring most of the gain. The key is to model boot and its tax with the CPA in advance rather than stumble into accidental boot by buying down or not replacing debt. This is an illustration, not a promise of a particular result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are these 1031 case studies real clients?

No. They are composite illustrations that show how strategies can come together, not client accounts or promises of results. Each investor's facts and outcome differ.

### What is the tired landlord exchange?

It is a common situation in which a longtime rental owner exchanges management-intensive rentals into passive DSTs. The owner can leave tenant calls and repairs, diversify a concentrated position, and defer a large tax while pursuing passive, diversified, tax-deferred income, often with an eventual step-up at death in mind.

### When would I need a reverse exchange?

When an exceptional replacement appears before you sell the current property and it will not wait. An EAT parking arrangement under Revenue Procedure 2000-37 acquires the replacement first, then the relinquished property must be sold within the window. It is more complex and costly, but can preserve an opportunity a forward exchange would foreclose.

### How does the 1031 help me diversify?

It lets an owner exchange one concentrated property into multiple replacements, such as several DSTs across sectors and markets and perhaps other asset classes, using the identification rules to name them. That can replace a single point of failure with a more resilient portfolio without the friction of selling and reinvesting after tax.

### What is the estate-focused swap-till-you-drop strategy?

It means chaining exchanges over a lifetime, using DST and 721 off-ramps to go passive without breaking the deferral chain, then holding until death. Heirs receive a stepped-up basis that can erase accumulated deferred gain and inherit real-estate wealth without that deferred income tax.

### What lessons run across the case studies?

The 1031 can serve passivity, opportunity, diversification, and legacy, not merely abstract deferral. Each illustration depends on the right structure, preparation, and execution rather than improvisation. DSTs are versatile but also illiquid and subject to investment risk.

### Can my exchange achieve outcomes like these?

Potentially, if it begins with a clear goal, appropriate structure, experienced guidance, and preparation. The outcomes are not automatic; your results depend on your facts. The deliberate process behind the illustrations can be applied to different investor goals.

### Why do DSTs appear in so many scenarios?

Their passive, diversified, fast-closing structure and 1031 eligibility make DSTs useful as a tired-landlord replacement, a diversification tool, and an estate off-ramp. That versatility explains their prominent role in modern 1031 planning.

### How do I know which scenario fits me?

Identify your primary goal: passivity, buying an opportunity before selling, diversification, or legacy. Many exchanges combine elements, but the main goal guides the suitable structure and team. An advisor can help match the goal and strategy.

### Are the tax outcomes in the case studies guaranteed?

No. Tax outcomes depend on individual facts, current law, and proper execution. Deferral requires meeting the rules, and a step-up depends on holding until death under current law. Model the situation with a CPA; the cases show possibilities, not promises.

### What's the most important factor in a successful exchange?

The common thread is a clear goal, a structure suited to it, and deliberate preparation with experienced professionals. A qualified intermediary, CPA, advisor, and estate attorney can each be important. None of the illustrations depended on improvisation.

### Do I need a long-term plan or can I do one exchange?

Either can fit. Passivity, diversification, and an opportunity can be addressed in one exchange. An estate-focused approach unfolds over decades. The right horizon depends on the owner's goals.

### Can I take some cash and still do an exchange?

Yes. A partial exchange reinvests most proceeds and defers that gain while taking planned cash as taxable boot. Unreplaced debt can also be taxable. Full deferral generally requires full reinvestment and debt replacement.

### How do I avoid accidental boot in a partial exchange?

Plan the cash-out with a CPA before the sale. Model exactly how much cash will be retained and what tax it creates, then structure the transaction to reach that target. Buying down or not replacing debt can create accidental boot.

### Which case study is most common?

The tired-landlord exchange into passive DSTs is among the most common, reflecting a desire to leave management while deferring tax. Diversification and partial-cash-out scenarios are also frequent. Reverse exchanges and full estate-focused strategies tend to be used for more specific situations.

## Glossary

**Case Study:** An illustrative composite scenario showing how an exchange can serve a goal; it is not a specific client result.

**Tired Landlord Exchange:** Exchanging management-intensive rentals into passive DSTs to leave management while deferring tax.

**Reverse Exchange:** Acquiring the replacement before selling through a parking arrangement to seize an opportunity.

**Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT):** The entity that parks property in a reverse exchange under Revenue Procedure 2000-37.

**Diversification:** Tax-deferred spreading of a concentrated position across sectors and markets through multiple replacements.

**Swap Till You Drop:** Chaining exchanges and holding until death so a step-up can erase deferred gain.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset at death that can eliminate accumulated deferred gain for heirs.

**721 Exchange (UPREIT):** Contributing property to a REIT operating partnership for units; it can be an estate-planning off-ramp.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A versatile passive, diversified, qualifying replacement used across several scenarios.

**Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax—the sale-tax burden an exchange can defer.

**Identification Rules:** The three-property, 200%, and 95% rules that permit multiple replacements for diversification.

**Bridge Financing:** Short-term financing used to fund a replacement purchase in a reverse exchange.

**Concentration Risk:** Exposure created by one large position; diversification through an exchange can address it.

**Estate Planning:** Coordinating an exchange strategy with transfer of wealth at death.

**Passive Income:** Income from investments requiring no management, such as DST interests.

**Suitability Review:** The assessment of whether a securities product such as a DST fits an investor.
