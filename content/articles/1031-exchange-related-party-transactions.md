# 1031 Exchange and Related-Party Transactions

I keep seeing related-party exchanges described as ordinary swaps with a family wrinkle. They are not. The relationship changes the tax analysis because the parties can coordinate price, basis, and timing in ways unrelated parties generally cannot.

Section 1031 permits these exchanges, but adds special safeguards. The central one is a mutual two-year holding requirement: an early disposition by either party can retroactively unwind the deferral. The definitions are broader than casual intuition, and indirect arrangements can be scrutinized as closely as direct swaps.

## What counts as a related party

For 1031 purposes, “related party” draws from Sections 267(b) and 707(b), not from casual family language. On the family side it includes a spouse, siblings, ancestors such as parents and grandparents, and lineal descendants such as children and grandchildren. In-laws are generally not included under these particular definitions.

It also includes controlled entities: corporations, partnerships, trusts, and estates in which the taxpayer has a controlling interest, generally more than 50% directly or indirectly. Attribution rules can treat ownership held by one related party as owned by another, expanding the definition further.

The rules apply even when the connection seems distant or the parties believe their deal is arm’s length. A sibling, child, or majority-owned LLC is related-party territory. Confirm the status with a CPA or tax counsel before designing an exchange around someone with whom you have a family or ownership connection.

## The two-year holding rule

Section 1031(f) generally requires both parties in a related-party exchange to hold the property received for at least two years. If either party disposes of its received property during that period, the exchange can be retroactively disqualified. The deferred gain is unwound and typically becomes taxable in the year of the early disposition.

The clock starts on the date of the last transfer in the exchange. During the two years, neither party can freely sell or make another disqualifying disposition. The condition is mutual: your deferral depends on the related party’s actions as well as your own.

The exceptions are narrow. They can include a disposition due to death of either party, an involuntary conversion, and a transaction shown to the IRS’s satisfaction not to have tax avoidance as a principal purpose. The ordinary rule remains simple: both parties need a genuine ability and intent to hold for two years.

> In a related-party exchange, both parties generally must hold for two years. One early disposition can unwind the other person’s deferred gain as well.

## Why the rules exist

The rules target basis shifting. Without them, related parties could move a high basis to the property the family plans to sell and a low basis to the property it wants to retain. The family could then sell the high-basis property with little gain, turning what would have been a taxable cash-out into a tax-deferred or tax-reduced one. The two-year rule blocks the quick cash-out.

Related parties do not necessarily deal at arm’s length. A quick sale after an exchange can look like a disguised sale by the original owner, routed through the related party to defer or reduce tax. Congress enacted Section 1031(f) to address that concern.

This anti-abuse purpose explains the rule’s severity and its narrow exceptions. Death, involuntary conversion, and genuinely non-tax-avoidance transactions are not necessarily abusive. But a quick disposition can signal exactly the basis-shifting or disguised sale the rule was designed to prevent.

## Common related-party pitfalls

The first trap is failing to recognize the relationship. Broad family definitions, majority-owned entities, and attribution rules can cause an investor to miss the two-year requirement and the Form 8824 Part II reporting.

The second is an early disposition by either party. It can happen because of a sale, a transfer, or certain refinancings that count as a disposition. Because the rule is mutual, one party’s action can defeat the other’s deferral. Both must understand what is restricted and coordinate for the full period.

The third is attempting an indirect structure, such as exchanging with an unrelated party that then transacts with the related party to reach the prohibited result. The IRS examines arrangements designed to avoid the rule. Confirm related status, commit to the hold, avoid circumvention structures, and use tax counsel before proceeding.

## Structuring to stay compliant

Start with relationship analysis. A CPA or tax counsel should apply the relevant definitions and attribution rules. If the counterparty is related, both sides need to be able to hold the property received for the full two years and avoid sale, certain refinancings, or transfers that could count as a disposition.

Reporting and evidence matter. Form 8824, including Part II, reports related-party information and the two-year holding period. Keep proof that both parties held the properties as required and that the exchange was not structured to shift basis as a tax-avoidance device.

Complex facts—partnership interests, multiple entities, or a desire to avoid the two-year lock-up—need tax counsel. An exchange with an unrelated party may be cleaner. A related-party exchange is permitted, but only when both parties can meet the conditions deliberately and with informed professional guidance.

## Key Takeaways
- Related party is broad: spouse, siblings, lineal relatives, and majority-owned entities, including attribution rules.
- Both parties generally must hold the received property for two years or retroactive disqualification can follow.
- The rules address basis shifting and disguised sales; death, involuntary conversion, and non-tax-avoidance circumstances are limited exceptions.
- Confirm the relationship, commit to the hold, report on Form 8824 Part II, and do not try to engineer around the rule.

## When a related-party exchange makes sense

The extra conditions do not mean a related-party exchange is never appropriate. It can make sense when the ideal replacement property is truly owned by a related party or when a family or affiliated entities have a legitimate objective such as consolidating holdings, aligning ownership with management, or [estate planning around inherited property](1031-exchange-on-inherited-property.html). If both parties intended to hold long term anyway, the two-year rule may be acceptable.

The decision depends on a genuine two-year commitment and a purpose beyond tax avoidance. When both parties want long-term ownership, the condition may not be an obstacle. When either needs flexibility to dispose of property sooner, the related-party structure is risky and an unrelated-party exchange or no exchange may be better.

First-order thinking asks whether the property is attractive. Second-order thinking asks whether both parties can actually absorb the shared two-year liquidity constraint. It is also worth asking whether the related-party structure is necessary. Often, the same goal can be met through an unrelated counterparty, without the two-year lock-up. Reserve the related-party approach for cases in which that counterparty or family/entity repositioning is genuinely needed and both sides can satisfy the rule.

## Buying replacement property from a related party

A direct swap is not the only concern. The IRS scrutinizes an arrangement in which you sell relinquished property to an unrelated buyer but buy your replacement from a related party. It can create a similar basis-shifting result: the related party effectively cashes out low-basis property through you while you defer gain.

The two-year holding concern and anti-abuse analysis can apply to that acquire-from-a-related-party pattern. Some transactions can be permissible, but they require careful analysis of whether tax avoidance is a principal purpose. Do not assume the purchase is harmless merely because the original sale went to an unrelated buyer.

Treat any related-party leg as a signal to analyze the entire structure—direct swap, sale to the related party, or purchase from one. Confirm the relationship, assess basis shifting and disguised-sale risk, honor holding requirements, and obtain tax counsel’s view. The rules are designed to reach indirect and structured arrangements as well as direct swaps.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I do a 1031 exchange with a family member?

Yes. It triggers related-party rules. Spouses, siblings, parents, and children are generally related, and both parties generally must hold what they receive for two years or the exchange can be retroactively disqualified.

### Who counts as a related party?

Generally a spouse, siblings, ancestors, and lineal descendants, but generally not in-laws; also controlled corporations, partnerships, trusts, and estates where ownership is generally more than 50%, directly or indirectly with attribution. Sections 267(b) and 707(b) govern the definitions.

### What is the two-year holding rule?

Under Section 1031(f), both parties generally hold received property for at least two years from the last transfer. A disposition within that period can retroactively disqualify the exchange and typically makes gain taxable in that disposition year.

### What happens if the related party sells early?

An early disposition by either party can retroactively disqualify the exchange and recognize deferred gain. Your deferral can fail because of the other party’s sale, not only your own.

### Why do the related-party rules exist?

They prevent high- and low-basis properties from being shifted within a family for a lower-tax cash-out, and they target disguised sales routed through a related party.

### Are there exceptions to the two-year rule?

Yes, but narrowly: death, involuntary conversion, and certain transactions proven to the IRS’s satisfaction not to have tax avoidance as a principal purpose. Confirm the exception with tax counsel.

### What's the most common related-party pitfall?

Missing that a counterparty is related and therefore missing the two-year hold and Part II reporting. The issue can emerge after an early disposition, when the disqualification risk has already become real.

### Can I structure around the related-party rules?

Trying to use an unrelated intermediary to reach the same result is risky. The IRS can apply the rules to indirect avoidance structures. Comply genuinely or use an unrelated-party exchange.

### How do I report a related-party exchange?

Use Form 8824, including Part II for related-party information and the two-year holding period, as well as the usual Parts I and III. Keep evidence of both parties’ holds and the legitimate transaction purpose; the CPA prepares the reporting.

### When does a related-party exchange make sense?

When the best replacement is truly owned by a related party or legitimate consolidation, estate planning, or ownership alignment is the aim, and both parties can genuinely hold for two years. If either needs an earlier exit, it is risky.

### Is it better to exchange with an unrelated party?

Often. An unrelated exchange avoids the special related-party rules and two-year lock-up, so it can be cleaner. Related-party exchanges are for situations where the counterparty is genuinely right and the conditions can be met.

### Do the related-party rules apply to entities I own?

Yes. Corporations, partnerships, trusts, and estates you control—generally more than 50% directly or indirectly, with attribution—are related. A majority-owned LLC can trigger the rule.

### Is buying my replacement from a related party a problem?

It can be. The IRS closely examines the pattern because it can enable a related party to cash out low-basis property through the exchanger. Analyze whether tax avoidance is a principal purpose; do not treat a purchase from a relative or controlled entity as automatically safe.

### Do the rules catch indirect or structured transactions?

Yes. The rules are designed to reach indirect arrangements intended to create the prohibited result, including an unrelated intermediary that later transacts with the related party.

### Does death of a party affect the two-year rule?

Yes. A disposition due to the death of either party is a limited exception. Involuntary conversion is another. Both are narrow, so seek tax-counsel confirmation.

### Should I get tax counsel for a related-party exchange?

Strongly advisable. The broad definitions, attribution rules, strict hold, indirect-structure analysis, and disqualification stakes justify a counsel-led review.

## Glossary

- **Related Party:** A person or entity related under Sections 267(b) and 707(b), triggering special 1031 rules.
- **Section 1031(f):** The Code subsection imposing special rules, including the two-year hold, on related-party exchanges.
- **Two-Year Holding Rule:** The requirement that both parties hold property received in a related-party exchange for two years.
- **Section 267(b):** The related-party definition covering family members and controlled entities used for 1031.
- **Section 707(b):** The related-party definition covering partnerships and their controlling partners.
- **Attribution Rules:** Rules treating one party’s ownership as another related party’s ownership, broadening the definitions.
- **Lineal Descendant:** A direct descendant, such as a child or grandchild, included in the family definition.
- **Controlling Interest:** Generally more than 50% ownership of an entity, making it related.
- **Basis-Shifting:** Moving basis between properties to lower tax on a cash-out; the rules target this.
- **Disguised Sale:** A sale routed through a related party to defer or reduce tax.
- **Disposition:** A sale or other transfer; one within two years can disqualify the exchange.
- **Involuntary Conversion:** Loss through condemnation or casualty, an exception to the two-year rule.
- **Form 8824 Part II:** The reporting section for related-party exchange information.
- **Tax Avoidance Purpose:** The guarded principal purpose; lack of it can support an exception.
- **Arm's Length:** Deal terms reached as unrelated parties would; related parties may not, prompting special rules.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent exchange facilitator, distinct from the related counterparty.
