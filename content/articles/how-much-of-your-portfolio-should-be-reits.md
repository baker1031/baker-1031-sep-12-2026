# How Much of Your Portfolio Should Be REITs?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing allocation charts turn a judgment call into a neat percentage. REITs are often the next line down: **5–15%**. That can be a useful place to start thinking. It is not a number to copy.

How much of a portfolio should be in REITs? There is no single right answer. The question reaches into goals, income needs, time horizon, risk tolerance, existing real-estate exposure, and the role the investor wants real estate to play. This guide covers the case for a REIT allocation, commonly cited ranges, income-versus-growth tilts, correlation benefits, and how to set a target that fits the whole picture.

The first-order view is that a REIT sleeve adds real estate and income. The second-order view is that its price, risk, liquidity, account placement, and relationship to property already owned matter just as much as the percentage itself. REITs can offer diversification, income driven by the **90%** distribution rule, and a real-asset character some investors treat as an inflation consideration. None of that guarantees an outcome. The commonly cited allocation ranges are general education, not recommendations or promises, and the right number can be higher, lower, or zero.

Before sizing the position, it helps to understand the investment being sized. Read the [comprehensive REIT primer](reits-guide.html) alongside this allocation discussion.

## The Case for a REIT Allocation

The case begins with diversification. REITs provide exposure to real estate as a separate asset class alongside stocks and bonds. Real estate has historically behaved somewhat differently from those assets, so a REIT sleeve can broaden a portfolio beyond traditional holdings.

Income is another part of the case. Because the **90%** distribution rule requires REITs to distribute most of their taxable income, their yields often run higher than the broad stock market. That can make them useful in an income plan, but a distribution is not guaranteed.

Real estate is also often discussed as an inflation consideration. Properties generate rents and hold physical assets, and property values and lease rates can rise over time. Some investors view that as a potential partial hedge against inflation. It remains a general consideration, not a guarantee.

Finally, REITs are accessible. Traded REIT shares can give an investor a slice of large, professionally managed, diversified real-estate portfolios without the capital, leverage, or management burden of owning a property directly. Those are sensible reasons to consider a real-estate sleeve. They are not reasons to assume the sleeve is right for every investor.

## Common Allocation Ranges

When investors and commentators discuss REIT allocations, the figures most often cited are roughly **5–15%** of a diversified portfolio. The logic is straightforward: real estate can be a meaningful sleeve without becoming the whole portfolio or concentrating risk in one asset class.

But **5–15%** is a conversation starter, not a prescription. It is a general educational reference point, not a recommendation. An investor with a home, rental properties, business real estate, or real estate held in funds may already have considerable exposure and may reasonably own fewer REITs or none. An income-focused retiree may lean toward a higher allocation for yield. A younger growth investor may hold less. Some investors hold no REITs at all, and that can be entirely appropriate.

The useful question is not whether a portfolio has reached a published percentage. It is whether total real-estate exposure, goals, and risk tolerance support the amount under consideration.

> The often-cited **5–15%** figure is a conversation starter, not a prescription. The appropriate REIT allocation may sit within that range, above it, or at zero.

## Income vs. Growth Tilt

The size and composition of a REIT allocation change with the investor's objective. An income-focused investor, often someone in or near retirement who wants [cash flow](reits-for-retirement-income.html), may use a larger REIT sleeve and emphasize income-oriented sectors such as net-lease, healthcare, and certain residential properties. Their steadier distributions can sit beside bonds and dividend stocks in an income strategy.

A growth-focused investor with a longer horizon may emphasize total return—income plus appreciation—and sectors with stronger growth characteristics, including industrial and data centers. That investor may keep the real-estate sleeve more modest so equities can carry more of the long-term growth role.

The structure matters too. An income investor who does not need liquidity may consider a non-traded REIT for a long-term income holding after a suitability review. Growth- and liquidity-minded investors may favor publicly traded REITs and REIT funds. Income versus growth shapes both the amount held and the type of exposure held.

## Correlation Benefits

Correlation measures how closely two assets move together. Assets that do not move in perfect lockstep can, in combination, reduce overall volatility for a given level of return. Real estate has historically shown only moderate correlation with stocks and bonds over longer periods. A REIT sleeve can therefore improve a [portfolio's diversification and risk-adjusted profile](reits-in-a-diversified-portfolio.html).

There is an important limit. Publicly traded REITs are still equities. In the short term, especially during sharp market sell-offs, they can move closely with the stock market. The diversification benefit can shrink just when investors most want it. Over longer horizons, rents, leases, and property cycles can reassert real estate's distinct fundamentals.

That makes the correlation benefit real but partial and time-dependent. Expect diversification over the long run, not immunity from a market downturn.

## Key Takeaways

- The case for REITs rests on diversification, income from the **90%** distribution rule, a general inflation consideration, and accessible real-estate exposure.
- Commonly cited allocations cluster around **5–15%**, but that range is education, not a recommendation. Your figure may be higher, lower, or zero.
- An income tilt can support a larger, income-sector sleeve; a growth tilt can support a more modest, total-return-oriented sleeve.
- Real estate's moderate long-run correlation with stocks and bonds can help diversification, while traded REITs can still move with equities during sharp sell-offs.

## Setting Your Target

Start by inventorying existing real estate. A home, rental properties, business real estate, and real estate held in funds all count in the broad picture. Heavy existing exposure often argues for fewer REITs.

Then clarify the goal: income or growth, the time horizon, and liquidity needs. Those decisions guide both the amount of REIT exposure and the type that may fit. Consider risk tolerance and how the sleeve interacts with the rest of the portfolio. The partial correlation benefit does not erase the fact that [REITs carry real risk](are-reits-a-good-investment-pros-and-cons.html): distributions can be cut, prices and NAVs fluctuate, and sectors can underperform.

Account placement belongs in the discussion. REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, so a tax-advantaged account can sometimes be more tax-efficient. Set a target range rather than an exact point, revisit it when circumstances change, and decide with a financial professional who can see the full picture. This is not a number to borrow from an article.

> The most important inputs to a REIT target are not a rule of thumb. They are the real estate already owned, the investor's goals, and tolerance for risk, weighed with an advisor who sees the whole picture.

## Common Allocation Mistakes

One mistake is double-counting or ignoring existing real estate. An investor with substantial property—a home, rentals, or business real estate—who adds a large REIT sleeve can become much more concentrated in real estate than intended.

Another is chasing yield. A high headline distribution rate says little by itself about risk, sector, or sustainability. High yields can signal higher risk, and distributions are not guaranteed.

Other common errors are expecting REITs to protect the portfolio in every downturn, neglecting tax-aware account placement, treating **5–15%** as a prescription, and setting an allocation once without revisiting it as life and markets change. The goal is a purposeful sleeve, not a default one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much of my portfolio should be in REITs?

There is no universal answer. Goals, income needs, time horizon, risk tolerance, and existing real estate all matter. Commonly cited ranges fall around **5–15%** of a diversified portfolio, but these are general educational reference points, not recommendations. The right figure can be higher, lower, or zero. A substantial home, rental, or business-property position may support fewer REITs or none; an income-focused investor may tilt higher and a growth investor may hold less. Use the range to frame a discussion with a financial professional. This is general education, not advice.

### What is a commonly cited REIT allocation range?

The figures most often cited cluster around roughly **5–15%** of a diversified portfolio. The range reflects a view that real estate can be meaningful without dominating the portfolio or concentrating risk in one asset class. It is not a recommendation. Existing holdings, income-versus-growth goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance can move the appropriate figure well above, below, or to zero. Some investors hold no REITs at all. Treat the range as a starting point for a conversation with a financial professional rather than a prescription.

### Why include REITs in a portfolio at all?

REITs can provide exposure to real estate as a separate asset class, which has historically behaved somewhat differently from stocks and bonds. The **90%** distribution rule can support a meaningful income stream. Real estate's rents and physical assets are often considered a potential partial inflation consideration, though not a guarantee. REITs can also provide liquid access to large, professionally managed, diversified property portfolios without the capital and management burden of direct ownership. None of these benefits is promised, and REITs carry real risk, so any allocation should fit the investor's goals.

### Do REITs help diversify a portfolio?

They can, though the benefit is real but partial. Assets that do not move in perfect lockstep can reduce a portfolio's overall volatility for a given level of return. Real estate has historically shown only moderate correlation with stocks and bonds over longer periods, so a REIT sleeve can improve diversification and risk-adjusted profile. Publicly traded REITs are still equities, however, and in sharp short-term sell-offs can move closely with the stock market. Expect a long-run diversification benefit, not protection in every downturn.

### Should retirees hold more REITs for income?

Some income-focused investors, including retirees, use a somewhat larger REIT sleeve because high payouts under the **90%** distribution rule can provide cash flow alongside bonds and dividend stocks. Net-lease, healthcare, and certain residential sectors often emphasize steadier distributions. More is not automatically better: REIT income is not guaranteed, distributions can be cut, REITs carry market and interest-rate risk, and yield concentration can increase risk. Mostly ordinary-income dividends can also be more tax-efficient in a tax-advantaged account. The size and composition should fit a diversified plan, risk tolerance, and liquidity needs with professional guidance, not be maximized for yield.

### Can REITs hedge against inflation?

REITs are often discussed as a potential partial inflation consideration, not as a guarantee. Real estate produces rents and holds physical assets; property values and lease rates can rise with inflation, and some leases have inflation-linked escalators. That can be useful when purchasing power is a concern. The relationship is not automatic: rising interest rates can pressure REIT prices, and effects vary by sector and lease structure. Treat the inflation angle as one general reason to consider real-estate exposure, not as a reliable or complete hedge.

### Does owning a home count toward my real estate allocation?

Many investors and advisors consider a home, rental properties, and business real estate when deciding whether to add REITs. Total exposure to real estate includes more than REIT holdings. A large REIT sleeve on top of substantial direct property can create more concentration than intended. A primary residence is a personal-use asset and differs from an income-producing REIT, so some investors weight it differently. Inventory total exposure so the REIT sleeve complements rather than duplicates what is already owned, then decide any additional exposure with a financial professional.

### What's the difference between an income and a growth REIT allocation?

The differences are size, sector, and structure. An income-tilted allocation tends to be larger and may emphasize net-lease, healthcare, and certain residential sectors that prioritize steady distributions. A growth-tilted allocation tends to be more modest, focuses on total return, and may emphasize industrial and data centers while allowing equities to drive more long-term growth. Income investors who do not need liquidity may consider a non-traded REIT as a long-term income holding after suitability review; growth- and liquidity-minded investors may favor publicly traded REITs and funds. Goals, horizon, and liquidity needs determine the tilt.

### Are REITs riskier than bonds?

Generally, yes, even though both can be used for income. REIT share prices fluctuate and can fall during sell-offs. Distributions can be cut when property income declines, and REITs—especially mortgage REITs—are sensitive to interest rates. High-quality bonds generally provide contractual interest and return of principal at maturity, subject to credit and rate risk, and are generally less volatile. REITs are more equity-like than bonds and usually complement rather than replace the bond portion of a portfolio. Size a REIT allocation accordingly and revisit it as conditions change.

### How does account placement affect my REIT allocation?

Account placement, sometimes called asset location, can affect after-tax value because REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income and can be relatively tax-inefficient in a taxable account. A traditional or Roth IRA can shelter those dividends from current tax while more tax-efficient assets remain taxable. This is general education, not a recommendation for any investor. There is a trade-off: the federal **20%** Section **199A** deduction on qualified REIT dividends generally applies only in taxable accounts. Where REITs are held can matter as much as how much is held; discuss the choice with a CPA because Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### Is it okay to hold no REITs at all?

Yes. No rule requires a portfolio to contain REITs, and the commonly cited **5–15%** range is a reference point, not a requirement. An investor with substantial direct real estate may already have ample exposure. Others may prefer other asset classes or decide that REITs do not fit their goals or risk tolerance. A zero allocation can be just as intentional as a **5%**, **10%**, or **15%** allocation. The decision should follow the full financial picture, ideally with a financial professional.

### How often should I revisit my REIT allocation?

Review the allocation periodically rather than set it once and forget it. A common practice is at least annually and after a home or property purchase or sale, a change in income or goals, retirement, or a significant market move. Those events can change total real-estate exposure, income needs, or risk tolerance. Market moves can also create allocation drift, which periodic rebalancing addresses. The point is not to react to every fluctuation. Set a target range, review on a regular cadence and after major changes, and adjust thoughtfully with professional help.

### Should I use REIT funds or individual REITs for my allocation?

For many investors, a broad REIT index fund or ETF is the simplest implementation because it offers low-cost diversification across many REITs and sectors. Individual REITs can offer targeted exposure and potential outperformance, but they require research, concentrate risk in fewer names, and demand more time and skill. The implementation question is separate from the allocation-size question. Set the target first, then choose a fund, individual REITs, or a mix that fits the investor's time, expertise, and willingness to monitor holdings.

### Are allocation ranges like 5-15% guarantees of any result?

No. A cited range such as **5–15%** is general education, not a recommendation, and it guarantees nothing about returns, income, or risk. Market conditions, the REITs selected, fees, timing, and the overall plan all matter. Prices and NAVs fluctuate, distributions can be cut, and sectors can underperform. Use a range to frame thought and discussion, never as a performance promise or a substitute for personal advice. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
- **Asset Allocation:** How a portfolio is divided among asset classes like stocks, bonds, and real estate.
- **REIT Allocation:** The share of a portfolio held in REITs.
- **Diversification:** Spreading investments to reduce overall risk.
- **Correlation:** A measure of how closely two assets move together.
- **Asset Class:** A group of similar investments, such as real estate.
- **Income Tilt:** Emphasizing cash flow and income-oriented holdings.
- **Growth Tilt:** Emphasizing total return and appreciation.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to pay out at least **90%** of taxable income.
- **Yield:** Annual distribution income as a percentage of price.
- **Inflation Consideration:** Real estate's potential, partial relationship to inflation.
- **Risk Tolerance:** How much volatility and loss an investor can accept.
- **Time Horizon:** How long until an investor needs the money.
- **Rebalancing:** Periodically restoring a portfolio to its target allocation.
- **Asset Location:** Placing assets in the most tax-efficient account type.
- **Risk-Adjusted Return:** Return measured relative to the risk taken to earn it.
