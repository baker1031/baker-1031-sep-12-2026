# Same-Store NOI Explained

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often a REIT’s growing income line gets treated as a verdict. It is a starting point. If income rises, the useful question is whether the buildings the REIT already owned actually performed better—or whether the company simply bought more buildings.

Same-store NOI, also called same-property NOI, is built to answer that question. It measures the change in net operating income from properties owned in both comparison periods, excluding acquisitions, dispositions, and recent development. That isolates the existing portfolio: rents, occupancy, and expense control.

First-order thinking sees higher total NOI and assumes the underlying business is stronger. Second-order thinking separates organic improvement from growth bought through deals, and asks whether that growth created value per share or only made the asset base larger. Same-store NOI is one of the more revealing tools for that job, but it is not a standalone conclusion. This is educational information, not investment advice; verify current data and your own situation with your advisors. Our [2026 REIT investing guide](reits-guide.html) places this measure in the wider context of how REITs are structured and evaluated.

## What Same-Store NOI Measures

Start with NOI. Net operating income is property revenue less operating expenses, calculated before debt service and depreciation. It is a core measure of property-level operating cash generation.

Same-store NOI narrows that measure to assets present in both the current and prior periods—the same-store or same-property pool. That makes the comparison like-for-like. Income from a recently bought building is excluded so it does not inflate the result; income from a sold building is excluded so it does not depress the result. What remains is the change in a stable, continuously owned portfolio.

The change is normally reported as a growth percentage, often year-over-year. It reflects the effect of rent changes, occupancy shifts, and expense control on the properties the REIT already owns. In practical terms, it is a read on organic operating performance and pricing power rather than the noise of buying and selling buildings.

## Organic vs. Acquisition Growth

Same-store NOI separates two forms of growth that can look similar in a headline.

**Organic growth** comes from improving the existing portfolio: higher rents, better occupancy, or more disciplined expenses. **Acquisition growth**, also called external growth, comes from buying or developing additional properties. Both can lift a REIT’s total NOI. They do not say the same thing about the business.

Steady same-store NOI growth is generally the higher-quality signal because it indicates that existing assets have pricing power and sound operations. A REIT can add NOI through acquisitions even if its owned portfolio is flat or deteriorating. It may also overpay for acquisitions or fund them by issuing shares or taking on debt, so deal-driven NOI growth does not automatically create value per share.

A REIT can always grow total NOI by buying more buildings. Same-store NOI asks the harder question: is the business it already owns getting better?

## How to Find It in Filings

Same-store NOI is usually not a line on the face of the income statement. Look instead in the quarterly supplemental operating and financial data package and the earnings materials that accompany financial filings. Public REITs commonly provide a supplemental alongside quarterly results. It often includes the same-store pool, the underlying revenue and expense changes, and the resulting NOI-growth percentage.

Earnings releases and management discussion in periodic SEC filings often refer to the measure as well. The underlying **10-Q**, **10-K**, and **8-K** exhibits, including supplementals, can be found through the SEC’s [EDGAR company-filings search](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/searchedgar/companysearch). The company’s investor-relations site often posts the supplemental directly.

Do not stop at the percentage. Read the company’s disclosure around it. REITs can differ on the number of properties in the pool, how long a property must be owned to qualify, how recently developed or redeveloped properties are treated, and whether the result is shown on a cash or GAAP basis. The detail is what tells you what is actually being compared.

## Interpreting the Trend

The trend matters more than one quarter. Consistent positive same-store NOI growth across several periods suggests a healthy organic engine: a REIT is raising rents, holding or improving occupancy, and managing expenses. That combination can point to pricing power and sound operations. Several good periods are more informative than one favorable quarter because they offer better evidence of durable rather than one-off performance.

Negative or decelerating growth deserves attention. It can mean rents are under pressure, occupancy is falling, or expenses are rising faster than revenue. Total NOI can still rise because acquisitions are adding income, while the existing portfolio weakens beneath the headline. A single negative period need not settle the question; a one-time item or a temporary factor can move it. A sustained negative or slowing trend is a reason to investigate.

Context matters. Compare the REIT’s result with its own history, sector peers, and the broader environment. Different property sectors have different structural growth rates, and macro conditions affect every landlord. The right reading is a multi-period, contextual one—not a reaction to a single number.

## Key Takeaways

- Same-store NOI measures the change in NOI from properties owned in both comparison periods, isolating organic performance.
- It separates organic improvement in rents, occupancy, and expenses from acquisition growth, and the organic result is often the higher-quality signal.
- Find it in quarterly supplementals and earnings materials, with underlying filings on SEC EDGAR; read the same-store-pool definition.
- Steady positive growth can signal organic health and pricing power. Negative or decelerating growth can warn of weakening fundamentals.

## Limitations to Watch

Same-store NOI is useful because it narrows the comparison. That narrowing also creates limits.

First, the pool is not standardized. Each REIT decides which properties qualify, the ownership period required, and how recently developed or redeveloped assets are treated. Two companies’ same-store results are therefore not perfectly comparable. A company can, intentionally or not, choose a pool that presents the outcome more favorably. Read the definition before comparing a peer’s number.

Second, one-time items can distort a single period. Lease-termination fees can temporarily boost revenue. Non-recurring repairs or expenses, bad-debt adjustments, and items tied to particular events can move the percentage without changing the ongoing direction of the business. Look at multiple periods and the supplemental disclosures rather than taking one quarter at face value.

Redevelopment exclusions need judgment too. A property taken partly or fully offline for repositioning, renovation, or re-tenanting may have temporarily lower revenue and higher expenses. Excluding it can give a cleaner picture of the stabilized pool. But the exclusion can also leave out an asset that is being repositioned for future income—or an underperforming asset. Ask how much of the portfolio is excluded and why.

Finally, same-store NOI deliberately ignores acquisition and development growth. It does not tell you whether external growth is creating value. It is powerful, but partial.

## Using Same-Store NOI in Analysis

Use same-store NOI with measures that capture the acquisition side: total NOI growth, [FFO and AFFO per share](affo-vs-ffo-which-metric-matters-more.html), and the development pipeline. Together, they show the organic engine, the external-growth engine, and whether acquisitions are accretive or simply add bulk.

Then look for what may lead the reported result. [Occupancy and leasing spreads](occupancy-and-leasing-spreads-in-reit-analysis.html) can foreshadow where same-store NOI is heading. The balance sheet matters too: leverage, debt maturities, and liquidity shape the capacity to fund and sustain growth. Compare the measure with the REIT’s own history and its sector peers, and read several quarters across a cycle. That process separates a durable operating trend from a temporary tailwind.

Same-store NOI should be one input among growth, profitability, leading-indicator, and balance-sheet measures. It is a window into organic quality, not a verdict on a REIT.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is same-store NOI?

Same-store NOI, or same-property NOI, is the change in NOI from properties held in both comparison periods. NOI is property revenue minus operating expenses before debt service and depreciation. By using only continuously owned assets, the measure excludes the effect of acquisitions and dispositions and shows the organic change from rents, occupancy, and expense control. It is usually a growth percentage, such as year-over-year.

### Why is same-store NOI important?

It distinguishes the health of existing assets from growth achieved by buying new ones. Strong total NOI can come from acquisitions even while existing buildings are weakening. Same-store growth therefore provides a more direct read on operating quality and pricing power, though it is not the only metric that matters.

### How is same-store NOI different from total NOI?

Total NOI includes every property owned in the current period, including recently acquired or developed assets and the effect of dispositions. Same-store NOI uses only the same continuously owned pool. Total NOI describes the current portfolio’s overall income; same-store NOI isolates the existing portfolio’s organic result.

### Where can I find a REIT’s same-store NOI?

Look in the quarterly supplemental package, earnings release, and investor-relations materials. The related 10-Q, 10-K, and 8-K filings are available on EDGAR. Read the pool definition, revenue and expense bridge, and whether the number uses cash or GAAP accounting.

### What is the difference between organic and acquisition growth?

Organic growth comes from improving rents, occupancy, and expenses on existing properties. Acquisition growth comes from buying or developing new properties. Both can raise total NOI, but only same-store NOI isolates the organic engine.

### What does positive same-store NOI growth mean?

It commonly reflects some combination of rising rents, stable or improving occupancy, and expense control. That can signal pricing power and sound operations. A steady positive trend across several periods is more useful than one strong quarter, and it should be compared with peers and other measures.

### What does negative same-store NOI growth signal?

It can signal pressure on rents, weaker occupancy, or expenses outpacing revenue in the existing portfolio. It can occur while total NOI rises from acquisitions. One negative quarter may be a temporary item; sustained negative or decelerating growth, especially with weak occupancy and leasing indicators, requires closer examination.

### Is same-store NOI standardized across REITs?

No. Each REIT sets its same-store-pool definition, ownership-period requirement, redevelopment treatment, and cash or GAAP presentation. It is especially useful for following one REIT through time, but comparisons across companies require attention to the definitions.

### How do one-time items affect same-store NOI?

Lease-termination fees, unusual repairs or expenses, bad-debt adjustments, and event-specific items can make a quarter look stronger or weaker than the ongoing business. Review the supplemental disclosures and several periods to avoid mistaking a temporary move for a lasting trend.

### Why are redevelopment properties sometimes excluded?

Redevelopment can disrupt income and raise expenses while an asset is repositioned, renovated, or re-tenanted. Excluding it can provide a cleaner stabilized comparison. But it can also leave out future upside or an underperforming building, so assess how the REIT treats redevelopment and the scale of exclusions.

### Can same-store NOI be manipulated?

The measure can be shaped through choices about the pool, required ownership period, redevelopment and recently acquired assets, cash or GAAP basis, and one-time items. Those choices are not necessarily improper, but they mean a careful analyst reads the methodology and disclosures rather than accepting a headline percentage alone.

### What other metrics should I use with same-store NOI?

Use total NOI, FFO and AFFO per share, the development pipeline, occupancy, leasing or renewal spreads, leverage, debt maturities, and liquidity. Add the REIT’s own history and sector peers. Same-store NOI is an input into a wider assessment of growth, profitability, balance-sheet capacity, and risk.

### Does same-store NOI predict future performance?

It describes recent organic performance and can offer context about direction, but it does not predict results with certainty. Interest rates, the economy, sector conditions, and company-specific events can change the path. Past performance does not guarantee future results; use the trend with leading indicators, the balance sheet, and the broader setting.

## Glossary

- **Same-Store NOI:** Change in NOI from properties owned in both comparison periods.
- **Same-Property NOI:** Another name for same-store NOI, the like-for-like organic measure.
- **Net Operating Income (NOI):** Property revenue minus operating expenses before debt service and depreciation.
- **Same-Store Pool:** The continuously owned properties included in the comparison.
- **Organic Growth:** Existing-property income growth from rents, occupancy, and expenses.
- **Acquisition Growth:** Income growth from purchasing or developing new properties.
- **Occupancy Rate:** The percentage of a property’s space that is leased.
- **Expense Control:** Managing operating costs to protect or grow NOI.
- **Supplemental Package:** A REIT’s detailed quarterly operating and financial data report.
- **SEC EDGAR:** The SEC database of public-company filings.
- **Cash vs. GAAP Basis:** Reporting conventions that affect how NOI is measured.
- **Redevelopment Exclusion:** Removing assets under repositioning from the same-store pool.
- **One-Time Items:** Non-recurring revenue or expense items that can distort a period.
- **Pricing Power:** The ability to raise rents without losing occupancy.
- **FFO / AFFO:** REIT earnings measures used alongside same-store NOI.
- **Leading Indicator:** A measure such as occupancy or leasing spreads that can foreshadow NOI.
