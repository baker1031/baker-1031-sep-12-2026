# Reverse 1031 Exchange: How to Buy Before You Sell

I keep seeing the same timing problem: a replacement property becomes available while the property that will fund it is still for sale. The first-order response is to focus on getting the new property under contract. The second-order question is tougher: can the old property actually sell, and can the financing, title, documentation, and calendar all hold together before the structure runs out of room?

A reverse exchange exists for that particular problem. It lets an investor acquire replacement property first and sell the relinquished property afterward while pursuing the tax deferral available under Section 1031. It can preserve an opportunity that a normal sequence would lose. It is also the most involved and expensive variation of a 1031 exchange, so the benefit has to be worth the machinery.

## What a reverse 1031 exchange is

In a standard [delayed exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), the taxpayer sells first and then acquires replacement property. A reverse 1031 exchange reverses that order: acquire the replacement first, then sell the relinquished property. The like-kind requirement, the aim of deferring capital gain and depreciation recapture, and the need to follow the exchange rules all remain. The sequence is what changes.

That single change creates the ownership problem. A taxpayer cannot simply buy the new property while still owning the old one and call the two purchases an exchange. One property has to be held apart while the sale catches up. That temporary holding arrangement is called parking.

## Why it exists — you can't own both

The parking arrangement makes the reversed sequence possible. An intermediary entity takes title to one property—usually the replacement property—so the taxpayer does not hold title to both properties at the same time. After the relinquished property is sold, the parked property transfers to the taxpayer and the exchange can be completed.

This is not a cosmetic step. The extra entity is what preserves the exchange treatment when the order is reversed. It is also why reverse exchanges bring extra fees, documents, carrying costs, and execution risk. The structure should follow the deal, rather than asking a deal to justify a structure that was never needed.

## How the parking structure works

IRS Revenue Procedure 2000-37 provides a safe harbor for reverse-exchange parking arrangements. Under it, an **Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT)**—often a single-purpose, single-member LLC formed by the qualified intermediary—takes legal title to a property during the exchange. The taxpayer and EAT sign a written **Qualified Exchange Accommodation Agreement (QEAA)** that records the arrangement.

The EAT may hold the parked property for up to 180 days under the safe harbor. The documentation has to show that the parties intend a like-kind exchange, and the parked property must be transferred to the taxpayer within that window. A reverse exchange outside the safe harbor may be possible, but it is riskier; most transactions are built within Revenue Procedure 2000-37 for a clearer framework.

The EAT holds title, but the taxpayer generally retains the economic burden and benefit. The taxpayer may lease and control the property and normally bears debt service, property taxes, and insurance during the parking period. That division between economic involvement and title is precisely why experienced handling matters.

## The Exchange Accommodation Titleholder

The EAT is a temporary accommodating titleholder, not the investor's permanent owner. In the more common **exchange-last** structure, it holds the replacement property while the investor markets and sells the relinquished property. In an **exchange-first** structure, the investor takes the replacement property directly and the EAT instead parks the relinquished property until sale.

Exchange-last is more common because the investor keeps operating and selling the familiar old property. Exchange-first can fit when financing the new property is easier in the investor's name. Either way, the parked property must transfer out of the EAT before the deadline, and the qualified intermediary must be able to form and operate the entity and documents correctly.

## The 45- and 180-day deadlines in reverse

The familiar exchange clocks still apply, but they run from the parking date. Within 45 days, the taxpayer identifies in writing the relinquished property to be sold. Within 180 days, the parked property must be transferred out of the EAT and the exchange must be completed.

These are hard calendar-day deadlines. There is no weekend grace period, and relief is generally limited to disaster-based situations. Our [1031 exchange timeline](1031-exchange-timeline.html) explains the discipline in a forward exchange; in a reverse exchange the pressure is more acute because the purchase has already happened while the sale is still uncertain.

## Financing a reverse exchange

Financing is often the practical constraint. Because the EAT holds title, a lender has to be willing to lend into or alongside the accommodation arrangement, often with the investor providing a guarantee or funds. Many conventional lenders will not do that without careful structuring.

For that reason, many reverse exchanges use cash, bridge financing, or portfolio and private lenders familiar with the EAT structure. An investor relying on conventional financing should get the lender's agreement before committing to the replacement purchase. A commitment that assumes the lender will accept the parking arrangement after the fact is a weak foundation for a clock that cannot be extended.

## Costs and complexity

A reverse exchange costs materially more than a standard delayed exchange. The taxpayer is paying for EAT formation and operation, additional legal documents, carrying costs on the parked property, and a qualified-intermediary fee that is often a multiple of a delayed-exchange fee. Our [1031 exchange cost memo](1031-exchange-cost.html) covers the broader cost picture.

First-order thinking treats these costs as a nuisance. Second-order thinking asks whether the cost is the price of securing a replacement that truly cannot be replicated. When a readily available forward exchange or a quick alternative will do, the cheaper route often has the better risk-adjusted result.

## When a reverse exchange makes sense

A reverse structure can earn its cost when a specific replacement property is at risk of being lost, the market is moving fast, a seller will not wait for the old property to close, or an off-market opportunity requires speed. It is a poor fit when the old property is not genuinely ready to sell, when the financing cannot be arranged, or when the transaction can be accomplished through a conventional delayed exchange.

A fast-closing [DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) can sometimes supply a lower-cost backup. It can close in days in a forward exchange, which may eliminate the need to acquire anything before the sale. If the concern is the [45-day identification rule](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html), a DST backup may also relieve that constraint without adding an EAT. It is not a substitute for a truly irreplaceable direct replacement; it is an alternative worth testing before accepting the reverse exchange's cost.

## A worked example

Consider an illustrative case. An investor owns a rental worth about $1,000,000 and finds a $1,200,000 replacement property. Its seller wants to close in 30 days, far faster than the investor can sell the rental.

The investor engages a qualified intermediary, which forms an EAT. In an exchange-last structure, the EAT takes title to the $1,200,000 replacement using the investor's cash and a lender comfortable with the arrangement. The replacement is parked. The investor then lists the $1,000,000 rental, identifies it in writing within 45 days of parking, and sells it within 180 days. The sale proceeds flow through the exchange, any bridge financing is repaid, and the EAT transfers the replacement property to the investor.

The example is hypothetical, not a projection or a recommendation. Its point is narrow: the investor secured a property that would otherwise have been lost, and the EAT, legal work, financing work, and carrying cost were the price of timing. The same facts would be unattractive if the old property had weak demand or could not realistically sell inside the 180-day window.

## Combining a reverse with a build-to-suit

A reverse exchange may be combined with an improvement, or build-to-suit, exchange. The EAT parks the replacement property and can hold it while exchange funds are used to construct or renovate it. That can allow the taxpayer to improve the property to absorb full replacement value rather than being limited to its value as acquired.

The calendar remains the constraint. Improvements intended to count toward the replacement value must be completed, and the improved property must transfer to the taxpayer, within 180 days. Work that continues past the deadline does not count toward the exchange, no matter how close to completion it is. Permitting, weather, and supply-chain exposure turn this from a filing date into a construction-management issue. Build only what can realistically finish and reserve contingency.

## Risks specific to reverse exchanges

The largest risk is the inverse of the benefit: the old property still has to sell inside 180 days. If it does not, the exchange can fail. The investor may be left with the parked property after paying for the structure, or face a costly unwind and tax complications.

The practical protection is not optimism about the listing price. It is pricing the relinquished property to move, confirming demand before the replacement commitment, and deciding whether the property is genuinely marketable. Financing risk compounds the problem when a lender delays or rejects the accommodation structure. Cost risk compounds it again because EAT fees and carrying costs are sunk whether the exchange succeeds or fails. Documentation errors under the safe harbor create still another failure point.

## Reverse vs. forward vs. improvement

A forward, or delayed, exchange sells first and buys second. It is the standard, simplest, and cheapest approach for most exchanges. A reverse exchange buys first and sells second, using the EAT to handle title and accepting higher cost. An improvement exchange uses exchange funds to build or renovate the replacement property within the 180-day period.

All three satisfy the same core exchange rules and use the same 180-day completion limit. They differ in sequence and machinery. A reverse-improvement exchange combines both advanced features when the investor must acquire a property first and improve it before taking title.

## A reverse exchange timeline

**Day 0:** The EAT acquires and parks the replacement property, using cash or bridge financing. Before this day, the investor should have arranged financing and tested the marketability of the relinquished property.

**Days 1–45:** The investor formally identifies the relinquished property in writing and lists it for sale in an exchange-last structure. A faster sale leaves more margin for the rest of the process.

**By day 180:** The relinquished property sells; proceeds flow through the exchange and may repay bridge financing; and the EAT transfers the parked replacement property to the investor. Selling the old property in time is the binding constraint. A forward exchange begins with the sale completed; the reverse exchange deliberately leaves it for last.

## Choosing professionals for a reverse exchange

The team is unusually important here. The qualified intermediary should have a demonstrated record forming and operating EATs and documenting QEAA arrangements under Revenue Procedure 2000-37. A general QI may be well suited to a forward exchange but lack the specific reverse-exchange experience needed here.

The investor also needs a lender comfortable with the EAT structure, a CPA to analyze tax treatment and reporting, and an experienced advisor to ask whether the reverse structure is truly necessary or whether a forward exchange with a fast-closing DST backup will meet the goal more simply. Referrals and transaction-specific experience matter. The cost of an inexperienced team can be an exchange that does not qualify.

## Is a reverse exchange right for you?

The right case has several facts at once: a specific desirable replacement is available now; waiting would likely lose it; the added cost and complexity are justified; the old property is marketable; and a credible plan exists to sell it within 180 days. Normal timing generally points back toward a forward exchange.

In the current market, I would protect capital by testing the old property's exit before paying for the new property's urgency. I would confirm lender cooperation, set a price meant to clear rather than test the market, and keep a fast-closing alternative in reserve. How are you weighing the cost of certainty against the risk that a sale takes longer than expected?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is a reverse exchange different from a normal 1031?

In a normal delayed exchange, you sell first and then buy. In a reverse exchange, you buy first and then sell, while an EAT parks one property in between.

### How long do I have to complete a reverse exchange?

The parked property generally must transfer within 180 days, and the relinquished property must be identified within 45 days. Both windows are measured from the parking date.

### Can I finance a reverse exchange?

Yes, but the lender must be willing to lend while the EAT holds title. Many reverse exchanges use cash or lenders experienced with the structure, so financing should be confirmed before commitment.

### Why does a reverse exchange cost more?

It requires forming and operating an EAT, preparing additional legal documents, and carrying the parked property. A delayed exchange does not have those elements.

### When does a reverse exchange make sense?

It can fit when a replacement cannot be lost, the market is moving fast, or the seller will not wait. If a standard delayed exchange works, it is usually cheaper.

### What is an EAT?

An Exchange Accommodation Titleholder is usually a single-purpose LLC formed by the qualified intermediary. It temporarily holds title to the parked property under a QEAA until the exchange is completed.

### What is Revenue Procedure 2000-37?

It is the IRS safe harbor for parking arrangements in reverse and improvement exchanges. It sets documentation expectations and a 180-day limit for holding the parked property.

### What are exchange-last and exchange-first?

In exchange-last, the EAT holds the replacement property while you sell the relinquished property; it is more common. In exchange-first, you take the replacement directly and the EAT holds the relinquished property. Financing and lender requirements can determine which fits.

### Can I combine a reverse exchange with an improvement exchange?

Yes. A reverse-improvement exchange acquires the replacement through an EAT and uses exchange funds to build or renovate it inside the 180-day window. It is the most complex form of the structure.

### Is a DST a better alternative to a reverse exchange?

Not usually if you must acquire a particular replacement first. But a fast-closing DST in a forward exchange can close in days and may accomplish the same timing goal with less cost and complexity.

### What is the biggest risk in a reverse exchange?

The relinquished property may not sell within 180 days. That can unwind the structure and create tax complications, which is why marketability and likely sale timing must be assessed before commitment.

### Can I use my own cash to fund the purchase?

Yes. Many investors fund the up-front purchase with cash and recoup it when the relinquished property sells and the proceeds move through the exchange. That avoids lender complications but ties up capital during parking.

### Who pays the costs while the property is parked?

You typically bear debt service, property taxes, and insurance, often while leasing and controlling the property. The EAT holds title only temporarily.

### How do I choose a qualified intermediary for a reverse exchange?

Look for a QI with experience forming and operating EATs and documenting reverse exchanges under Revenue Procedure 2000-37. Ask about volume and track record, and seek referrals from attorneys or advisors who have completed reverse exchanges. A general QI may not be equipped for this structure.

### Are reverse exchanges riskier than forward exchanges?

Somewhat. They add the risk of the old property failing to sell in time, financing risk on the up-front purchase, and EAT complexity. Experienced professionals and a marketable relinquished property can manage those risks, but cannot remove them.

## Glossary

- **Reverse Exchange:** A 1031 exchange in which the replacement property is acquired before the relinquished property is sold.
- **Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT):** An entity that temporarily holds title to the parked property during a reverse exchange.
- **Revenue Procedure 2000-37:** The IRS safe harbor that establishes rules for parking arrangements in reverse exchanges.
- **Parking:** Temporary title holding through an EAT until the exchange can be completed.
- **Qualified Exchange Accommodation Agreement (QEAA):** The written agreement between taxpayer and EAT governing a safe-harbor parking arrangement.
- **Parked Property:** The property the EAT holds until the exchange can be completed.
- **Exchange-Last:** A reverse structure where the EAT holds the replacement property while the relinquished property is sold.
- **Exchange-First:** A reverse structure where the taxpayer acquires the replacement directly while the EAT holds the relinquished property.
- **Bridge Financing:** Short-term financing used for the up-front purchase before sale proceeds are available.
- **Forward Exchange:** The standard sequence: sell the relinquished property first, then acquire the replacement property.
- **Reverse-Improvement Exchange:** A structure that combines reverse acquisition with construction or improvements on the replacement property.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that typically facilitates the exchange and forms or operates the EAT in a reverse transaction.
- **Carrying Costs:** Debt service, taxes, and insurance on the parked property, generally borne by the taxpayer during parking.
