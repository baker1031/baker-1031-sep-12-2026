# What Is the 180-Day Exchange Period?

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read**

I keep coming back to the final stretch of a 1031 exchange. The first 45 days get the attention because the identification deadline feels abrupt. But many otherwise sound exchanges fail later, when a lender, appraisal, seller, title issue, or tax filing date uses up a closing cushion that once looked generous.

The first-order view is simple: 180 days is six months, so there is time. The second-order reality is that the 45-day identification period runs inside the same 180 days, the tax-return date can be earlier, and real-estate logistics do not care about the calendar. The closing period is a risk-management problem, not a countdown to watch passively.

This guide covers the rule, the tax-return-date trap, how the days are counted, closing and financing, disaster relief, and what to do if a closing cannot occur in time.

## The 180-Day Rule in Detail

The replacement property must be acquired within 180 calendar days after the relinquished property is sold. A [qualified intermediary](insights.html) transfers the exchange funds directly to the replacement closing; the proceeds never pass through the exchanger. The [45-day identification](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) period is not separate—it runs concurrently from that same sale date.

For full deferral, the property closed on must have been identified within the first 45 days, must be of equal or greater value, and must use all exchange equity while replacing any debt. Closing on an unidentified property, or closing after day 180, generally fails the exchange.

There are 135 days left after identification. That sounds comfortable until financing, inspections, appraisals, or seller issues absorb the buffer. There is no grace period for a closing on day 181.

## The Tax-Return-Date Trap

The actual closing deadline is the **earlier** of 180 days or the due date, including extensions, for the tax return for the year in which the relinquished property was sold.

That matters for a late-November or December sale. The following April 15 filing date can arrive well before 180 days, silently cutting the period by weeks or months. An exchanger planning around a full 180 days can discover that the real deadline has already passed.

The practical fix is to file an extension for that year’s return. It moves the return due date past day 180 and preserves the full exchange period. If a sale will occur in the last months of the year, the extension should be part of the plan, with CPA confirmation.

## Counting the 180 Days

The count uses calendar days, including weekends and holidays, beginning the day after the relinquished sale closes. As with the 45-day deadline, a weekend or holiday day 180 does not automatically move to the next business day.

The qualified intermediary should calculate and confirm the exact deadline. Track it independently anyway. A day 165 target leaves room for the sort of last-minute movement that is normal in real estate.

Counting conservatively is not optional. A bad count, or an assumption that a weekend deadline rolls forward, can cost the exchange just as surely as failed financing.

## The Relationship to the 45-Day Window

The 45-day period is the first 45 days of the same 180-day period. It is not 45 days followed by a new 180 days.

That overlap is why early identification matters. The sooner a replacement is identified with conviction, the more time remains to negotiate, finance, and close. An exchanger who waits until day 45 leaves a compressed process and little slack.

It also explains the value of a fast-closing backup. If the primary property stalls, a DST identified inside the original 45-day window can close in days during the remaining period.

## Coordinating the Closing

The closing is a logistics exercise involving the seller, lender, title or escrow company, and qualified intermediary. Any one of them can delay the transaction.

Start early: order title work and inspections when the property goes under contract, keep the lender moving, and confirm how and when the QI will wire funds. The QI transfers the proceeds directly to the replacement closing, not to the exchanger.

Regular communication is the defense. Surface problems early and keep a fallback. A closing managed from week one is far less likely to collide with day 180.

## Financing Within 180 Days

New financing is often the largest risk. Underwriting, appraisal, and title work routinely take six to ten weeks, and a slow lender—or one that re-trades terms late—can put the deadline at risk.

Begin the lender conversation before the relinquished sale closes and use a lender that understands 1031 timing. Match the loan to the debt-replacement target, generally at least the debt paid off, to avoid mortgage [boot](insights.html) while satisfying the equal-or-greater-value rule.

This is one reason a leveraged DST can help with a tight clock. Its debt is pre-arranged and non-recourse, eliminating a loan application and the delay it can create.

## Why Closings Slip

The causes are familiar: financing delays, low appraisals, title defects, inspection findings that require renegotiation, and sellers who cannot perform on schedule. Any one can turn 180 days into a scramble.

The real danger is waiting. If a low appraisal or title problem appears at day 150, there may not be enough time left to cure it and close by day 180.

Buffer and a backup change the odds. Target a closing well before day 180 and keep an identified, fast-closing DST in reserve if the primary deal falls apart late.

## The DST as a 180-Day Safety Net

A pre-identified [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) can be a dependable backup because its property is already acquired and its structure is in place. It can close in a few business days, well inside a remaining exchange window.

The strategy is to identify a suitable DST by day 45 alongside the primary target. If the primary closes, the DST is unused. If the primary stalls or fails, the DST can close quickly and preserve full deferral.

A DST can also give precision on equal-or-greater value and debt replacement: an exchanger can invest an exact dollar amount and choose a leveraged trust whose debt matches the old debt. The deadline remains hard; the preparation makes it manageable.

## Key Takeaways

- Close within 180 days—or the earlier tax-return due date. File an extension for a late-year sale.
- Every day is a calendar day, with no grace period; treat day 165 as the target.
- Start financing early and identify a fast-closing DST backup within the 45-day window.

## Disaster Relief and Postponements

The 180-day deadline cannot be extended on request. The exception is IRS disaster relief: when a federally declared disaster affects an area, the IRS may issue a notice postponing 1031 deadlines for affected taxpayers.

Those postponements are tied to a particular disaster, location, and IRS notice. They are not something to build a plan around. If a disaster affects an exchange, check the relevant notice and consult a CPA promptly.

Outside that relief, assume the deadline does not move. For the great majority of exchanges, it does not.

## Boot and the Final Closing

The closing is where boot becomes final. Full deferral generally requires equal-or-greater replacement value, reinvestment of all equity, and replacement of debt. A cheaper purchase or a debt shortfall generally creates taxable boot.

If cash remains after the purchase—for example, because the replacement was less expensive—the qualified intermediary releases it at the end of the exchange period as cash boot. It is taxable up to the amount of gain.

Do the value and debt math before sitting at the closing table. That makes a zero-boot result intentional, or makes any small amount of boot a deliberate choice rather than a surprise.

## A Real-World 180-Day Closing Scenario

Consider an apartment-building sale that closes June 1. Day 180 is November 28; identification is due July 16. The exchanger identifies a primary value-add property and a DST backup by July 5, safely inside the 45-day period.

The primary requires a new $1,200,000 loan. The lender discussion started in May, so underwriting is underway, but a low September appraisal prompts the lender to re-trade terms. With two months left, the deal is uncertain.

Instead of gambling on the primary, the exchanger pivots in early October to the DST already identified. Its property is acquired and its debt is pre-arranged, so it closes in five business days, around October 15—well before November 28. The leveraged DST replaces the old debt automatically, and the investment amount satisfies equal-or-greater value without mortgage boot.

The exchange completes with full deferral. The two protections were the timely backup identification and the decision not to wait until day 175 to abandon a failing primary. Had the original sale closed in late November rather than June, the exchanger would also have needed a tax-return extension so April did not shorten the window.

The takeaway is practical. Financing and sellers consume cushion. The investors who finish cleanly build buffer, begin financing early, and keep a fast-closing DST in reserve.

## Planning Backward From Day 180

The useful way to manage this deadline is to work backward from adjusted day 180 rather than forward from the sale date.

A working schedule can target identification by day 35–40, a replacement under contract by day 70–90, fully approved financing by day 130, and closing by day 160–165. Every milestone leaves room for the previous one to slip without causing a missed deadline.

The backward view also reveals a tax-return-date problem early. Mapping the dates before the sale allows an extension to be filed where necessary and turns “six months” into a concrete closing plan.

## Putting the 180-Day Period to Work: A Pre-Sale Checklist

Start by mapping the likely closing date, day 45, and day 180. Work backward and, if the sale will close in the last months of the year, plan the tax-return extension so the filing date does not shorten the period.

Line up financing before the sale. Since loan underwriting, appraisal, and title can take six to ten weeks, the lender should be moving before the relinquished property closes. Match the new debt to the debt-replacement target, or consider a leveraged DST with pre-arranged debt.

Build a replacement shortlist and identify backups. A DST identified by day 45 can close quickly if the primary transaction stalls, preserving the maximum closing runway.

Set interim milestones rather than watching only the final date. “Under contract by day 80” and “financing approved by day 130” expose a day-160 problem while it can still be addressed.

Assemble the team early: a qualified intermediary before closing, a CPA to handle the extension and Form 8824, and an experienced independent advisor to source and vet replacement property and coordinate deadlines.

Finally, communicate with the seller, lender, title company, and QI. Most missed closings arise from delays that were visible earlier and addressed too late. Preparation, buffer, and an identified backup are how capital is protected here.

## What If You Can't Close in Time?

If day 180 arrives without a closing on an identified property, the exchange generally fails. The sale becomes taxable, and the qualified intermediary returns the funds under the exchange agreement, typically after the period ends.

That is what the pre-identified DST safety net is designed to prevent. If a suitable DST was identified within the 45-day window, it can often close while time remains even after a direct deal fails.

If a closing is at risk as day 180 approaches, act immediately. An experienced independent advisor may be able to pivot the exchange to a fast-closing, exchange-eligible property that was identified in time. There is no substitute for having that viable backup already on the list.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the 180-day exchange period?

It is the 180-calendar-day window after the relinquished-property closing to acquire an identified replacement property. The 45-day identification period is inside it, and the actual deadline is the earlier of day 180 or the applicable tax-return due date including extensions.

### What is the tax-return-date trap?

For a late-year sale, the return due date can arrive before day 180 and shorten the exchange period. Filing an extension generally restores the full 180 days.

### Are the 180 days calendar days?

Yes. They include weekends and holidays, start the day after closing, and have no grace period or automatic next-business-day roll.

### Can the 180-day deadline be extended?

Not on request. IRS notices may postpone deadlines for taxpayers affected by federally declared disasters, but otherwise the date is fixed.

### How do the 45-day and 180-day periods relate?

The 45-day identification window is the first 45 days of the same 180-day period. It is not 45 days followed by a new 180-day closing period.

### Why might my closing slip past 180 days?

Financing, appraisal, title, inspection, and seller-performance problems can use the cushion. Plan an early close and keep a fast-closing backup.

### How does financing affect the 180-day deadline?

Underwriting, appraisal, and title work can take six to ten weeks. Start the lender early, match debt to avoid mortgage boot, or use a leveraged DST whose debt is already in place.

### How does a DST help me meet the 180-day deadline?

A DST can close in a few business days because its property is acquired. Identifying it by day 45 offers a fast closing path if the primary deal stalls, helping preserve full deferral.

### What happens to leftover cash at the end of the exchange?

The qualified intermediary releases it as cash boot at the end of the period. It is taxable up to the amount of gain.

### What happens if I miss the 180-day deadline?

The exchange generally fails, the sale becomes taxable, and the QI returns funds under the exchange agreement after the period. There is no grace period.

### Should I file a tax extension for my 1031 exchange?

If the sale occurs in the last months of the year, an extension generally prevents the return due date from cutting off the full 180-day period. Confirm timing with the CPA handling the exchange.

### Can I close on a property I didn't identify?

No. The replacement must have been identified within the 45-day window. This is why backup properties should be identified too.

### How early should I close before day 180?

Around day 160–165 is a reasonable target because appraisals, title, and lender processes often slip by a few days and day 180 has no grace period.

### Who wires the funds at the replacement closing?

The qualified intermediary holds the proceeds and transfers them directly to the replacement closing. Coordinate the wire with the QI and closing agent in advance.

### Does a reverse or improvement exchange use the same 180 days?

Broadly yes, but both have additional machinery. A reverse exchange parks property with an exchange accommodation titleholder; an improvement exchange requires all value-adding construction to be completed and paid for within 180 days. Both demand more advance planning.

### Can I get more time if a deal falls through near day 180?

Generally no. A failed deal does not create an extension. An identified DST backup is useful precisely because it can close inside the original window.

### What if the 180th day is a weekend or holiday?

It still counts. Plan to close before a weekend or holiday day 180 rather than after it.

### How does the 180-day deadline interact with my CPA?

The CPA can file the extension that avoids the tax-return-date trap and report the completed exchange on Form 8824. Early coordination also supports correct basis and boot reporting.

## Glossary

- **180-Day Exchange Period:** The period after closing to acquire the identified replacement property.
- **Tax-Return Due Date:** The due date, including extensions, that can shorten the period if it comes first.
- **Calendar Days:** All days, including weekends and holidays, used for the 180-day count.
- **Disaster Relief:** Situation-specific IRS notice postponements for federally declared disasters.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The required party that holds proceeds and wires funds to the replacement closing.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** The requirement to acquire at least the relinquished property’s value for full deferral.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Debt relief not offset by new debt or cash; taxable.
- **Cash Boot:** Leftover proceeds not reinvested and released by the QI at the exchange end; taxable.
- **Replacement Property:** The identified like-kind property that must be acquired within 180 days.
- **Leveraged DST:** A DST with pre-arranged non-recourse debt that closes quickly and replaces leverage.
- **Tax Extension:** An extension of the return due date that can preserve the full 180-day period for a late-year sale.
- **Escrow:** The neutral closing process through which title and funds are exchanged.
