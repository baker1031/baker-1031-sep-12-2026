# REIT Taxation: How REIT Dividends Are Taxed

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep finding that a REIT distribution looks simple until the Form 1099-DIV arrives. The cash may be one payment, but its tax character can be several things at once.

REIT dividends are reported on Form 1099-DIV as ordinary, qualified, capital-gain, and return-of-capital components, each treated differently. This guide explains those classifications, the **20%** Section 199A deduction, return of capital, and the taxable-versus-retirement-account trade-off.

First-order thinking is to treat every dollar of a distribution as identical. Second-order thinking follows the classification, cost basis, account location, and current tax rules. For the structural background, see this [primer on REITs and the 721 exchange](reits-guide.html).

## How REIT dividends are classified

REIT distributions are not one uniform kind of income. Form 1099-DIV generally separates them into four components: ordinary (non-qualified) dividends, usually the largest portion and taxed at ordinary income rates; qualified dividends, usually a smaller portion taxed at lower capital-gains rates; capital-gain distributions from property sales, also taxed at capital-gains rates; and return of capital, which is not currently taxed but reduces cost basis.

The same dollar amount called a REIT dividend can therefore receive different treatment depending on its classification. A flat-rate assumption will be wrong. The Form 1099-DIV is the starting point for the correct analysis.

## Ordinary vs. qualified dividends

Most REIT dividends are ordinary, or non-qualified, dividends taxed at ordinary income rates—the same brackets that apply to wages. That result follows from the REIT structure: a REIT generally avoids corporate-level income tax by distributing most of its income, so most distributions do not receive the lower qualified-dividend treatment given to dividends from already-taxed corporate earnings.

Qualified dividends are taxed at lower long-term capital-gains rates, but normally make up a smaller part of a REIT distribution, such as dividends the REIT received from taxable corporations it owns. The trade-off is clear: the REIT generally avoids corporate tax, while shareholders face ordinary-rate treatment on most distributions.

## Return of capital treatment

Return of capital, or ROC, is not current taxable income. It returns part of an investor’s own capital and reduces the cost basis in the REIT shares, deferring tax for the current year.

ROC often results from large non-cash depreciation deductions: taxable income can be lower than cash distributed. The deferral is not permanent. A lower basis increases taxable gain, or reduces loss, on sale. That can shift ordinary-rate income today into capital-gain treatment later, or potentially eliminate deferred tax through a basis step-up at death. It is neither automatically good nor bad; it must be tracked carefully.

## The 20% QBI deduction

The [20% Section 199A (QBI) deduction](reit-dividends-and-the-20-percent-qbi-deduction.html) applies to qualified REIT dividends, which include the ordinary REIT dividend portion. It generally permits a **20%** deduction, reducing the amount subject to tax and lowering the effective top federal rate on eligible ordinary REIT dividends to roughly **29.6%**.

Unlike other QBI, qualified REIT dividends generally do not face the same wage/property tests or income limitations. The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the deduction permanent after it had been scheduled to sunset at the end of 2025. Tax rules can change, so current treatment should be verified with a CPA.

## REITs in taxable vs. retirement accounts

Because most REIT dividends are ordinary income, REITs are often considered well suited to [tax-advantaged accounts (IRA, 401(k))](investing-in-reits-with-a-self-directed-ira.html), where ordinary income is sheltered from current tax and can compound without an annual ordinary-rate drag.

There is an important counterpoint: the **20%** Section 199A deduction helps only in taxable accounts. There is no current tax to deduct against inside an IRA or 401(k), so that benefit is forfeited there. A retirement account shelters ordinary income; a taxable account preserves the deduction. The right asset location depends on rates, other holdings, and individual facts.

## Key Takeaways

- Form 1099-DIV classifies REIT distributions as ordinary, qualified, capital-gain, and return-of-capital components, each with separate treatment.
- Most REIT dividends are ordinary income because the REIT generally paid no corporate-level tax; only a smaller portion is qualified.
- Return of capital is not currently taxed but reduces basis, while the **20%** Section 199A deduction can reduce the effective top rate on eligible ordinary dividends to about **29.6%**.
- Retirement accounts shelter ordinary income, but taxable accounts preserve the 199A deduction; coordinate account placement with a CPA.

## Capital-gain distributions and reporting

When a REIT sells a property at a long-term gain, it can pass some gain through as a capital-gain distribution. That distribution is taxed at lower long-term capital-gains rates regardless of how long the investor held the REIT shares.

Form 1099-DIV reports ordinary dividends, qualified dividends, capital-gain distributions, and return of capital in the relevant boxes to both the investor and the IRS. It is the roadmap a CPA uses to apply ordinary rates, capital-gains rates, the 199A deduction, and any basis reduction correctly. Keep the forms and track basis for the ROC portion.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How are REIT dividends taxed?

They are classified on Form 1099-DIV into ordinary dividends, qualified dividends, capital-gain distributions, and return of capital. Ordinary dividends, usually the largest component, are taxed at ordinary rates; qualified dividends and capital-gain distributions receive capital-gains treatment; ROC is not currently taxed but reduces basis. The 20% Section 199A deduction can reduce tax on the eligible ordinary portion in taxable accounts. Tax rules are technical and fact-specific, so coordinate with a CPA.

### Why are most REIT dividends taxed at ordinary rates?

A REIT generally avoids corporate-level tax by distributing at least **90%** of taxable income. Because that income was not first taxed at the corporate level, most of the resulting dividends do not qualify for lower qualified-dividend rates. The result is single-level shareholder taxation, usually at ordinary income rates, with Section 199A potentially softening the tax.

### What is the difference between ordinary and qualified dividends?

Ordinary dividends are taxed at ordinary income rates. Qualified dividends are taxed at lower long-term capital-gains rates. For REITs, most distributions are ordinary because the REIT generally paid no corporate tax; only a smaller portion, such as qualifying dividends received from taxable corporations, normally receives qualified treatment.

### What is return of capital in a REIT distribution?

It is a distribution that is not currently taxed because it is treated as a return of invested capital. It reduces cost basis. ROC often arises when depreciation lowers taxable income below cash distributed, and the deferred tax becomes relevant when shares are sold.

### Is return of capital good or bad?

Neither automatically. ROC defers current tax and can move income toward capital-gain treatment later, but it lowers basis and increases gain or reduces loss when shares are sold. A basis step-up at death can erase deferred ROC tax. Investors should track basis and consider whether the distribution is supported by sustainable operations.

### What is the 20% QBI deduction for REIT dividends?

Section 199A generally allows a **20%** deduction on qualified REIT dividends, including the ordinary REIT dividend portion. It can reduce the effective top federal rate on that eligible income to roughly **29.6%**. The qualified-REIT-dividend deduction generally applies broadly without the wage/property tests or income limitations associated with other QBI; confirm current rules with a CPA.

### Did the 199A deduction expire?

No. The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act made Section 199A permanent after it had been scheduled to end in 2025. That permanence removed a source of uncertainty, but tax rules can still change and should be verified before relying on them.

### Should I hold REITs in a retirement account or a taxable account?

It depends on the trade-off. Retirement accounts shelter normally ordinary REIT income from current tax, while taxable accounts preserve the **20%** 199A deduction. In an IRA or 401(k), there is no current tax against which to use the deduction. A CPA can weigh current tax rates, other holdings, and overall asset location.

### How are capital-gain distributions from a REIT taxed?

They receive lower long-term capital-gains treatment, regardless of how long the investor held the REIT shares, when they represent the REIT’s long-term property-sale gain. They are reported separately on Form 1099-DIV and are often a smaller component than ordinary dividends.

### What is Form 1099-DIV and how does it relate to REITs?

It is the IRS form that reports dividend and distribution income to the investor and the IRS. For REITs, it identifies ordinary dividends, qualified dividends, capital-gain distributions, and nondividend distributions such as return of capital. The breakdown tells the CPA how to report each component and supports 199A treatment and basis tracking.

### Are REIT dividends double-taxed like regular stock dividends?

Generally no. A regular corporation pays corporate income tax and then shareholders pay tax on dividends. A REIT generally avoids corporate-level tax by distributing at least **90%** of taxable income, so the income is generally taxed once at the shareholder level. The trade-off is ordinary-rate treatment for most REIT dividends rather than the qualified-dividend rates that partially mitigate C-corporation double taxation.

### Does the type of account change how REIT dividends are taxed?

Yes. In a taxable account, the components are currently taxed according to their character and eligible ordinary REIT dividends can receive Section 199A treatment. In a traditional IRA or 401(k), dividends compound tax-deferred and withdrawals are generally ordinary income; the component distinctions and 199A deduction do not apply inside the account. Qualified Roth IRA withdrawals can be tax-free. Account type makes asset location important.

### Are REIT dividends subject to state income tax too?

Generally, yes, depending on the investor’s state of residence and its rules. State treatment of ordinary, qualified, capital-gain, and ROC components can differ from federal treatment. Section 199A is federal, and many states do not conform to it. Some states have no income tax; others tax dividends fully. Confirm state treatment with a CPA.

## Glossary

- **Form 1099-DIV:** The IRS form reporting dividend and distribution components.
- **Ordinary Dividends:** Non-qualified REIT dividends taxed at ordinary income rates.
- **Qualified Dividends:** Dividends taxed at the lower capital-gains rates.
- **Capital-Gain Distribution:** A REIT's property-sale gains passed to shareholders.
- **Return of Capital:** A distribution not currently taxed, reducing basis.
- **Cost Basis:** Your investment's tax basis, reduced by return of capital.
- **Section 199A:** The tax code section providing the 20% QBI deduction.
- **QBI Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
- **Qualified REIT Dividend:** The ordinary REIT dividend eligible for the 199A deduction.
- **Effective Rate:** Roughly 29.6% top rate on ordinary REIT dividends after 199A.
- **Corporate-Level Tax:** Tax a REIT generally avoids by distributing income.
- **Double Taxation:** Corporate-then-shareholder tax that REITs avoid.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to distribute most taxable income.
- **Tax-Advantaged Account:** An IRA or 401(k) sheltering income from current tax.
- **Asset Location:** Choosing which account to hold REITs in for tax efficiency.
- **Basis Step-Up:** A basis reset at death that can erase deferred ROC tax.
