# 1031 Exchange vs. Paying Capital Gains Tax

I have been thinking about how a tax payment changes an investment before a new property is even chosen. Paying tax cuts the reinvestment base permanently. Deferral keeps that capital working, but it also requires continuing real-estate exposure and accepting 1031's rules.

First-order thinking is, “Deferral means paying later.” Second-order thinking measures the full four-layer tax, the compounding lost on it, the step-up-at-death possibility, and the value of flexibility when staying in real estate is no longer the right risk decision.

## Pay now or defer

A taxable sale can include federal capital-gains tax up to 20%, depreciation recapture up to 25% on prior depreciation (often the single largest layer on long-held property), 3.8% [NIIT](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. The combined cost often exceeds a third of gain. A qualifying 1031 defers these layers and reinvests the full pre-tax proceeds; paying tax leaves only the after-tax remainder for any investment.

Illustratively, $1,000,000 of equity with $400,000 gain and roughly 30% tax, or $120,000, leaves about $880,000 after tax versus $1,000,000 to exchange. At 6% for 20 years, the illustrations are $2,822,000 versus $3,207,000 (about $2.82M versus $3.21M): roughly $385,000 more from deferral, more than three times the original tax deferred. A 30-year horizon would amplify the same compounding principle. Results are assumptions, not guarantees, and the CPA should model actual facts.

## Estate, flexibility, and risk

Deferral can compound across exchanges. If final property is held until death, a step-up in basis can erase the deferred gain; that is the swap-till-you-drop strategy. But paying can be preferable where proceeds are needed outside real estate, gain is modest, offsetting losses exist, horizon is short, or the investor values freedom to diversify into any asset class.

> Deferred tax remains invested in the portfolio like an interest-free loan from the government; paying it today ends its compounding permanently.

An exchange requires like-kind real estate, qualified intermediary, same taxpayer, strict 45-day identification and 180-day closing, reinvestment, and debt matching; see the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html). Paying releases these constraints. Deferral may also keep capital concentrated in real estate, so compounding has to be weighed against diversification, liquidity, and performance risk.

The larger pre-tax reinvestment base generally also produces more current income than reinvesting the after-tax remainder, and reinvested income compounds on that larger base. Paying offers the other benefit: flexibility to leave real estate or invest in any asset class without exchange deadlines.

## Key Takeaways

- Paying tax removes a four-layer amount that can no longer compound.
- Deferral keeps the full proceeds invested; over long horizons, the difference grows exponentially.
- A step-up at death can turn long-term deferral into permanent elimination of the deferred gain.
- Paying may be right for non-real-estate needs, small tax, short horizon, or a deliberate diversification exit.

## How to decide for your situation

Ask a CPA four questions: How large is the specific four-layer tax? What is the holding horizon and intent? What is needed from the proceeds? Can the investor accept the like-kind, QI, same-taxpayer, 45-day, 180-day, debt, and reinvestment constraints? Model the after-tax reinvestment path and the full-proceeds exchange path with actual assumptions.

## Deferring vs. paying the tax at a glance

| Factor | 1031 Exchange (Defer) | Sell & Pay Tax |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax at sale | Deferred under §1031 | Due now: gains, recapture, NIIT, state |
| Capital reinvested | Full pre-tax proceeds | After-tax remainder |
| Compounding base | Larger | Smaller after tax leakage |
| Flexibility | Like-kind real estate and rules | Any use or asset class |
| Estate planning | Step-up may erase deferred gain | No deferred gain to erase |
| Complexity | QI, identification, deadlines | Straight sale |
| Best when | Real-estate exposure continues | Investor wants to exit real estate |

## Which should you choose?

Deferral usually wins the math where the investor intends to remain in real estate for years and the replacement is attractive on its own. Paying can be correct for a real exit, non-real-estate cash needs, or a small gain. The choice is individual.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is exchange better than paying?
Often for long-term real-estate investors with meaningful gain, because full proceeds compound and a step-up may erase gain. Paying may be right for cash needs, small tax, short horizon, or an exit from real estate.

### What is the cost of paying?
Potentially capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax, plus lost compounding on the tax paid.

### Is compounding guaranteed?
No. It depends on investment performance; model realistic assumptions.

### How does deferral build more wealth?
It keeps the tax amount in the reinvestment base. Returns then earn returns on a larger base, so the difference widens over time if the two paths earn similar returns.

### What's the long-term difference in the example?
The stated illustration is $2,822,000 versus $3,207,000 after 20 years, a $385,000 gap from keeping $120,000 invested. Actual results vary.

### What is the estate-planning multiplier?
Holding exchanged property until death can step basis to fair market value for heirs and erase deferred gain, turning “pay later” into potentially “never pay.”

### Don't I owe deferred tax eventually?
Not necessarily. Repeated exchanges and a death-time step-up can eliminate it.

### When does paying make more sense?
When cash is needed outside real estate, tax is small, losses offset tax, horizon is short, or flexibility matters more than deferral.

### How big must gain be?
No fixed threshold; larger tax creates a larger compounding benefit. Model it with the CPA.

### Does time horizon matter?
Yes. Deferral's compounding advantage is modest over a short period and grows over decades.

### Can I defer indefinitely?
Yes, through a chain of qualifying exchanges, subject to each exchange's requirements.

### What constraints come with deferral?
Like-kind real estate, QI, deadlines, same taxpayer, reinvestment, and debt replacement for full deferral.

### How do I decide?
Use the four-question CPA framework: tax size, horizon, proceeds purpose, and tolerance for exchange constraints.

### Does deferral produce more current income?
Generally, full pre-tax proceeds create a larger income-producing base than the after-tax remainder.

### Does deferring keep me too concentrated?
It can; paying may enable diversification outside real estate. Weigh concentration against compounding.

### Does paying offer flexibility?
Yes. It allows any asset class without exchange deadlines or reinvestment restrictions.

## Glossary

- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing tax through a 1031 while keeping full proceeds invested.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax.
- **Capital Gains Tax:** Federal tax on sale gain, up to 20% for long-term gain.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, up to 25%, often the single largest layer on long-held property.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** 3.8% tax on investment income for higher-income taxpayers.
- **Compounding:** Exponential return-on-return growth over time.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Original cost reduced by depreciation; low basis creates larger taxable gain.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Reset to fair market value at death that can erase gain for heirs.
- **Swap Till You Drop:** Chaining exchanges and holding until death.
- **Reinvestment Base:** Full proceeds when deferred versus after-tax proceeds when paid.
- **Like-Kind:** Investment real-property standard for a 1031 exchange.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** Independent holder preventing seller constructive receipt.
- **Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received, taxable up to gain.
- **Holding Period:** Length held; longer horizons magnify compounding.
- **Interest-Free Loan Analogy:** Deferred tax stays invested like an interest-free government loan.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** Securitized real-property interest that can qualify as 1031 replacement.
