# Boot, Debt Replacement, and Full Deferral

I have been thinking about the exchanges that look clean because every dollar of sale proceeds was reinvested. That is a useful checkpoint. It is not the whole test.

A 1031 exchange does not defer gain automatically simply because the cash moved into another property. Full deferral requires two things to work together: reinvest all net equity and acquire replacement property of equal or greater value. Because value includes debt, the second requirement usually means replacing the debt that was paid off on the property sold. Miss either target and the difference can be taxable boot inside an otherwise valid exchange.

First-order thinking asks, “Did I put all the cash back?” Second-order thinking asks whether the replacement value, equity, and debt all meet the targets at closing. That is where an exchange can quietly change from full deferral to partial deferral.

This guide explains cash boot, mortgage boot, the equal-or-up rule, debt replacement, and ways to close a gap before it becomes a tax cost. It is educational only. Every figure is illustrative, and a Delaware Statutory Trust, or DST, is a speculative, illiquid, accredited-only security that can lose principal. Consult your CPA, attorney, and qualified intermediary, or QI, before acting.

## Key takeaways

- Full deferral requires all net equity to be reinvested and replacement property of equal or greater value, which usually requires replacing debt paid off at sale.
- Boot is the part of an exchange that is not deferred. Cash boot is equity taken out; mortgage boot is debt not replaced. Both are taxable.
- New debt or outside cash can replace old debt. Cash and debt are interchangeable when filling an old-debt gap, but extra debt does not erase cash boot.
- A valid exchange can still produce a partial tax bill when an investor buys down in value, takes cash out, or carries less debt than was retired.
- A DST can be a way to absorb a remaining equity or debt gap because its non-recourse debt is pre-arranged at the trust level and it can close through a subscription rather than a new personal loan.

## The equal-or-up rule

To defer the entire gain, replacement property must be equal to or greater than the relinquished property in two respects: total value and reinvested equity.

The **value test** requires the replacement purchase price to be at least the net sale price of the property sold. The **equity test** requires all net proceeds—the cash left after paying off loans and closing costs—to go into the replacement. Clear both tests and the gain is fully deferred. Fall short on either and the difference is taxable.

The practical shorthand is “trade up or trade even, never down.” Buying a more expensive replacement, reinvesting all equity, and taking on new debt at least equal to the old debt usually satisfies the structure. Pull cash out and there is cash boot. Buy a cheaper property and there can be boot. Carry a smaller loan than the one retired and there can be mortgage boot even if all sale cash was reinvested.

The old property’s value was made of equity and debt. The replacement needs to rebuild that picture: all equity must come back in, and debt must be replaced with a new loan or with outside cash. Our [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) places the rule in the full exchange process.

## What boot actually is

Boot is any value received in an exchange that is not like-kind replacement real estate. It does not void an exchange. It makes the exchange partially taxable: gain is deferred to the extent the exchange is properly completed, while the boot is recognized.

That is different from missing the 45-day identification deadline, which can void the entire exchange. Boot is a measurable leak, not necessarily a catastrophe.

The recognized gain is the lesser of total realized gain or the boot received. If total gain is $400,000 and boot is $50,000, the recognized amount is $50,000. If total gain is $30,000 and boot is $50,000, the recognized amount is $30,000. You cannot recognize more gain than you actually have, but each dollar of boot within that limit is a dollar of deferral surrendered.

## Cash boot

Cash boot is equity an investor takes out instead of reinvesting. Consider a sale for $1,000,000, a $300,000 loan payoff, and $700,000 of net proceeds. Full deferral requires the full $700,000 to move through the QI into replacement property. Retain $50,000—deliberately or accidentally—and that $50,000 is taxable cash boot.

The QI holds the proceeds so the investor does not receive them. Direct receipt can create cash boot. The issue can also arise in smaller closing details: using exchange funds for non-transactional costs, pocketing a credit at closing, or purchasing a replacement that does not need all of the available equity. Broker commissions, title fees, and the QI’s fee are generally exchange expenses and do not create boot. Prorated rents, security deposits, and loan-related charges paid from exchange money can. The cleaner habit is to put the full equity into replacement property and use outside cash for incidental costs.

## Mortgage boot and debt replacement

Mortgage boot, also called debt-relief boot, is the old debt paid off on the relinquished property that is not replaced on the new property. The IRS treats debt relief as a benefit received.

If a $400,000 mortgage is paid off and the replacement carries only a $300,000 loan, the un-replaced $100,000 is taxable mortgage boot. Reinvesting every dollar of equity does not eliminate it.

The rule that avoids an unnecessary loan is this: debt can be replaced with cash. If the old loan was $400,000, the new loan is $250,000, and the investor contributes $150,000 of outside cash, the debt relief has been fully offset and there is no boot from that gap. Cash and debt are interchangeable when filling the hole created by old debt.

The netting does not operate in reverse. If an investor takes $50,000 of cash boot, borrowing an additional $50,000 on the replacement does not make the cash boot disappear.

| Scenario | What happened | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trade up, full debt | Buy higher value property, reinvest all equity, and take new debt at least equal to old debt | Full deferral, no boot |
| Cash boot | Reinvest equity but keep some proceeds | Tax on the cash retained |
| Mortgage boot | Reinvest all equity but carry a smaller loan | Tax on debt not replaced |
| Debt replaced with cash | Take a smaller new loan but add outside cash to cover the gap | Full deferral, no boot |
| Buy down in value | Buy a replacement for less than the sale price | Boot equal to the shortfall |

For detailed calculations, see [how boot is calculated](1031-exchange-boot.html). The [debt-replacement calculator](debt-replacement-ltv-calculator.html) can help test the figures before a replacement is committed to.

## A worked example

Suppose a rental sells for a net price of $1,000,000. A $400,000 mortgage is paid off and, after closing costs, there is $580,000 of net equity to reinvest. Full deferral requires all $580,000 of equity to be placed in the replacement and the $400,000 debt to be replaced. The simplest structure is replacement property worth at least $1,000,000, with the $580,000 equity and a new loan of at least $400,000. Every component is equal to or greater than its target, so the entire gain defers.

Change one number. If replacement property costs $900,000, all $580,000 of equity may still be reinvested, but only a $320,000 loan is needed. The value and debt are both $100,000 below the targets, producing $100,000 of boot. No cash had to reach the investor for that tax result to arise. A larger property, a larger loan, or a second replacement that absorbs the gap can restore the structure.

## Using a DST to hit full deferral

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) can be useful when the main replacement leaves a measurable gap. A DST is a passive, pre-packaged, 1031-eligible interest in institutional real estate. Its minimum is often around $100,000, which can allow it to absorb the residual equity that did not fit into a primary purchase. If a sale was $1,000,000 and the main property cost $900,000, a $100,000 DST position can fill that $100,000 gap.

The second feature is debt. DST debt is non-recourse and pre-arranged at the trust level, so an investor’s position adds a proportional share of that debt to the replacement side without applying for a personal loan. If $400,000 of debt was retired and the main replacement carries $300,000, a DST allocation can provide the missing $100,000 of replacement debt through its built-in leverage.

The subscription process can also close quickly enough to fit inside the 180-day exchange window, even if the gap is discovered late. [How a 1031 into a DST works](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) explains the mechanics. A DST is not a riskless plug: it is a security with its own risks and requires transaction-specific analysis.

## Planning the exchange to avoid boot

The relevant numbers can be known before closing. Write down three targets for the property being sold:

1. Net sale price.
2. Net equity after loan payoff and closing costs.
3. Debt that will be paid off.

The replacement plan should meet or exceed all three. The classic error is to focus only on equity, reinvest every dollar of cash, and overlook the debt number. That is how a transaction that feels clean produces mortgage boot.

Three habits help. First, decide before sale whether you want to be debt-free. If you do, plan enough outside cash to replace the old debt and confirm it is available. Second, build a backup that can absorb a shortfall. Naming a DST among identified properties can provide a place for leftover equity and replacement debt if the principal purchase comes in light. Third, run the numbers before signing the replacement contract, not after. Coordinate the value, equity, debt, [timeline](1031-exchange-timeline.html), and CPA review before closing day.

## When partial deferral is the right call

Boot is not always a mistake. An investor may need cash to pay other debt, fund a project, or diversify. A partial exchange is valid: the gain associated with the reinvested portion can remain deferred, while tax is paid on the boot. The important point is to choose that result deliberately and price it before closing.

Tax on boot can include long-term capital-gains tax at 15% or 20% for most investors, the 3.8% [net investment income surtax](net-investment-income-tax.html) for higher earners, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) of up to 25% on the recapture portion, and state tax. Recapture deserves special attention because recognized boot is generally treated as recapture first, which can be taxed at a higher rate than regular capital gain. The actual treatment is fact-specific; use a CPA to understand the cost of a deliberate dollar of boot.

## Common boot mistakes

Unwanted boot usually comes from watching the wrong number.

- **Reinvesting equity but ignoring debt:** all the cash goes back in, but the new loan is smaller than the old one.
- **Buying down in value:** the replacement price is below the sale-price target even when all equity is reinvested.
- **Using exchange cash for non-transaction costs:** prorations or repairs can create cash boot.
- **Assuming extra debt cancels cash boot:** it does not; netting runs only one way.
- **Leaving a small gap unfilled:** a small taxable shortfall remains taxable even when a right-sized DST or slightly larger loan could have addressed it.
- **Forgetting closing costs in the value test:** a replacement that looks equal on paper can fall short after fees are accounted for.

The discipline is direct: meet or beat the sale price, reinvest all equity, and replace debt with a new loan or outside cash.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is boot in a 1031 exchange?

Boot is value received that is not like-kind replacement real estate. It includes cash boot—equity taken out—and mortgage boot—debt not replaced. It does not invalidate an otherwise valid exchange; it makes that portion of gain taxable while the rest remains deferred.

### What do I have to do for full deferral?

Buy replacement property of equal or greater value and reinvest all net equity. Since total value includes debt, the old loan generally must be replaced with a new loan or outside cash. Meet both tests and the entire gain can be deferred.

### What is mortgage boot?

Mortgage boot, or debt-relief boot, is the debt paid off on the relinquished property that is not replaced on the new property. The IRS treats the relief from that liability as a benefit received, so a smaller replacement loan can be taxable even if all sale cash is reinvested.

### Can I replace debt with cash instead of a new loan?

Yes. Outside cash can offset debt relief. If $400,000 of old debt is paid off, a $250,000 new loan plus $150,000 of outside cash fully fills the gap, with no mortgage boot from that difference. Cash and debt are interchangeable for this purpose when they replace old debt.

### Does extra debt cancel out cash boot?

No. Cash boot and mortgage boot net only in one direction. Cash can replace debt relief, but $50,000 of extra new debt does not erase $50,000 of exchange cash that was retained.

### How does a DST help me avoid boot?

A DST can have a low minimum that absorbs exact leftover equity, and its pre-arranged trust-level debt adds a proportional debt share to the replacement side without a new personal loan. That can close both an equity and debt gap, and a subscription can close within the 180-day window. A DST is still a speculative, illiquid security with risk.

### Is taking boot ever the right choice?

Sometimes. If cash is genuinely needed, an investor can take boot intentionally, defer the remaining gain, and pay tax on the portion removed. A partial exchange is valid. The choice should be priced first, particularly because boot is generally treated as recapture before capital gain.

## Glossary

- **Boot:** Value received in a 1031 exchange that is not like-kind replacement real estate; it is taxable while the rest of the gain stays deferred.
- **Cash Boot:** Equity from the sale taken out instead of reinvested; it is taxable in the exchange year.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Debt paid off on the relinquished property but not replaced on the new one; it is taxable debt-relief boot even if all cash is reinvested.
- **Equal-or-Up Rule:** The requirement for replacement property to equal or exceed the relinquished property in total value and reinvested equity for full deferral.
- **Debt Replacement:** Offsetting debt paid off with at least as much new debt or equivalent outside cash.
- **Net Equity:** Cash left from a sale after loans and closing costs, all of which must be reinvested for full deferral.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds sale proceeds so the investor does not take receipt, which would create cash boot.
