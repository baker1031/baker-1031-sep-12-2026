# Understanding DST Projected Returns & IRR


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read**

I keep seeing projected returns read as if they were results. A cash-on-cash yield, an internal rate of return (IRR), and perhaps an equity multiple can be useful tools, but none is a promise.

First-order thinking sees the headline yield or IRR. Second-order thinking asks which occupancy, rent-growth, expense, reserve, debt-service, and exit-price assumptions produced it—and how much the answer changes when one of them misses.

DST offerings present cash-on-cash yields and IRRs, but what do they actually mean, and how reliable are they? This guide covers cash-on-cash versus IRR, how projections are built, why returns are not guaranteed, how to stress-test assumptions, and how to compare offerings fairly.

A DST's total return has three possible sources: current distributions during the hold, appreciation realized at sale, and debt paydown (amortization) that builds equity when the DST uses financing. Cash-on-cash measures only current income. IRR seeks to capture all three, weighted for timing. Change an input and the projection changes with it. Every return, yield, and IRR discussed here is a projection, not a promise; past performance does not guarantee future results, and actual results can differ materially. ## Cash-on-Cash vs. IRR

The two common return metrics answer different questions, and confusing them can lead to a poor comparison. Cash-on-cash return is annual cash distributions divided by the equity invested. If an investor puts in **$100,000** and receives **$5,000** in distributions over one year, cash-on-cash return is **5%**. It describes current cash flow for that period; it does not describe appreciation, debt paydown, or the eventual sale.

IRR—internal rate of return—is a time-weighted total-return measure. It annualizes the distributions received each year, debt paydown that builds equity, and sale proceeds, while giving an earlier dollar more weight than a later dollar. A DST can therefore have a [modest cash-on-cash yield but a higher total return](dst-distribution-yield-vs-total-return.html) when appreciation and debt paydown contribute meaningfully at sale.

Cash-on-cash is annual distributions divided by equity: current income for one period, ignoring appreciation and sale. IRR is time-weighted total return over the full hold: distributions, debt paydown, and sale proceeds. Use the former to understand current yield and the latter to understand total return over time. They are not interchangeable.

## How Projections Are Built

Every DST projection is a chain of assumptions. On the income side, a sponsor projects occupancy, rent levels and rent growth, and the timing of lease-up or renewals. Those inputs determine expected gross income year by year.

On the expense side, the model subtracts taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, and utilities, and provides capital reserves for larger repairs and improvements. If the DST uses debt, interest and principal amortization are included. Finally, the model assumes an exit: a future sale price commonly derived from projected exit net operating income and an assumed exit cap rate—the capitalization rate a buyer would pay. Projected income, expenses, reserves, debt service, and exit price produce the distribution stream and the sale proceeds used for cash-on-cash and IRR.

The headline number is only as reliable as this stack of inputs: occupancy, rent growth, expenses, reserves, debt service, and an exit price tied to an assumed cap rate. A projected IRR is not a measurement. It is the output of a model, and its rents, occupancy, and exit-cap-rate assumptions determine its result.

## Why Returns Aren't Guaranteed

Every input can miss. Occupancy can fall if a major tenant leaves or lease-up takes longer. Rent growth can stall or reverse in a soft market. Expenses, including insurance and property taxes, can exceed budget. Reserves can prove too thin when more capital work is needed. The future sale price rests on conditions years away that no one knows today.

The exit cap rate merits particular attention. A projection may assume sale at the same cap rate paid at acquisition. If rates rise or the market weakens, buyers may require a higher cap rate. For the same income, that means a lower sale price and a lower realized IRR, even if operations were sound. Distributions can also be reduced or suspended when income falls short. Actual return can be above or below a projection. Yield, IRR, and equity multiple in an offering are projections, and past sponsor or asset-class performance does not guarantee future results.

## Stress-Testing the Assumptions

The useful exercise is not finding one “right” answer. It is learning how sensitive the projection is when key inputs are less favorable. A projection that works only when everything goes right is fragile; one that remains acceptable under conservative inputs is more robust.

Test lower occupancy by several points, a non-renewed major lease, flat rent growth instead of the assumed increase, and higher operating expenses. Then test a higher exit cap rate, which produces a lower sale price. Re-run the logic and observe the change in cash-on-cash and IRR. If the deal still works with lower rents and a higher exit cap rate, that is useful information. If a modest cap-rate change erases most of the return, the projection relies heavily on a favorable exit. Sensitivity analysis turns a single number into a range of plausible outcomes.

## Key Takeaways

- Cash-on-cash return measures current income (distributions ÷ equity); IRR measures time-weighted total return over the whole hold.

- Every DST projection relies on occupancy, rent growth, expenses, reserves, debt service, and an exit cap rate. The return is only as reliable as those inputs.

- Returns are not guaranteed. Any assumption can miss, and the exit cap rate has an outsized effect on realized IRR.

- Stress-test assumptions and compare offerings with the same metrics and the same assumptions lens. A return that survives conservative inputs is more robust.

## Comparing Offerings Fairly

Comparing headline IRRs without their inputs is a trap. One DST can show a higher projected IRR because it uses more aggressive rent growth or a more favorable exit cap rate, not because the real estate is better.

Put like metrics beside like metrics: cash-on-cash against cash-on-cash and IRR against IRR. Compare rent-growth and occupancy assumptions, exit cap rates, [fee loads](dst-fees-and-load-explained.html), leverage, and hold periods. Then, if possible, run both offerings with the same conservative inputs. A higher return might come from a stronger asset; it might instead come from rosier assumptions. The disciplined comparison identifies which is which.

A higher headline IRR can reflect a better property—or a more optimistic spreadsheet. Compare assumptions, not merely the numbers they produce.

## Reading the Full Return Picture

Total return combines current distributions, appreciation at sale, and debt paydown. Cash-on-cash captures the first component for a single period. IRR combines all three across time. An equity multiple—total dollars returned divided by dollars invested—can help too, but it ignores timing and therefore complements, rather than replaces, IRR.

Ask where an offering's return is expected to come from. Is it mainly current income, with a higher cash-on-cash return and modest appreciation? Or is it driven by a projected gain at sale, with lower current income but a higher IRR dependent on [appreciation rather than cash flow](dst-cash-flow-vs-appreciation-setting-expectations.html) and a favorable exit cap rate? Income-driven returns depend chiefly on remaining leased and producing distributions. Appreciation-driven returns depend more on exit conditions years away. Neither is automatically better, but they carry different risk profiles and can make two similar IRRs very different investments.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is cash-on-cash return in a DST?

Cash-on-cash return is annual cash distributions divided by invested equity. A **$100,000** DST investment that pays **$5,000** over a year has a **5%** cash-on-cash return for that year. It is a useful way to see expected current income, but it ignores appreciation, debt paydown, and sale proceeds. A modest yield can still accompany a strong total return if the property appreciates; a high current yield does not ensure a good overall result. Treat cash-on-cash as one part of the return picture and pair it with IRR.

### What is IRR in a DST?

IRR is the annualized, time-weighted total return over the hold. It includes annual distributions, debt paydown when the DST uses financing, and sale proceeds. Because earlier cash flows count more than later ones, IRR reflects both how much is received and when. A modest current yield can coexist with a higher IRR if appreciation and debt paydown contribute at sale, while a high current yield can coexist with a lower IRR if the exit is weak. A projected IRR is a model output, not a guaranteed result, and actual IRR can differ materially.

### What's the difference between cash-on-cash and IRR?

Cash-on-cash is annual distributions divided by invested equity. It is a current-income measure for one period and omits appreciation, debt paydown, and sale. IRR is time-weighted total return for the whole hold, including distributions, debt paydown, and sale proceeds. Cash-on-cash answers, “What current income am I getting?” IRR answers, “What is the annualized total return, accounting for timing?” Do not compare one DST's cash-on-cash with another's IRR; compare like with like.

### How are DST return projections built?

They are year-by-year cash-flow models. Sponsors project occupancy, rent levels, rent growth, and lease-up or renewal timing to estimate gross income. They subtract operating expenses—taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, and utilities—and capital reserves. Financing adds interest and principal amortization. The model then assumes a sale price, generally by dividing exit net operating income by an exit cap rate. These inputs generate distributions and sale proceeds, then cash-on-cash and IRR. Change any input, and the projected return changes.

### Why aren't DST returns guaranteed?

Every input can miss. Tenants can leave, lease-up can take longer, rents can soften, expenses and repairs can exceed budget, and exit conditions can change. The exit cap rate is especially influential: a higher rate means a lower sale price and lower IRR for the same income. Distributions may also be reduced or suspended when income falls short. All yield, IRR, and equity-multiple figures are projections rather than promises, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### What is an exit cap rate and why does it matter?

The exit cap rate is the capitalization rate assumed for the property's future sale. A model divides projected exit net operating income by that rate to estimate sale price. A lower cap rate produces a higher assumed sale price and IRR; a higher cap rate produces a lower sale price and IRR, for the same income. Cap rates move with interest rates and market conditions. If a model assumes the acquisition cap rate at sale but buyers later demand a higher rate, realized return can fall substantially despite sound operations. Ask what exit cap rate is assumed and test it.

### How do I stress-test a DST projection?

Re-run the model with less favorable inputs: lower occupancy, a lost major lease, flat rents rather than growth, higher expenses, and especially a higher exit cap rate. Compare resulting cash-on-cash and IRR with the base case. A result that remains acceptable under tougher inputs is relatively robust; one that loses most of its return after a small cap-rate or occupancy change is fragile. This turns one headline estimate into a useful range. A broker-dealer can help run and interpret the scenarios.

### How can I compare two DST offerings fairly?

Match cash-on-cash to cash-on-cash and IRR to IRR. Review rent-growth, occupancy, exit-cap-rate, leverage, fee-load, and hold-period assumptions. A higher IRR can represent better real estate, or simply a lower assumed exit cap rate and stronger growth inputs. When possible, apply the same conservative stress case to both offerings. Compare metrics, inputs, and stressed outcomes—not headline figures in isolation. A broker-dealer can help line them up consistently.

### What is an equity multiple in a DST?

An equity multiple is total dollars returned—all distributions plus the investor's share of sale proceeds—divided by dollars invested. A **1.6x** multiple means **$1.60** returned for each **$1.00** invested over the hold. Unlike cash-on-cash or IRR, it ignores timing. Two DSTs can have the same multiple and very different IRRs if one returns capital sooner. Read it with IRR and cash-on-cash, and remember it is an estimate based on assumptions, not a guarantee.

### Is a higher projected IRR always better?

No. It can signal stronger real estate, a stronger market, or a more capable sponsor; it can also reflect aggressive rents, high occupancy, or a low exit cap rate. A high IRR driven by a large gain at sale can be riskier than a lower return supported by durable current income. Ask where the return comes from and how it responds to a change in cap rate or occupancy. Higher is not automatically better.

### What drives a DST's total return?

Three sources: current distributions from net rental income, appreciation realized when the sponsor sells, and debt paydown as loan principal amortizes. IRR combines the three, weighted by timing. An income-driven DST depends largely on leases and distributions; an appreciation-driven DST relies more on future exit conditions; debt paydown is more mechanical but requires leverage. All three are projected, not guaranteed.

### How does leverage affect DST returns?

Financing adds debt paydown as principal amortizes, building equity for sale. It also amplifies gains and losses: a property-value change becomes a larger percentage change in equity. Interest reduces current income, and debt service can squeeze or eliminate distributions if property income falls. Most DSTs use non-recourse financing, so the lender's recourse is generally limited to the property rather than investors personally. Leverage may support projected return and debt replacement for 1031 investors, but it increases sensitivity to performance. Review the loan terms and risk.

### Can DST distributions be reduced or suspended?

Yes. Distributions come from net rental income after expenses and debt service. Lower occupancy, softer rents, higher expenses, or debt service can reduce what is available, and a sponsor can reduce or pause distributions to preserve property finances. A steady projected distribution assumes the income assumptions are met. Do not treat it as a fixed payment; size income expectations conservatively and test lower income. Past distributions do not guarantee future ones.

### Should I focus on yield or total return when evaluating a DST?

Consider both. Current yield is useful for income but misses appreciation, debt paydown, and sale; it can also reflect return of capital or an unsustainable distribution rather than durable performance. A lower current yield with appreciation and debt paydown can produce a higher total return than a high-yield DST with a weak exit. Review IRR and equity multiple alongside current yield, understand the source of return, compare like metrics, and stress-test assumptions before deciding.

## Glossary

**DST** — A Delaware Statutory Trust holding income-producing real estate as fractional interests.

**Cash-on-Cash Return** — Annual cash distributions divided by invested equity — a current-income measure.

**IRR (Internal Rate of Return)** — The time-weighted total return over the hold, accounting for timing.

**Equity Multiple** — Total dollars returned divided by dollars invested (ignores timing).

**Total Return** — Distributions plus appreciation plus debt paydown over the hold.

**Distributions** — Periodic payments of net rental income to DST investors.

**Appreciation** — Any increase in the property's value, realized at sale.

**Debt Paydown (Amortization)** — Loan principal repaid over the hold, building equity.

**Occupancy** — The share of the property leased — a key income assumption.

**Rent Growth** — The assumed rate at which rents rise over the hold.

**Operating Expenses** — Costs (taxes, insurance, management) subtracted from gross income.

**Capital Reserves** — Funds set aside for larger future repairs and improvements.

**Debt Service** — The interest and principal payments on the DST's financing.

**Exit Cap Rate** — The capitalization rate assumed for the future sale price.

**Net Operating Income (NOI)** — Income after operating expenses, before debt service.

**Stress Test** — Re-running a projection with conservative inputs to test sensitivity.
