# 721 Exchange Risks and Drawbacks to Know

I keep coming back to one hard fact about a 721 exchange: the attractive parts are easy to describe, while the parts that matter most show up after an investor has given up the ability to change course cheaply. Deferral, diversification, liquidity, passive income, and estate planning can all be compelling. None makes the trade free of risk.

The first-order view is that a 721 exchange can turn direct real estate into a passive REIT interest. The second-order view asks what is being surrendered for that result: 1031 flexibility, control, an independent exit, and a direct say over property decisions. A responsible decision has to weigh the risks as clearly as the benefits.

## Key Takeaways

- A 721 exchange is generally a one-way commitment to REIT ownership. OP units are not real property, so the move ends future 1031-exchange flexibility.
- The investor becomes passive: the REIT controls management, leasing, financing, improvements, acquisitions, sales, and distributions.
- Results depend on the REIT and its management. Liquidity can be taxed and limited; holding value can fluctuate or decline.
- Fees, transaction complexity, and sponsor quality are practical risks alongside the structural trade-offs.

## The one-way commitment risk

In a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), a property owner contributes real estate for [OP units](op-units-explained.html) in an operating partnership. That may defer the gain, but it is generally an irreversible move into REIT ownership. OP units are not real property, so they are not eligible for another Section 1031 exchange. An investor who later wants to return to direct real estate cannot do so tax-free.

That is the foundational drawback. If circumstances, goals, or preferences change, the investor must convert the units, recognize the deferred gain, and buy property with after-tax proceeds. The structure can therefore lock an investor into REIT ownership even if the investor later prefers direct control, further exchanges, or a different real-estate strategy.

The decision requires conviction before contribution. REIT ownership should be the intended endpoint, not a tentative stop. Owners who value control or ongoing 1031 flexibility should first consider [who should not do a 721 exchange](who-should-not-do-a-721-exchange.html).

## Loss of control

Direct owners control management, leasing, financing, capital improvements, and decisions to hold, sell, or exchange their properties. After a 721 exchange, the investor is a passive OP-unit holder. The REIT controls its portfolio and the investor has no vote on ordinary property decisions.

That can be a welcome trade for an owner who wants to step back from management. It is a major drawback for an owner who wants to choose tenants, decide financing, approve acquisitions or dispositions, or set the pace of distributions. A REIT may make decisions about acquisitions, sales, financing, or distribution levels that the investor would not choose, with little recourse available to a passive unit holder.

The risk is not that passive ownership is inherently wrong. It is that the loss of control must match the investor's actual preference. The benefits of passivity are meaningful only for someone willing to accept the REIT's decisions.

## REIT performance and management risk

After the contribution, distributions, total return, and the value of the holding depend on the REIT's portfolio, strategy, financial health, and management. An investor no longer controls the outcome through direct property management. If the REIT makes poor decisions, has a struggling portfolio, uses weak strategy, or faces financial difficulty, the unit holder bears the result.

A poorly managed or underperforming REIT can reduce distributions and reduce the value of units or shares. A strong, well-managed REIT can reduce this risk, but cannot eliminate it. This is why quality, management, track record, financial health, and sponsor diligence should be examined before a contribution. The fact that an [underperforming REIT directly harms you](721-exchange-risks-reit-underperformance.html) is not a reason to avoid every 721 exchange; it is a reason to evaluate the selected REIT with care.

## Liquidity and conversion-tax risk

OP units can provide a path to liquidity through conversion to REIT shares. That path has two important limits. First, conversion triggers the deferred gain. Liquidity beyond the deferral is real, but it is taxable; the investor cannot access the full value tax-free.

Second, conversion is not always immediate. OP units commonly have a holding period before conversion. Share liquidity also depends on the REIT type. A publicly traded REIT generally offers more robust market liquidity, while a non-traded REIT may offer only limited, program-based liquidity. An investor should not assume fast, unlimited, or free access to capital.

## Market, share-price, and valuation risk

Holding value can fluctuate after a 721 exchange. In a publicly traded REIT, the share price and the economic value represented by OP units can rise or fall with REIT market conditions, interest rates, and investor sentiment. There is no promise of a stable price.

In a non-traded REIT, the concern is different but still material. Shares are not continuously market-priced. The reported value relies on periodic valuations that can be less transparent or less timely than a traded market price, so the investor may have uncertainty about current value.

Neither format removes broad real-estate-market risk. The REIT's properties remain exposed to cycles, changing rates, and sector or local conditions. A 721 exchange can change an investor's risk profile; it does not produce a fixed value or remove the possibility of loss.

## Fees, complexity, and sponsor risk

Transaction costs, ongoing management fees, expenses, and other REIT charges reduce net returns. The fee structure varies by REIT and should be understood before the contribution. Fees are a drag on return in any managed investment, and their effect matters more when distributions or property performance are under pressure.

The transaction is also complex: partnership contribution, securities treatment, tax deferral, basis, conversion, recapture, documentation, and ongoing OP-unit terms all require professional guidance. That effort and cost are part of the trade-off. They also create room for error, including the [common mistakes in a 721 UPREIT exchange](common-mistakes-in-a-721-upreit-exchange.html).

Finally, the investor relies on the sponsor and REIT management. Integrity, competence, conflicts of interest, and financial strength matter. Sponsor risk is the risk that poor management, conflicts, or financial trouble damage the investment. Evaluating a sponsor's track record, reputation, and financial position is part of risk control.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main risks of a 721 exchange?

The main risks are one-way loss of 1031 flexibility, loss of direct control, REIT performance and management dependence, liquidity and conversion-tax limits, market or valuation fluctuations, fees, transaction complexity, and sponsor risk. They do not necessarily outweigh deferral, diversification, liquidity, passivity, and estate-planning benefits, but all should be understood before this generally irreversible decision.

### What's the biggest risk of a 721 exchange?

The one-way commitment is generally the biggest risk. Once property is contributed for OP units, the investor loses 1031 eligibility and cannot tax-free return to direct real estate. If preferences change, exiting requires conversion, recognition of deferred gain, and an after-tax purchase.

### Do I lose control in a 721 exchange?

Yes. The REIT controls management, leasing, financing, improvements, and buy-or-sell decisions. The investor becomes a passive unit holder with little recourse if the REIT's choices differ from the investor's preference. That may suit an owner seeking passivity but is a substantial trade-off for an owner who values control.

### What if the REIT performs poorly?

Poor management, a struggling portfolio, financial difficulty, or strategic errors can hurt distributions and the value of units or shares. The investor is dependent on the REIT and cannot manage the properties directly. Evaluating quality, management, track record, and financial health beforehand mitigates but does not eliminate the risk.

### Is the 721's liquidity risky?

It has costs and limits. Converting OP units to shares triggers deferred gain, so liquidity is taxed. A holding period may apply before conversion. Publicly traded REIT shares can be liquid, while non-traded REIT liquidity may be program-based and constrained. Do not assume easy, free access to wealth.

### Can my holding lose value after a 721 exchange?

Yes. A traded REIT's share price can decline with market conditions, interest rates, or investor sentiment. A non-traded REIT's periodic valuation can be uncertain. The real estate in either portfolio remains exposed to market cycles and may decline in value.

### Are there fees in a 721 exchange?

Yes. Transaction costs and ongoing REIT fees and expenses, including management fees, reduce net returns. The fee schedule varies by REIT and should be evaluated. Complexity also requires professional guidance and adds cost and effort.

### What is sponsor risk?

Sponsor risk is reliance on the sponsor's and management's integrity, competence, and financial health. Poor management, conflicts of interest, or financial trouble can affect the REIT and its investors. Review track record, reputation, and financial strength before contributing.

### Do these risks mean I shouldn't do a 721 exchange?

Not necessarily. They must be weighed against deferral, diversification, liquidity, passivity, and estate planning. They may be acceptable for an owner ready for REIT ownership and unacceptable for an owner who needs direct control or 1031 flexibility. The risks inform the decision; they do not automatically disqualify it.

### How can I mitigate the 721 exchange's risks?

Be certain before committing, select a strong REIT and sound sponsor, understand fees, plan conversion tax, and work with tax, legal, and securities professionals. If liquidity and pricing matter, a publicly traded REIT may be preferable. The REIT's internal diversification can reduce property-specific exposure, but no mitigation eliminates the risks.

### Are non-traded REITs riskier in a 721 exchange?

They carry particular liquidity and valuation concerns: liquidity is often program-based rather than market-traded, periodic appraisals replace market pricing, and fees can be higher. They can also have merits depending on an offering. [FINRA cautions investors to review non-traded REITs carefully](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/real-estate-investment-trusts-reits).

### Should I get professional advice on the risks?

Yes. A financial advisor or securities professional can evaluate suitability and risk, a CPA can address tax risks, and an attorney can address structuring and documents. The transaction's consequence and complexity make independent professional guidance important.

### How do the risks compare to keeping my property?

Keeping direct property has concentration, management burden, illiquidity, tenant, and local-market risk. A 721 exchange can reduce single-property concentration and management work, but it substitutes REIT dependence, market or valuation exposure, and one-way-commitment risk. This is a comparison of risk profiles, not risk versus no risk.

### Are the risks different for a publicly traded versus non-traded REIT?

Yes. A publicly traded REIT has daily market-price volatility but generally robust liquidity and transparent pricing. A non-traded REIT can have less daily volatility but more valuation uncertainty, limited program liquidity, and often higher fees. The investor should decide which set of trade-offs fits the need for liquidity and transparency.

## Glossary

- **One-Way Commitment Risk:** The risk of being locked into generally irreversible REIT ownership.
- **Loss of Control:** Becoming a passive investor while the REIT controls the real estate.
- **REIT Performance Risk:** The risk that the REIT underperforms and hurts returns.
- **Management Risk:** The risk arising from the REIT's management decisions.
- **Conversion-Tax Risk:** The tax triggered when liquidity is accessed through conversion.
- **Liquidity Limitation:** The holding-period and non-traded constraints on liquidity.
- **Share-Price Risk:** The volatility of a traded REIT's share value.
- **Valuation Risk:** The uncertainty of a non-traded REIT's value.
- **Real Estate Market Risk:** The broad cyclical risk affecting REIT properties.
- **Fees:** REIT costs that reduce net returns.
- **Complexity:** The transaction's intricacy and need for professional guidance.
- **Sponsor Risk:** The risk tied to the REIT sponsor's integrity and competence.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** A REIT with limited liquidity and valuation uncertainty.
- **Publicly-Traded REIT:** A REIT with market liquidity and pricing.
- **Irreversibility:** The generally permanent nature of the 721 move.
- **Suitability Review:** The assessment that weighs risks against an investor's circumstances.
