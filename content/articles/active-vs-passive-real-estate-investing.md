# Active vs. Passive Real Estate Investing

I have been thinking about how often “real estate investor” covers two very different jobs. One owner is screening tenants, finding a roofer, and refinancing a loan. Another is reading a quarterly statement and depositing a distribution. They may hold exposure to the same asset class, but their time, control, taxes, and exit options are not remotely the same.

Active ownership means holding title and operating the property. Passive ownership means funding a sponsor-managed position—such as a Delaware Statutory Trust, REIT, or syndication—and accepting the results of someone else’s operating decisions. Neither label is a verdict. They are choices about work, control, risk, and the type of return an investor is trying to earn.

First-order thinking says passive is easier and active has more upside. Second-order thinking asks what the investor gives up for that ease, whether a sponsor’s incentives match the investor’s, how tax and liquidity change, and whether the investor has been paid enough for the loss of control. The right choice is usually less about a broad label than about the investor’s actual life and capital needs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Active** generally means direct ownership and control. **Passive** generally means a fractional or fund position run by a sponsor.
- The choices form a spectrum: self-managed rental, managed rental, syndication, DST, and REIT. Each move typically gives up effort and control for simplicity.
- Active owners retain the full direct-owner toolkit: depreciation, cost segregation, **1031** eligibility, and potentially real-estate-professional status.
- Passive owners can still receive depreciation, but §**469** restricts passive losses. A DST can remain **1031** eligible and is reported on a **1099** plus grantor letter rather than a K-1.
- REIT income is generally **1099**-DIV ordinary dividend income. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** §**199**A deduction. Public REIT shares are liquid; direct real estate and DSTs are not.
- An owner can shift from active to passive through a **1031** exchange into a DST or a **721** contribution to a REIT operating partnership.

## Active and Passive, Defined Plainly

An active real-estate investor holds title and runs the asset. That owner finds the deal, signs the loan, sets rents, approves capital projects, chooses refinancing, and decides when to sell. The upside created through operations belongs to the owner. So do vacancies, special assessments, financing pressure, and late-night calls.

Passive ownership puts capital into a vehicle someone else operates. The investor may own a fractional beneficial interest in a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html), shares in a [REIT](reits-guide.html), or a limited-partner interest in a syndication. The sponsor or manager selects tenants, manages the roof, carries out the business plan, and reports results to investors.

Who operates the property drives most of the comparison: time demanded, control retained, tax treatment, liquidity, and fit.

## The Spectrum Between Them

Active and passive are endpoints, not separate boxes. Investors often move along the spectrum as their time, skill, liquidity needs, or estate plans change.

1. **Direct rental, self-operated.** The investor owns a duplex or small commercial property and does everything. It is maximum control and maximum work.
2. **Direct rental with a property manager.** The owner still decides whether to buy, sell, or refinance, but delegates daily work. The usual management cost is about **8** to **10** percent of rents. Work falls; the major decisions, financing, and liability stay with the owner.
3. **Syndication.** The investor becomes a limited partner in a sponsor’s single-property deal, receives a K-1, and gives up operating control. The [private REIT versus syndication comparison](private-reit-vs-syndication.html) explains how that concentrated partnership interest differs from a pooled fund.
4. **DST.** The investor owns a fractional beneficial interest in a trust holding one or more institutional properties. The sponsor manages it. A DST has relatively low minimums and can be **1031** eligible; a syndication LP interest is not.
5. **REIT.** The investor owns shares in a company that can hold dozens or hundreds of properties. It is broadly diversified and hands-off. A public REIT’s shares can be sold in seconds.

Moving toward passivity tends to cut the hours required and the control retained. It also changes reporting and liquidity. Hiring a manager does not make a direct landlord passive for tax purposes. Completing a **1031** into a DST does.

## The Time and Effort Trade-Off

The cost that rarely appears on a statement is time. A self-managed rental is a real operating job: screening tenants, pulling credit, handling turns, paint and cleaning, re-leasing between occupants, approving a compressor, parking-lot work, or roof replacement, and finding the cash to pay for it. Financing does not manage itself either. Renewing or refinancing a loan requires re-qualification and rate shopping.

Each task may be manageable alone. Together, across a portfolio, they form a standing commitment that does not pause for travel, illness, or a weekend.

A property manager can remove daily friction without removing ownership. The owner still answers questions, approves significant expenses, carries the debt, and remains liable for the central decisions.

Passive ownership removes most operations after funding. A DST or REIT sponsor handles leasing, capital projects, and debt. The investor’s work may become reading a quarterly distribution notice and preparing a tax form once a year. For a person with a busy career, frequent travel, a change in life stage, or no desire to continue managing tenants, that difference can be the entire point.

## The Control Trade-Off

Control and effort move together, but they are not the same thing. A direct owner sets the business plan, chooses when to sell into a strong market or hold through a weak one, decides between cash-out refinancing and debt reduction, and can pursue value creation by raising rents, adding units, or repositioning a building. That authority is why active ownership can have a higher ceiling.

A passive investor delegates those choices. The investor does not select the refinance, vote on a sale, or change the hold period. The outcome rests on the sponsor’s judgment, discipline, and alignment.

In a DST, the constraints go further. [Revenue Ruling 2004-86](dst-guide.html) underpins the structure’s **1031** treatment and sharply limits what the trustee can do. That can be a benefit when passivity is desired and a restriction when an investor wants a say. In plain terms, the investor pays for the manager’s time with a slice of control.

## The Tax Trade-Off

The tax consequences deserve careful treatment. This is general education, not advice for a tax return.

### What Active Direct Owners Get

Direct owners may take depreciation against rental income and can accelerate some deductions with a cost-segregation study that assigns portions of a building to shorter recovery lives. When a property is sold, an owner may use a [**1031** exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) to defer gain into a replacement selected on the owner’s own timetable.

An owner who actually meets the material-participation rules for real-estate-professional status may be able to have rental losses offset other income, including wages and business income. That result is powerful, narrowly defined, and available only to someone who is genuinely active.

### What Passive Investors Get—and Give Up

Passive owners can still receive real estate’s central non-cash deduction: depreciation can flow through and shelter part of the income collected. The difference is loss treatment. Under §**469**, passive losses generally offset only passive income, not salary or active-business income. Excess losses are suspended and carried forward until passive income arises or the activity is disposed of.

Vehicle structure then matters.

- **DST.** Under Revenue Ruling **2004-86**, a qualifying DST is a grantor trust and the investor is treated as owning a direct interest in the underlying real estate. That is why it can be **1031** eligible. Reporting is a **1099** plus grantor letter, not a K-1. Depreciation can pass through, and the investor can generally pursue another **1031** when the DST sells.
- **REIT.** A REIT issues a **1099**-DIV. Most income is ordinary dividend income rather than long-term capital gain. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** §**199**A deduction, which is permanent under **2025** law. The deduction softens the ordinary rate; it does not convert the income to a long-term capital gain. REIT shares are not **1031** eligible.
- **Syndication.** A typical LP interest produces a K-1, can pass through depreciation, and remains subject to §**469**. The LP interest itself is not **1031** eligible.

For context, long-term capital-gain rates can be **0**, **15**, or **20** percent depending on income. The **3.8** percent net investment income tax can apply once modified adjusted gross income exceeds **$200,000** for a single filer or **$250,000** for a married couple filing jointly. The main real-estate gain-deferral choices are a **1031** exchange, a DST, an Opportunity Zone fund, and a **721** or UPREIT contribution. The [tax-deferral strategies comparison](tax-deferral-strategies-compared.html) outlines their differences.

## Liquidity and Getting Capital Back

Liquidity is a quiet but decisive difference. Direct property is naturally illiquid: selling can take months, costs commissions, and exposes the owner to the market on the closing date. A DST is illiquid too. There is little meaningful secondary market, and the typical hold period can run five to ten years.

Public REITs sit at the other end. Their shares trade on an exchange and can clear in seconds. Non-traded REITs fall between these choices: they may offer periodic redemptions, but redemptions are capped, may be gated in stress, and are not a promise of liquidity.

The fit must match the time horizon. Capital needed in two years does not belong in an illiquid DST or non-traded REIT.

## The Passive Vehicles Up Close: DSTs and REITs

### The Delaware Statutory Trust

A DST can fit an owner who wants continued real-estate exposure, deferred gain, and no operating role. It is **1031** eligible, so sale proceeds can move into one or more DSTs without triggering current tax when the exchange rules are met. Minimums are low relative to buying a building outright—often around **$100,000**—which can allow an exchange to be spread among several DSTs and property types rather than concentrated in one replacement.

The sponsor handles operations and distributions are reported through the **1099**-and-grantor-letter approach. The trade is serious: illiquidity, little to no control, and offering loads and ongoing fees. The [DST mechanics guide](how-to-invest-in-a-dst.html) explains the purchase process, and the [DST versus REIT comparison](dst-vs-reit.html) places the two structures side by side.

### The REIT, Public and Non-Traded

A REIT provides broad exposure to a portfolio rather than a single property. Public REITs generally offer daily liquidity, low minimums, and visible pricing. The cost is share-price volatility that may follow stock-market conditions as much as property fundamentals.

Non-traded REITs are sold away from an exchange and valued periodically, rather than moment by moment. That can reduce the visibility of price movement, but it comes with limited redemption, higher fees, and less transparency. Both forms use **1099**-DIV reporting and can provide the §**199**A benefit for qualifying dividends, but neither share class is eligible for a **1031**. The [REIT guide](reits-guide.html) explains the structures in more depth; a **721** is the route that can connect property to REIT operating-partnership exposure.

## Who Fits Active, and Who Fits Passive?

The choice is a function of time, expertise, life stage, and estate goals. It is not a claim that one person is smarter than another.

Active ownership can suit an investor who has time, enjoys the work, has or wants operating expertise, and seeks the higher ceiling available from control and value creation. It can also suit someone with a long horizon who wants access to real-estate-professional status and its potential loss treatment.

Passive ownership can suit someone whose time has higher value elsewhere, who is winding down, who wants diversification and income without operations, or who is planning an estate transition. Passive interests may be simpler to pass than a building with tenants and debt. In particular, DST interests can receive a step-up in basis at death, subject to the investor’s circumstances and advice. It can also suit the person who has had enough of the **2** a.m. phone call.

Many investors hold both: a small group of direct properties for control and some passive exposure for income and diversification. The mix can be the point.

## Moving From Active to Passive

The practical question is often not whether active or passive is better in theory. It is how an owner with appreciated, hands-on property steps back without writing a large check to the IRS.

One route is a **1031** exchange into one or more DSTs. The owner sells, uses a qualified intermediary, follows the exchange timing rules, and moves the proceeds into passive interests. The gain is deferred, depreciation continues, and operations stop at the exchange closing. The [**1031** exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) covers the relevant deadlines and identification process.

Another route is a **721** exchange, often called an UPREIT transaction. The owner contributes property—or commonly a DST interest held for a period—into a REIT operating partnership in exchange for OP units. Gain is deferred and the result is diversified REIT exposure. The meaningful trade-off is finality: the **721** route is generally one-way. Once inside the REIT, the investor cannot generally **1031** back into direct property. The [721 exchange guide](721-exchange-guide.html) explains the mechanics and lock-in.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating passive as risk-free.** Less work does not mean less risk. DSTs and non-traded REITs can be speculative, illiquid, and subject to loss of principal. Sponsor competence and property fundamentals still matter.
- **Ignoring liquidity until it is needed.** Capital committed to a DST or non-traded REIT may not have an available exit when life changes.
- **Assuming REIT dividends are capital gains.** Most are ordinary **1099**-DIV income. The §**199**A deduction helps but does not make it a long-term capital gain.
- **Expecting passive losses to shelter salary.** §**469** generally limits passive losses to passive income. A passive investor does not become a real-estate professional by owning a passive position.
- **Expecting a DST K-1.** A DST generally reports through a **1099** and grantor letter, not a K-1.
- **Forgetting that a 721 is generally one-way.** OP units defer tax but end the investor’s ability to **1031** back to direct property. Assess that finality before accepting units.

## Active vs. Passive at a Glance

| Factor | Active (direct) | Passive (DST / REIT) |

| Time and effort | High: leasing, capital expenditures, and financing | Minimal after funding; sponsor operates |
| Control | Full: buy, sell, refinance, business plan | Delegated to sponsor or manager; little or no say |
| Typical minimum | Full purchase price, down payment, and reserves | DST around **$100k**; a public REIT can start with **1** share |

| Typical fit | Hands-on investor with time, expertise, and a long horizon | Busy or retiring investor seeking income and diversification |

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between active and passive real estate investing?

Active investing means direct ownership and operation, with both control and responsibility. Passive investing means funding a sponsor-managed vehicle—such as a DST, REIT, or syndication—and receiving a share of the results without operating the property.

### Does going passive mean giving up real estate’s tax benefits?

No. Depreciation can still pass through. The main limit is §**469**, which generally restricts passive losses to passive income. A DST can remain **1031** eligible, so depreciation and tax deferral can remain available when the relevant requirements are met.

### How is a DST taxed and reported?

Under Revenue Ruling **2004-86**, a qualifying DST is a grantor trust. The investor is treated as owning underlying real estate, which supports **1031** eligibility. Income is generally reported on a **1099** plus a grantor letter rather than a K-1, and depreciation can pass through.

### How is REIT income taxed?

A REIT issues a **1099**-DIV, and most of the income is ordinary dividend income rather than capital gain. Qualified REIT dividends can receive the **20%** §**199**A deduction, which is permanent under **2025** law. REIT shares cannot be used in a **1031** exchange.

### Which is more liquid?

Public REITs are generally the most liquid because shares trade on an exchange and clear in seconds. Direct property and DSTs are illiquid. Non-traded REITs may offer capped redemption programs that can be gated under stress.

### Can I move from active to passive without paying tax?

A **1031** exchange into a DST can defer gain while moving an owner from direct management to a passive interest. A **721** contribution to a REIT operating partnership can also defer gain into diversified REIT exposure, but it is generally one-way.

### Is passive investing lower risk than active investing?

No. It is less operationally demanding, but DSTs and non-traded REITs can be speculative, illiquid, and subject to loss of principal. Passive investors also surrender control and depend on the sponsor’s judgment. Passive removes the operating burden, not investment risk.

## Glossary

- **Passive Activity:** A trade or business in which the investor does not materially participate. Under §**469**, losses generally offset only passive income, with excess suspended and carried forward.
- **Real Estate Professional Status:** A tax designation for a taxpayer who satisfies IRS material-participation and hours tests. It can allow rental losses to offset other income, including wages, when otherwise blocked for passive investors.
- **Depreciation:** A non-cash deduction that recovers a building’s cost over time and can shelter rental income. It may pass through to DST and syndication investors as well as direct owners.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust:** A trust holding institutional real estate in which investors own fractional beneficial interests. It can be **1031** eligible under Rev. Rul. **2004-86** and generally reports **1099** plus a grantor letter rather than a K-1.
- **REIT:** A real estate investment trust that owns a portfolio of properties and distributes mostly ordinary **1099**-DIV income. Qualified dividends can receive a **20%** §**199**A deduction.
- **Syndication:** A pooled, usually single-property investment with a sponsor and limited partners. LP interests use a K-1 and are not **1031** eligible.
- **§199A Deduction:** A **20%** pass-through deduction that can apply to qualified REIT dividends, reducing the effective tax rate on that income. It is permanent under **2025** law.
- **Cost Segregation:** An engineering-based study that moves portions of a building into shorter recovery lives to accelerate depreciation in the early years of ownership.
