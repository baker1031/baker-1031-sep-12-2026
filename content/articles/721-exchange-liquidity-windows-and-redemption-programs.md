# 721 Exchange Liquidity Windows & Redemption Programs

I keep seeing liquidity described as if the investor simply presses “sell.” That is not how it works after a 721 exchange. Before cash is available, the investor may have to wait through a lock-up, follow conversion procedures, recognize deferred gain, and then use either a public market or a limited redemption program.

Public REIT shares can be sold in the market after conversion. Non-traded REIT shares rely chiefly on periodic, limited, and discretionary redemption programs.

First-order thinking says convertible OP units create liquidity. Second-order thinking asks when conversion is permitted, whether a redemption cap is full, whether a program can be suspended, what the price will be, and how much tax conversion creates. Liquidity that is conditional should never be used to fund an urgent, fixed-date obligation.

## How liquidity works after the lock-up

Once [the lock-up period](721-exchange-lock-up-periods-explained.html) ends, liquidity follows two steps. First, the investor converts [OP units](op-units-explained.html) to REIT shares. Conversion generally triggers the deferred gain. Second, the investor accesses cash from those shares: a market sale for a publicly traded REIT or a redemption request for a non-traded REIT.

The conversion is not the same thing as cash. It is the gateway. The market or redemption program is the second mechanism, and the investor’s actual liquidity depends on that mechanism and the REIT type.

For a traded REIT, conversion plus market sale can provide a relatively direct route to cash. For a non-traded REIT, conversion is followed by the terms of the redemption program. The tax result begins at conversion even if cash is delayed.

## Conversion windows

The partnership agreement can set conversion windows, notice requirements, minimum or maximum amounts, and other procedures. A conversion may be available only at specified times or after a notice period. It is not always instantaneous simply because the lock-up has ended.

Terms vary by REIT. Some process requests with relative flexibility; others use defined windows or procedures. The investor should read the partnership agreement and offering materials to understand exactly when and how units can become shares.

Conversion timing affects both the eventual share liquidity and tax. A properly timed plan accounts for the conversion calendar, notice requirements, the holder’s income, and the cash need that prompted the request.

## Redemption programs (non-traded REITs)

For [non-traded REITs](insights.html), there is no public trading market. The primary liquidity mechanism is a redemption program: periodically, such as quarterly, the REIT may offer to buy back a limited amount of investor shares. The investor requests redemption after converting OP units to shares.

These programs are periodic, limited, conditional, and subject to the REIT’s discretion. A program can have a cap expressed as a percentage of outstanding shares, a stated pricing method based on net asset value, or a discount to NAV, particularly for early redemption. It is not an open promise to buy every share whenever the investor wants cash.

Frequency, cap, pricing, eligibility, and discretion are the key terms. For a non-traded REIT, the redemption program is the core liquidity mechanism and should be understood before the investment is made.

## Limits and conditions

Redemption caps matter. When requests exceed the cap for a period, shares can be prorated or queued. The investor may receive only partial cash or wait until a later period. The REIT can generally limit, suspend, or modify a program, especially in adverse conditions or when aggregate redemption demand is high.

That means non-traded REIT liquidity is not guaranteed. A request may be accepted late, in part, or not at all if the program has reached its limit or been suspended. Redemption programs at non-traded REITs have been restricted in stressed conditions, which is why they are not a substitute for an emergency reserve.

Publicly traded REIT shares normally avoid redemption caps after conversion because a market sale supplies the cash. They still carry price risk and conversion terms, and the conversion remains taxable, but the mechanics are generally more reliable than a discretionary buyback program.

## Planning your liquidity

Planning begins with the specific [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) and destination REIT. Identify the lock-up period, conversion windows, notices, market or redemption path, redemption frequency, cap, pricing, and tax consequence before committing capital.

An investor in a non-traded REIT should request redemption early if cash may be needed and should maintain other sources of liquidity for material near-term needs. Do not assume a rapid or full redemption. A traded REIT offers a more direct market path after conversion, but tax timing and market price remain part of the plan.

The practical approach is to match the asset with the cash-flow calendar: needed cash, other reserves, planned conversions, expected tax, and the REIT’s written terms. Planning protects against the unpleasant surprise of discovering that an asset is liquid in theory but not on the date capital is needed.

### Key takeaways

- After the lock-up, liquidity is a two-step process: convert OP units to shares, which triggers gain, then sell shares or request redemption.
- Conversion may involve windows, notices, and procedures under the partnership agreement.
- Non-traded REIT redemption programs are periodic, capped, conditional, and discretionary.
- Plan around timing, tax, caps, and other liquidity sources. Do not assume immediate, unlimited cash access, especially in a non-traded REIT.

## Traded vs. non-traded liquidity

The distinction is central. In a publicly traded REIT, shares obtained after conversion can generally be sold quickly in the market at market prices. The main constraints are lock-up, conversion procedures, tax, and price volatility.

In a non-traded REIT, shares are not traded in a public market. Liquidity is through the redemption program, which has its own dates, limits, pricing, and discretion. The program can be useful, but it is structurally less certain and less immediate.

If liquidity is a high priority, the traded-versus-non-traded decision deserves substantial weight. A traded REIT generally provides more reliable post-conversion market liquidity. A non-traded REIT demands more conservative planning around cash needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I access liquidity after a 721 exchange?

After the lock-up, convert OP units to shares, which generally triggers deferred gain. Then sell shares in the market for a traded REIT or request redemption for a non-traded REIT. It is a two-step, not one-step, process.

### What are conversion windows?

They are the periods, notices, and procedures governing when OP units can be converted after lock-up. The partnership agreement can require notice or impose other terms. They vary by REIT and affect when share liquidity can begin.

### What is a redemption program?

It is a non-traded REIT’s periodic program for buying back a limited amount of investor shares. Investors request redemption after conversion rather than selling on a public market. The program is the main liquidity path for a non-traded REIT.

### Are there limits on redemptions?

Yes. Programs often cap redemptions per period, frequently as a percentage of shares. Excess requests can be prorated or queued. The REIT can also limit, suspend, or modify the program, especially in adverse conditions.

### Is non-traded REIT liquidity guaranteed?

No. It depends on the cap, program conditions, pricing, and REIT discretion. A redemption request may be delayed, partially filled, or unavailable. Investors should not rely on it for time-sensitive or critical needs.

### How is traded REIT liquidity different?

After conversion, public REIT shares can generally be sold in the market, without a REIT redemption cap. Market liquidity is usually stronger than redemption liquidity, but market price, conversion timing, and tax still matter.

### How do I plan my liquidity after a 721 exchange?

Know the lock-up, conversion procedures, and, for a non-traded REIT, redemption frequency, caps, pricing, and conditions. Maintain other near-term liquidity sources and plan conversions with the tax impact. Do not assume cash is immediate or unlimited.

### Can the REIT suspend redemptions?

Yes. A non-traded REIT can generally limit, suspend, or modify a program, particularly in adverse conditions or when redemption demand exceeds capacity. A suspension can make liquidity temporarily unavailable.

### Does converting to shares give me immediate liquidity?

For a traded REIT, largely yes after conversion procedures are completed, because shares may be sold in the market. For a non-traded REIT, no: converted shares still depend on a periodic, limited redemption program.

### How does Baker 1031 help with liquidity planning?

### What if I need liquidity but the redemption program is full?

The request may be prorated, queued, or unavailable until a later period or reopening. This is why non-traded REIT redemptions should not be used for urgent needs and why other liquidity should be maintained.

### Are there fees to redeem or convert?

Possibly. A non-traded REIT may redeem below NAV, especially for early redemptions. Conversion itself generally has no direct fee but can trigger tax. A market sale has usual brokerage costs. Review the specific terms for net liquidity.

### Does the lock-up period affect these liquidity mechanisms?

Yes. The lock-up comes first. No conversion, market sale of converted shares, or redemption program access is available through OP units until conversion is permitted. Distributions may continue during the lock-up.

### How does the step-up interact with these liquidity mechanisms?

An investor can hold units until death, when a step-up may erase deferred gain for heirs under current law. Heirs can then use conversion, redemption, or sale with reduced tax on prior appreciation. Investors may use liquidity for needed cash during life and hold remaining units toward a possible step-up.

### Should liquidity expectations affect my choice of REIT?

Yes. A traded REIT generally provides more reliable market liquidity after conversion. A non-traded REIT has program-based, capped, discretionary liquidity. Liquidity needs should be a major factor in selecting the REIT.

### Can I plan conversions and redemptions around tax years?

Yes. Because conversion triggers deferred gain, conversions can be spread across tax years, timed in lower-income years, or coordinated with losses. The CPA should coordinate liquidity timing and tax planning.

## Glossary

### Liquidity Window

A period or mechanism for accessing cash after the lock-up.

### Conversion

Exchanging OP units for REIT shares, the first liquidity step and generally a taxable event.

### Conversion Window

The times and terms for converting units to shares.

### Redemption Program

A non-traded REIT’s periodic share-buyback mechanism.

### Redemption Cap

The limit on shares redeemable in a period.

### Proration

A reduction of redemption amounts when requests exceed the cap.

### REIT Discretion

The REIT’s ability to limit or suspend redemptions.

### Market Liquidity

Selling traded-REIT shares in the market after conversion.

### Periodic Redemption

Redemptions at set intervals rather than continuously.

### Lock-Up Period

The initial holding period before conversion is allowed.

### Notice Requirement

Advance notice required for a conversion or redemption request.

### Suspension

The REIT pausing redemptions, a non-traded liquidity risk.

### Traded REIT

A REIT whose shares have market liquidity after conversion.

### Non-Traded REIT

A REIT with limited, redemption-based liquidity.

### Conversion Tax

The deferred gain generally triggered by converting units.

### Liquidity Planning

Aligning cash needs with the available mechanisms and constraints.
