# The CPA's 2026 Guide to 1031 Exchanges

I have been thinking about the point at which a 1031 exchange actually becomes difficult. It is rarely when a client reads the statute. It is when a sale is already headed for closing and nobody has mapped the taxpayer, basis, intermediary, replacement plan, and tax-return deadline.

This is a working reference for advising clients on Section 1031: tax mechanics, reporting, recurring traps, and a client-ready checklist. It pairs with the client-facing [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html). Confirm current law against primary authority before applying it.

First-order thinking says the exchange is a way to avoid today’s tax. Second-order thinking says it is a tightly timed nonrecognition structure that carries the client’s tax basis, lower future depreciation, and future taxable gain into the replacement asset. The deferred tax has not disappeared. Good advice makes that clear before a contract is signed.

## Key Takeaways for Advisors

- After TCJA, Section 1031 applies only to real property. A qualifying exchange defers capital gain, unrecaptured §1250 recapture, and potential NIIT through substituted or carryover basis.
- **Form 8824** reports the exchange, recognized gain to the extent of boot, and replacement-property basis.
- The **45-day** identification and **180-day** completion periods are effectively jurisdictional. Late-year sales require return-extension planning.
- Watch constructive receipt, debt and boot netting, related-party §1031(f), drop-and-swap facts, and state clawback rules, including California **FTB Form 3840**.

## How a 1031 Exchange Works: Advisor Refresher

Section 1031 permits a taxpayer to defer gain when real property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment is exchanged for like-kind real property held for the same purpose. The **2017** Tax Cuts and Jobs Act limited the provision to real property; personal property and intangibles ceased qualifying effective **2018**.

Most transactions are delayed, or Starker, exchanges. The client sells relinquished property, a qualified intermediary holds the proceeds under the §**1.1031(k)-1** safe harbor, the client identifies replacement property within **45** days, and acquires it within **180** days. Reverse and improvement, or build-to-suit, exchanges use the parking safe harbor in Rev. Proc. **2000-37**. The mechanical steps are simple enough to describe; the adviser’s job is to make sure the structure, records, and reporting preserve nonrecognition.

## Tax Treatment and Basis

A fully qualifying exchange defers realized gain, including unrecaptured §1250 gain—which can be taxed at up to **25%**—and exposure to the **3.8%** net investment income tax. The mechanism is substituted, or carryover, basis. Broadly, replacement-property basis equals relinquished-property basis, decreased by boot received and increased by gain recognized and additional consideration paid. The holding period tacks.

That basis carries the deferred gain forward. On an eventual taxable sale, it remains available for recognition, unless a §**1014** step-up at death changes the result. A low replacement basis also means lower future depreciation and greater gain on a later sale. It is tax deferral, not forgiveness.

## Depreciating the Replacement Property

Reg. §**1.168(i)-6** creates an issue that is easy to miss in a transaction memo. The **exchanged basis** of the replacement property generally continues to depreciate over the relinquished property’s remaining recovery period and method. **Excess basis**, meaning additional cash invested, is newly placed in service on a fresh schedule.

The taxpayer can elect out of that default and treat the entire basis as new property placed in service at the replacement acquisition. The choice can change near-term depreciation, so it deserves a short projection. It also needs to be coordinated with any [cost-segregation](cost-segregation.html) study on the replacement.

## Boot, Netting, and Partial Exchanges

Gain is recognized to the extent of **boot**—cash or non-like-kind property received—capped at realized gain. Cash boot is direct. Mortgage, or debt-relief, boot arises when liabilities assumed by the other party exceed liabilities taken on by the taxpayer. That debt relief can be offset by cash paid, but cash boot does not work in the reverse direction.

Net consideration carefully. Customary exchange expenses can generally be paid from proceeds without creating boot. Loan-acquisition costs, prorations, and certain credits may not receive that treatment. If boot causes recognized gain, recapture is generally triggered first.

For complete deferral, the client must trade equal-or-up in value, reinvest all equity, and replace debt. The client-facing [boot memo](1031-exchange-boot.html) explains the mechanics in more detail.

## Reporting: Form 8824 and the Return

Report the exchange on **Form 8824, Like-Kind Exchanges**, with the return for the year in which the relinquished property transferred. The form records identification and exchange dates, calculates realized and recognized gain, identifies boot, and calculates replacement-property basis. Recognized gain and recapture then flow to Schedule D or Form **4797**, as appropriate.

For an exchange that straddles a tax year—sale late in one year, replacement acquisition in the next—make sure the return is extended. Otherwise, the original filing due date can cut short the **180-day** period. Keep basis-computation workpapers. They can travel with the client for years and into a later exchange.

## Deadlines, Identification, and Elections

The **45-day** identification period and **180-day** completion period start together on the transfer date. They are calendar-day deadlines; weekends do not provide grace. They are extended only by §**7508A** disaster relief.

Identification must be written, signed, and delivered to the QI or another party to the exchange. It must satisfy the [three-property, **200%**, or **95%** identification rule](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html). The **180-day** period is also capped at the tax-return due date, including extensions. For a late-year sale, file the extension to preserve the full window. Put every client exchange on a deadline calendar. A missed date is common and difficult to repair.

## Recurring Traps to Flag

- **Constructive receipt.** Client access to proceeds outside the QI safe harbor disqualifies the exchange. Confirm the QI is engaged before closing and is not a disqualified person.
- **Related-party exchanges (§1031(f)).** Both parties generally must hold for two years. An early disposition by either may unwind deferral. Indirect related-party swaps using a QI are also scrutinized under Rev. Rul. **2002-83**.
- **Drop-and-swap / swap-and-drop.** Partnership and LLC distributions of TIC interests before or after an exchange create held-for-investment and timing questions. Document intent.
- **Vesting and the same-taxpayer rule.** The taxpayer that sells must acquire, disregarded entities aside. A mismatch breaks the exchange.
- **Primary residence and dealer property.** Address Section 121 interactions and the exclusion for property held primarily for sale.

## Client Due-Diligence Checklist

1. **Confirm eligibility.** The property must be held for investment or business, not as a residence or dealer inventory.
2. **Engage the QI before closing.** Review fund security, including segregated or qualified escrow and bonding.
3. **Model the alternative.** Calculate the tax on an outright sale: gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax. Clients should understand what is being deferred.
4. **Calendar the **45**/**180** deadlines.** Confirm an extension for a late-year sale.
5. **Plan the replacement.** Model value, debt, and equity to avoid boot. Identify backups; a quick-closing [DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) is a common fallback.
6. **Address state issues.** Review clawback, withholding, and nonresident filing requirements.
7. **Document basis and the depreciation election.** The replacement schedule deserves the same care as the closing mechanics.

## State and Multistate Considerations

Most states conform to Section 1031, but important exceptions remain. California and some other states use clawback regimes. They track deferred in-state gain when a client exchanges into out-of-state replacement property and later sells. California requires annual reporting on **FTB Form 3840** until the deferred gain is recognized.

States may also impose nonresident withholding on real-estate sales. Confirm conformity, certificates or exemptions, and composite or nonresident filing obligations in both the relinquished-property state and the replacement-property state.

## What Clients Should Know

The client should understand four points: a 1031 exchange defers rather than eliminates tax absent a step-up at death; its deadlines are rigid; sale proceeds cannot reach the client; and carryover basis can mean lower later depreciation and larger gain at a taxable sale.

For a client tired of property management, a [DST](dst-guide.html) can be a passive qualifying replacement. The eventual [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) path may also be relevant. But a replacement should be suitable because of its underlying economics, not simply because it closes before a deadline. The useful planning happens before listing, not after a property is under contract.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What form reports a 1031 exchange?

**Form 8824, Like-Kind Exchanges**, filed with the return for the year the relinquished property transferred. It calculates recognized gain to the extent of boot and replacement-property basis; recognized gain flows to Schedule D or Form **4797**.

### How is the replacement property’s basis determined?

It is substituted, or carryover, basis: relinquished-property basis decreased by boot received and increased by gain recognized and additional consideration paid. The holding period tacks.

### How is the replacement property depreciated?

Under Reg. §**1.168(i)-6**, exchanged basis generally continues over the relinquished property’s remaining recovery period, while excess basis is newly placed in service on a fresh schedule. The taxpayer may elect to treat all basis as newly placed in service.

### Does a 1031 exchange defer depreciation recapture?

Yes. A fully qualifying exchange defers unrecaptured Section 1250 gain along with capital gain. To the extent boot causes recognized gain, recapture is generally triggered first.

### What is the related-party rule for 1031 exchanges?

Section 1031(f) generally requires both related parties to hold their property for two years. An early disposition can unwind the deferral, and indirect QI-mediated swaps receive scrutiny.

### What state issue most often surprises clients?

Clawback. A state such as California can track deferred in-state gain after an out-of-state replacement acquisition and require annual **FTB Form 3840** reporting until gain is recognized. Nonresident withholding can also apply at sale.

## Glossary

- **Substituted (Carryover) Basis:** Replacement-property basis derived from relinquished-property basis, preserving deferred gain.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form for reporting a like-kind exchange and calculating recognized gain and replacement basis.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The §1.1031(k)-1 safe-harbor party that holds proceeds so the taxpayer avoids constructive receipt.
- **Unrecaptured §1250 Gain:** Depreciation-related gain potentially taxed at up to **25%**, deferred in a qualifying exchange.
- **§1031(f):** The related-party rules that impose a two-year holding requirement in relevant exchanges.
- **FTB Form 3840:** California’s annual report tracking deferred in-state gain exchanged into out-of-state property.
