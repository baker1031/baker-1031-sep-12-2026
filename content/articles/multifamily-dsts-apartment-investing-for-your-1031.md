# Multifamily DSTs: Apartment Investing for Your 1031

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about how quickly a familiar property type can feel safe by default. Apartments are familiar. That is useful, but familiarity is not the same thing as a margin of safety.

A multifamily Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) holds one or more income-producing apartment communities. Investors own fractional beneficial interests in the trust. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured DST interest is treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. That can allow an investor to defer capital-gains tax while stepping away from active landlording and into a passive, professionally managed interest in a larger, often institutional-quality apartment portfolio.

First-order thinking says, “Housing is needs based, so this must be dependable.” Second-order thinking asks what is happening in the specific metro and submarket: household formation, jobs, affordability, new construction, lease resets, operating expenses, debt terms, sponsor execution, and the investor's own liquidity needs. People need housing. That does not make every apartment community or DST suitable.

## Why multifamily DSTs are popular

Apartments are easy for many exchangers to understand. They are often more familiar than a net-lease drugstore or a [logistics warehouse](industrial-dsts-warehouse-and-logistics-investing.html). Many investors selling rental houses or apartments they once managed directly are also drawn to remaining in a property type they know while letting a professional sponsor take over leasing, maintenance, management, and the eventual sale.

The DST wrapper adds other attractions. Properly structured interests can be 1031-eligible like-kind real property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. The structure can offer passive ownership, the potential for rental income, diversification across many units and sometimes markets, relatively low minimums, and a fast close that can help meet the 45- and 180-day exchange deadlines. It can also help replace debt from a relinquished property.

Housing demand is broad and needs based. That is one reason multifamily has long been regarded as a core DST sector and why sponsors offer many apartment DSTs. Still, broad demand and a popular category do not guarantee an individual property's occupancy, rents, income, or eventual value.

## Apartment demand drivers

Apartment demand begins with household formation. When young adults move out, form households, or relocate for work, they create renters. The size of those age cohorts influences how many new renters enter a market. Job and population growth can reinforce the process: employment attracts residents and supports their ability to pay rent, which can help occupancy and rents in well-located communities.

Homeownership affordability is another important force. When home prices are high and mortgage rates are elevated, buying a first home is harder. More households may rent for longer, including people who would otherwise purchase. Owning and renting are substitutes to a degree, though that relationship changes with the housing cycle.

Shelter is a necessity rather than a discretionary purchase, so apartment demand can be more resilient than demand for some other property types when the economy softens. The operative word is “can.” These forces vary by market. Household formation, job and population growth, and homeownership affordability are a backdrop for underwriting—not a promise of rent growth or occupancy at any one asset.

## Risks: vacancy and short leases

The defining feature of apartment economics is the short lease. Most apartment leases run about a year. That lets owners reprice quickly when a market is strong. As leases roll, rents can move toward market levels and capture rising demand.

The same feature turns against the owner in a weak market. If demand softens or new supply arrives, rents can reset downward just as quickly. An apartment community has less of the long-term contractual rent protection common in net-lease property. Short leases create opportunity and exposure at the same time.

Vacancy has two useful measures. Physical vacancy is the share of units sitting empty. Economic vacancy is broader: it includes empty units, concessions such as free rent, below-market leases, bad debt, and other lost income even when units appear occupied. A property can look nearly full and still collect less income than its physical occupancy suggests.

New-supply risk connects these issues. When many new apartments are delivered into a submarket at once, competition can increase vacancy and concessions, pressuring rents at even a well-run community. An economic downturn can deepen the problem by slowing the household and job growth needed to absorb the new units. In a DST, the investor is passive, so the sponsor's market selection and management must carry that work.

## What to look for in a multifamily DST

Start with the market. Review the metro and submarket's job and population growth, the diversity of its employment base, and the supply pipeline. A growing, economically diverse market with limited new construction can support occupancy and rents better than a stagnant or oversupplied one. It is a tendency, not a guarantee.

Then assess the property: age, quality, renovations, amenities, and position relative to competing communities. Current occupancy and the recent trends in occupancy and rent provide a view of the asset's present operating position. Achieved rents compared with market rent can indicate both pricing power and remaining headroom.

Debt belongs near the top of the review, not at the back. Examine leverage, the loan-to-value ratio, interest rate, whether the rate is fixed or floating, and maturity. Highly leveraged or short-maturity debt can create a difficult situation if rates rise or refinancing becomes necessary in a weaker market.

Finally, scrutinize the sponsor. Look at its record across cycles and in multifamily specifically, its fee load, business plan, and projected distributions. Projections are estimates, never guarantees. A capable sponsor, a quality well-occupied property, conservative debt, and a healthy market are more meaningful than a headline projected yield.

## Key takeaways

- Market work comes first: job and population growth, employment diversity, and the new-supply pipeline influence occupancy and rent.
- Property quality, current occupancy, and rents relative to market help show pricing power and headroom.
- Debt deserves close attention: leverage, interest rate, fixed versus floating structure, and maturity can all add risk.
- The sponsor's multifamily experience, fees, and business plan matter. Projected distributions are estimates, never guarantees.

## Sample multifamily offerings

Multifamily DSTs come in several recognizable forms. A common example is a single larger garden-style or mid-rise community in a growing suburban or Sun Belt metro. It may be a newer property with modern amenities, intended to provide stable income with some potential for rent growth. Other offerings group two or more communities, sometimes in different markets, to spread occupancy and market risk.

Strategy also changes the risk profile. A stabilized, income-oriented offering may hold a fully leased, modern property with conservative debt for investors prioritizing steadier projected distributions. A value-add offering may plan to renovate units and raise rents. That can bring more potential upside, but it also adds execution risk and may mean more variable income early in the hold.

Leverage ranges from modest to higher, in part because exchangers often need to replace mortgage debt. The target hold period is commonly about five to seven years. After that, a sponsor may seek to sell or refinance and return capital. The timing depends on market conditions, property performance, and sponsor judgment; it is not guaranteed and could be earlier or later than projected.

These are general illustrations of common characteristics, not descriptions of a particular security or a promise of results. The practical step is matching the offering's income, leverage, execution, and liquidity profile to the investor's goals after a suitability review.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a multifamily DST?

A multifamily DST holds one or more income-producing apartment communities. Investors own fractional beneficial interests. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, properly structured interests are treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, allowing capital-gains tax deferral when the exchange is completed correctly. A professional sponsor handles leasing, maintenance, management, and sale, while investors receive their share of income and any appreciation. The structure can offer passive, fractional ownership of a larger community or several communities, with diversification across units and sometimes markets at a relatively low minimum.

### Why are multifamily DSTs so popular with 1031 investors?

Apartments are familiar, and many exchangers are selling rental property they once managed themselves. A multifamily DST lets them stay in a known property type while giving up active landlording. It also layers DST benefits onto housing: potential tax deferral, professional management, income potential, diversification, relatively low minimums, fast closing for exchange deadlines, and debt replacement. Broad housing demand and a wide set of offerings help explain the popularity, but none of that guarantees a particular property's outcome.

### What drives apartment demand?

Household formation is the core driver. Young adults forming households and people relocating create renters. Job and population growth can support occupancy and rent in well-located communities. Homeownership affordability matters too: high prices and mortgage rates can keep would-be buyers in rental housing longer. Shelter is a necessity, which can support demand in a softer economy, but local market conditions still decide the result.

### Why are short apartment leases both an advantage and a risk?

Most apartment leases are about a year. In a strong market, frequent renewal lets owners move rents toward market levels and capture improving demand. In a weak market or an oversupplied submarket, the same schedule lets rents reset downward quickly. This gives multifamily less contractual rent certainty than long-lease net-lease retail or industrial property. A passive DST investor depends on the sponsor to manage both the opportunity and the downside.

### What is the difference between physical and economic vacancy?

Physical vacancy is simply empty units with no rent-paying tenant. Economic vacancy measures lost income more fully: it includes empty units plus concessions, free rent, below-market leases, bad debt, and similar leakage. A community can have low physical vacancy while still reporting material economic vacancy. Both are important, but economic vacancy is the more complete view of income actually collected.

### What is new-supply risk in multifamily?

New-supply risk is the risk that developers deliver many apartments into a submarket at the same time. The new competition can increase vacancy, force concessions, and pressure rent across the area. Short leases can make the effect show up quickly as rents roll. The construction pipeline and the market's ability to absorb deliveries should be part of due diligence, especially when an economic slowdown could reduce household formation and job growth.

### How do I evaluate a multifamily DST?

Review the market's employment and population trends, employment diversity, and supply pipeline. Examine property age, quality, renovations, amenities, competitive position, occupancy, and rent trends. Compare achieved rents with market rents. Then assess the debt: leverage, interest rate, fixed or floating structure, and maturity. Review the sponsor's cycle and multifamily record, fees, business plan, and projections. The work is about weighing risk, not chasing the highest projected return.

### What hold period should I expect from a multifamily DST?

Most multifamily DSTs have a target hold period of roughly five to seven years, after which the sponsor may sell or refinance and return capital. That is a target, not a fixed term. The actual exit depends on market conditions, property performance, and sponsor judgment, and may occur earlier or later. DST interests are illiquid during the hold: there is no public market and investors generally cannot sell or redeem on demand. At sale, an investor can often pursue another 1031 exchange or cash out and pay the deferred tax then.

### Are multifamily DST distributions guaranteed?

No. Sponsor income figures are projections based on assumptions about occupancy, rents, expenses, and financing. Occupancy can fall, rents can soften, expenses and interest costs can rise, and supply or a downturn can pressure the property. Income may decline and distributions may be reduced or suspended. Annual lease resets make apartment income more variable than income from a long-net-lease asset. Higher leverage and floating-rate or short-maturity debt can add stress if rates rise or refinancing is needed. Allocation size should reflect that uncertainty; past performance does not guarantee future results.

### What types of multifamily DST offerings are available?

Offerings may hold a single garden-style or mid-rise community or several communities across markets. They can be newer, amenity-rich properties in growing suburban or Sun Belt metros. A stabilized, fully leased, conservatively financed offering is usually designed for steadier projected income. A value-add plan seeks to renovate and raise rents, trading more potential upside for greater execution risk and potentially more variable early income. Leverage can be modest or higher to support exchange debt replacement. These are illustrative descriptions, not specific securities or guaranteed returns.

### Can I use a multifamily DST as 1031 replacement property?

Yes, if the DST is properly structured and the exchange is executed correctly. IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a fractional beneficial interest in a qualifying DST as a direct real-estate interest for 1031 purposes. An investor can sell relinquished investment property, identify the DST within the 45-day identification window, and close within the 180-day exchange period. DST financing is typically non-recourse at the trust level and can help replace relinquished debt. A qualified intermediary holds exchange proceeds. Because DST interests are securities, access is through a broker-dealer for accredited investors after suitability review.

### How much leverage do multifamily DSTs use?

Leverage varies by offering. Some sponsors use modest debt for a more defensive profile and steadier projected income. Others use more debt because a 1031 exchange generally requires replacement of both equity and debt to fully defer tax. Terms matter as much as amount. A moderate fixed-rate loan with a long maturity is generally lower risk than a highly leveraged, floating-rate, or short-maturity loan that could strain cash flow in a difficult refinancing market. Trust-level debt is non-recourse to the investor, but it still changes the risk and return of the investment.

### How does multifamily compare to other DST sectors?

Multifamily stands apart through short, roughly annual leases and broad needs-based housing demand. Its leases reset more often than the long leases common in net-lease retail, industrial, and medical office, giving more responsiveness to the market but less contractual rent certainty. Its demand profile is different from industrial, which is tied to e-commerce and logistics, senior housing, which is tied to aging demographics and operator quality, and medical office, which has healthcare demand but reimbursement exposure. Each sector has risks: multifamily has oversupply and vacancy risk; industrial has tenant concentration; senior housing has operator risk; medical office has reimbursement risk.

### Is a multifamily DST a good fit for a conservative investor?

It can be, depending on the offering and investor goals, but no DST is risk free. A more risk-averse multifamily profile might be stabilized, well occupied, income oriented, in a growing and diversified market with limited new supply, managed by an experienced sponsor, and financed with conservative fixed-rate debt at a sensible maturity. That profile can aim for steadier projected income and lower execution risk than a value-add or highly leveraged alternative. It still carries lease-reset, vacancy, supply, illiquidity, and non-guaranteed-distribution risk. Suitability—not the label “conservative”—determines whether it fits.

### What are the main risks of a multifamily DST?

The risks include rent-reset risk from short leases; physical and economic vacancy; new supply; market and economic weakness; sponsor execution; financing and leverage; floating-rate or short-maturity debt; illiquidity during the multi-year hold; and fees and load that reduce net returns. The investor is passive, so sponsor decisions matter. Distributions are projections, not guarantees, which is why suitability is important.

## Glossary

**Multifamily DST.** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding income-producing apartment communities.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST).** A trust whose fractional interests can be 1031-eligible like-kind real property.

**Beneficial Interest.** An investor's fractional ownership share in a DST.

**1031 Exchange.** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**IRS Rev. Rul. 2004-86.** The ruling that makes qualifying DST interests 1031-eligible real property.

**Household Formation.** New households forming, a core driver of rental demand.

**Homeownership Affordability.** How attainable buying is; lower affordability can support renting.

**Physical Vacancy.** The share of apartment units sitting empty.

**Economic Vacancy.** Income lost to concessions, bad debt, and below-market rents.

**New-Supply Risk.** The risk that new construction pressures rents and occupancy.

**Master Lease.** A lease structure some DSTs use to manage the property.

**Value-Add.** A plan to renovate and raise rents for upside, with associated risk.

**Loan-to-Value (LTV).** The ratio of debt to property value, a measure of leverage.

**Hold Period.** The roughly five-to-seven-year expected DST ownership term.

**Sponsor.** The firm that structures and manages the DST and its property.

**Accredited Investor.** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DST securities.
