# DSTs for 1031 Exchanges Under $500K

I keep seeing the same constraint in smaller exchanges: the sale is real, the 45-day clock is real, and the choices suddenly look much worse than they did before the property went under contract. A single rental house, duplex, or modest commercial unit can generate proceeds well under $500,000. That amount can still carry a meaningful capital-gains tax bill, but it may not buy the kind of replacement property the owner actually wants.

First-order thinking says the smaller exchanger should simply buy another small property. Second-order thinking asks what that purchase preserves: another tenant list, repairs, financing work, and a single concentrated asset—possibly selected because it was available before the deadline rather than because it was compelling. Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs) can change the set of choices, but they do not remove the need to assess price, liquidity, fees, debt, suitability, and the tax rules with qualified professionals.

## The Small-Exchange Challenge

An investor selling for $300,000 or $400,000 may want to defer capital-gains tax through a 1031 exchange into like-kind real estate. The difficulty is that institutional-grade multifamily, industrial, and net-lease property commonly trades for millions of dollars. A modest exchange often cannot purchase those assets outright.

The direct alternative can be another small rental or small commercial unit. That can preserve control, but it can also preserve active landlord work: tenants, repairs, financing, vacancies, and concentration in one asset, one market, and one tenant base. The other alternative is to search until the [45-day identification](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) deadline forces a decision. The exchange deadlines are unforgiving: identify replacement property within **45 days** and close within **180 days**. Without a ready, affordable, suitable replacement, an exchanger may settle for a weaker property or fail the exchange and face tax.

The issue is not that a $300,000 or $400,000 exchange is too small to matter. It is that the available choices may be too narrow. That is the problem a fractional structure is intended to address.

## Low DST Minimums

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) pools capital from many investors to acquire real estate. Rather than fund the whole property, an investor purchases a fractional beneficial interest sized to the investment. DST minimums are often around $100,000 or less, although minimums vary by sponsor and offering.

That can allow a $300,000-$400,000 exchanger to invest in one or more DSTs. The investor can own a fractional interest in professionally managed multifamily, industrial, or net-lease real estate that would be out of reach in a direct purchase. Under IRS Revenue Ruling **2004-86**, a qualifying DST interest is treated as like-kind real property for 1031 purposes. The tax treatment and the precise structure should be confirmed with a CPA.

> A $300,000 exchange cannot buy a professionally managed apartment complex outright. It can buy a fractional interest in one, which is what low DST minimums make possible.

Low minimums are an access mechanism, not a free benefit. The offering documents still determine the property, leverage, fee structure, projected distributions, hold period, and risks.

## Accessing Institutional Property

For a smaller exchanger, the value of the structure is not merely getting across the 1031 finish line. It can be access to larger real estate: Class A apartment communities, modern logistics warehouses leased to creditworthy tenants, and single-tenant net-lease property with long leases and national operators. These assets are selected, financed, and operated by experienced real-estate sponsors.

Through a DST, the investor owns a fractional stake while the sponsor handles leasing, maintenance, financing, reporting, and the eventual sale. That is a change from active ownership to passive ownership. It also commonly gives the exchanger access to property-level non-recourse financing already arranged by the sponsor. In a 1031 exchange, that financing may help address the debt-replacement requirement without the investor qualifying for or personally guaranteeing a new loan.

None of that means an institutional label guarantees a result. Location, tenants, financing, sponsor execution, and real-estate conditions still matter. A passive investor is relying on someone else to make the decisions.

## Diversifying a Modest Exchange

Low minimums can also make diversification practical. A $400,000 exchange might be allocated across three or four DSTs, often at about $100,000 each, rather than concentrated in one replacement property. The mix can span multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, different geographic markets, and different sponsors.

With a direct small-property purchase, all capital and risk reside in one building, one market, and one tenant base. A diversified group of DSTs does not eliminate risk, but it can make no single property the entire outcome. If one property underperforms, the others may offset some of the impact.

The 1031 identification rules matter here. The **3-property rule** permits identification of up to **three** replacement properties regardless of value. That is often enough for a diversified small-exchange set. If more properties are needed, the **200% rule** can allow any number of properties if their total value does not exceed **200%** of the relinquished property’s value. The **95% rule** permits any number but requires acquisition of at least **95%** of the identified value.

> The investor who once had to select one small rental may be able to spread a modest exchange across several institutional properties. The benefit is diversification, not immunity from loss.

### Key takeaways

- Smaller exchanges can be hard because modest proceeds may not buy quality institutional real estate, creating a subpar-property or deadline risk.
- DST minimums are often around $100,000 or less, allowing a fractional interest that can qualify as replacement property.
- A DST can provide professionally managed, passive ownership and sponsor-arranged non-recourse financing.
- Several DSTs can spread a modest exchange across sectors, markets, and sponsors instead of concentrating everything in one property.

## Getting Started

Preparation begins before the relinquished property sale closes. Engage a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) (QI) before the sale. The QI must hold the proceeds; if the exchanger takes actual or constructive receipt of them, the exchange can be disqualified.

Also confirm [accredited-investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html) status with the broker-dealer. DST interests are Regulation D securities available to accredited investors after a suitability review. The review considers whether the investment fits the investor’s financial situation, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance.

After the sale, identify suitable DSTs within **45 days** and close within **180 days**. A DST can generally close in days because the sponsor has already acquired and structured the property and financing; a direct acquisition may require weeks or months of negotiation, due diligence, financing, and escrow. Work with the broker-dealer to consider income, growth, sector, market, budget, debt replacement, and identification rules. The QI then funds the subscription from exchange proceeds. A CPA should confirm tax treatment and that equity and debt are properly replaced.

## Weighing the Trade-Offs

DSTs are not a way to retain every benefit of direct ownership. They are illiquid. An investor generally remains invested until the sponsor sells the underlying property, often after a **five-to-seven-year** hold, with little or no secondary market. That makes a DST unsuitable for capital that may be needed in the near or medium term.

Distributions are projections, not guarantees, and depend on property performance. Sponsor and offering fees affect net returns. DSTs are available only to accredited investors after suitability review. The investor gives up control over property decisions and the timing of the exit; the sponsor makes those decisions.

The first-order appeal is straightforward: access better property, become passive, diversify, and meet a strict deadline. The second-order question is whether those benefits justify illiquidity, projected-not-guaranteed income, fees, accredited-only access, and loss of control for this particular investor. A DST can be suitable for an accredited small exchanger, but it is not automatically preferable to a direct property or another strategy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are smaller 1031 exchanges harder than larger ones?

Modest proceeds—often $300,000 or $400,000—cannot generally buy million-dollar institutional assets outright. The exchanger may have to choose between another active, concentrated small property and a scramble to identify a suitable replacement within 45 days. DST minimums can provide fractional access to institutional property, but an investor should confirm the tax details with a CPA.

### What are typical DST minimums?

Minimums are often around $100,000, sometimes less, depending on the offering. A $300,000 or $400,000 exchange may therefore fit one or more DSTs. The stated minimum is not the whole analysis: the proceeds and debt that must be replaced for deferral, along with each offering’s terms, matter too.

### Can a $300,000 exchange really access institutional-grade property?

Yes, through a fractional beneficial interest. A DST can pool capital into an apartment community, logistics warehouse, or net-lease property that a $300,000 investor could not buy alone. A qualifying DST interest can be like-kind replacement property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. That does not eliminate the need for accredited status, suitability, and CPA confirmation.

### Can I diversify a small exchange across multiple DSTs?

Yes. A $400,000 exchange could be split among three or four DSTs across sectors, markets, and sponsors. The 3-property rule frequently supports three DST identifications regardless of value. More identifications may require use of the 200% or 95% rule. Diversification reduces concentration; it does not guarantee a result.

### How do the 1031 identification rules apply to a small exchange?

They apply the same way as they do to a larger exchange. Within 45 days, identify under the 3-property rule, the 200% rule, or the 95% rule, then close within 180 days. Coordinate the formal identification with the QI, broker-dealer, and CPA rather than relying on an informal list.

### Do I need to be an accredited investor to use a DST for a small exchange?

Generally, yes. DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors after suitability review, regardless of whether the exchange is $300,000 or several million dollars. If an investor is not accredited, a DST generally is unavailable and another replacement strategy is needed. Verify early, before the sale starts the clock.

### How fast can a DST close compared with buying a property?

A DST can typically close in days after identification and completed subscription paperwork because the property and financing are already in place. A direct purchase may take weeks or months of negotiation, diligence, financing, and escrow. That speed can help with the 45-day and 180-day deadlines, but timing should still be coordinated with the QI and broker-dealer.

### What are the trade-offs of using a DST for a small exchange?

They include illiquidity for a typical five-to-seven-year hold, little or no secondary market, projected rather than guaranteed distributions, fees, accredited-only eligibility, and loss of control over property and sale decisions. These costs must be weighed against passive ownership, potential quality, diversification, and deadline flexibility.

### Is a DST better than buying a small property outright for a 1031?

It depends on the owner’s goals. Direct ownership can provide control and liquidity but keeps active management and concentration. A DST can provide passive, professionally managed fractional ownership, diversification, and quick closing, but requires accepting fees, illiquidity, projected-not-guaranteed income, and sponsor control. Suitability—not a generic ranking—decides the answer.

### Can a DST handle the debt on my relinquished property?

Many DSTs have non-recourse financing in place. A leveraged DST can provide replacement debt without requiring the investor to qualify for or personally guarantee a loan. To fully defer gain, an exchanger generally needs to replace relinquished debt or contribute additional cash. A CPA should model the required amount and the broker-dealer should assess suitable offerings.

### What kinds of properties do DSTs hold for small exchangers?

Common sectors include multifamily, industrial and logistics, net-lease retail, healthcare such as medical office and senior housing, and self-storage. A small investor can hold a fractional interest in these assets. Specific availability changes with sponsor offerings, so property quality and fit require current review.

### How much of my exchange should go into DSTs?

The amount depends on proceeds, debt replacement, whether full or partial deferral is desired, liquidity needs, and the broader portfolio. Some investors place the full exchange in DSTs. Others combine DSTs with a direct property, or take taxable cash—called boot—and exchange the rest. Plan the allocation before sale with a CPA and broker-dealer.

### What happens at the end of a DST’s hold for a small investor?

At the end of the defined hold, commonly five to seven years, the sponsor sells and the investor receives a share of proceeds. The investor may take cash and pay deferred capital-gains tax then due, complete another 1031 exchange into a DST or other like-kind property, or, if offered, potentially move to a REIT through a **721**/UPREIT exchange with operating-partnership units. That future decision needs planning because another exchange has its own deadlines.

### Do small DST investments carry higher relative fees?

Generally, a small investor pays the same percentage fee structure as a large investor in the same offering. Sponsor, offering, organizational, and asset-management fees still reduce net returns, and several DST investments can create several fee loads. Review each offering document and compare fees against diversification, property, debt, and projected net income.

## Glossary

- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.
- **Fractional Beneficial Interest:** An undivided share of a DST’s real estate sized to the investor.
- **DST Minimum:** The smallest investment a DST accepts, often around $100,000 or less.
- **Institutional-Grade Property:** Large, professionally managed, well-located real estate.
- **Relinquished Property:** The property sold to start a 1031 exchange.
- **Replacement Property:** The like-kind real estate, such as a DST, acquired in the exchange.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds proceeds and facilitates a 1031 exchange.
- **45-Day Identification Period:** The window to identify replacement property after a sale.
- **180-Day Closing Period:** The window to close on replacement property in an exchange.
- **3-Property Rule:** Identify up to three replacement properties regardless of value.
- **200% Rule:** Identify any number of properties up to 200% of relinquished value.
- **95% Rule:** Identify any number but acquire at least 95% of identified value.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** DST financing not personally guaranteed by the investor.
- **Diversification:** Spreading an exchange across multiple DSTs, sectors, and markets.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Regulation D offerings.
