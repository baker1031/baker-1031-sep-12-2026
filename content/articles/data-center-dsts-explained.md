# Data Center DSTs Explained

I keep seeing data-center exposure move from a specialist topic to a standard 1031 question. The attraction is easy to describe: cloud computing and artificial intelligence need physical capacity, and real estate that supports it can look like a clean way to own an important part of that buildout.

But a good story is not the same as a good investment. A data center holds the servers, storage, and networking equipment behind the internet, cloud computing, and AI. A data center DST holds one or more of those facilities, leases them to cloud providers, enterprises, or colocation operators, and lets accredited investors hold fractional beneficial interests that can qualify as like-kind real property in a 1031 exchange under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86.

First-order thinking is “cloud and AI are growing, so data centers must be attractive.” Second-order thinking asks whether this facility has enough reliable power, the right cooling, a tenant base that can be underwritten, acceptable concentration, a capable sponsor, workable debt and fees, and an exit that makes sense. The sector's demand story can be real while a particular offering still carries concentrated risk.

This is educational information, not investment, tax, or legal advice. DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after a suitability review, and projected distributions and returns are never guaranteed. If the structure itself is new, start with [the definitive DST guide](dst-guide.html).

## Why data centers are in demand

Data centers sit behind two long-running shifts. First, enterprises increasingly rent computing capacity from large providers instead of operating their own server rooms; that capacity lives in data centers. Second, AI training and inference are highly compute-intensive and require specialized, high-power facilities. These are structural trends, not simply a short cycle.

Historically, those trends have drawn hyperscale cloud providers, large enterprises, and colocation operators into the sector. A data center can be core infrastructure for a tenant, and moving servers is costly and disruptive. In the right market, that can support long leases and sticky tenants.

The qualification matters. Demand and rent observations are general, non-promissory observations. Conditions change. Neither occupancy nor a return is guaranteed for a particular DST. The appeal is passive exposure to an infrastructure-like real-estate sector with secular tailwinds; the work is deciding whether the asset, tenant, and financing support that appeal.

## How data center DSTs work

A data center DST uses the same broad mechanics as other Delaware Statutory Trusts. The trust holds title to one or more income-producing data-center facilities. A sponsor structures the offering, arranges financing where applicable, and engages property and asset management. [Accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html) purchase fractional beneficial interests, which may be treated as like-kind real property for a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) under Rev. Rul. 2004-86. An investor selling other investment real estate may therefore exchange into a suitable DST, defer capital-gains tax, and take passive exposure to the sector.

After the investment, the trust receives tenant rent, pays operating expenses and debt service, and distributes net cash flow—typically monthly or quarterly—to investors. The ordinary DST constraints still apply: trustee restrictions often called the “seven deadly sins,” a roughly **five-to-seven-year** anticipated hold, illiquidity, and fees.

What changes is the asset. Data centers are specialized and capital-intensive. Sponsor experience with the property type, the facilities' condition, and tenant quality carry more weight than they might in a simpler sector. These offerings are less common than core DST sectors and are available only when a sponsor brings an appropriate offering to market.

## Tenant credit and lease length

Income depends heavily on who rents the facility and how long they are committed. Data-center tenants often include hyperscale cloud providers, large enterprises, and established colocation operators. They can sign long leases—sometimes a decade or more—and strong credit with substantial remaining term can support a more predictable income profile through an economic cycle.

That is not a one-sided advantage. A facility may receive most of its rent from one tenant or only a few. If a major tenant defaults, does not renew, or encounters financial stress, the effect on cash flow can be disproportionate. When a long lease expires, re-leasing a highly specialized facility can be difficult, especially if a new tenant needs different power density, cooling, or configuration.

Tenant credit, lease length, and tenant concentration must be evaluated as one set of facts. Long contracts and sound credit can provide visibility. They do not make a distribution a promise.

## Specialized risks to consider

Data-center DSTs have the ordinary DST risks—illiquidity, fees, trustee limits, and no assured result—plus asset-specific risks.

- **Power and cooling intensity.** Servers use substantial electricity and generate heat. Reliable, affordable power and effective energy management are central to both competition and operating cost. A power-constrained, high-cost, or aging facility can be disadvantaged in ways that a simpler building is not.
- **Capital expenditure.** These facilities are expensive to build and maintain. They can require continued investment to keep pace with tenant needs and technology.
- **Tenant concentration.** A few large tenants can drive a large share of rent. A departure or default can affect income materially.
- **Technological obsolescence.** Data-center specifications evolve. A building designed for one computing generation may need costly upgrades or become less competitive as power, cooling, and configuration needs change.

Those risks do not automatically rule out the sector. They do raise the required standard for sponsor expertise, facility quality, and underwriting. The DST wrapper does not remove any of them.

### Four facts to retain while assessing the sector

- Data center DSTs own facilities leased to cloud, enterprise, or colocation tenants; demand is linked to cloud and AI growth.
- Income commonly rests on a few large, creditworthy tenants under long leases. That can be stable, but it also creates concentration risk.
- Power and cooling intensity, capital expenditure, concentration, and technological obsolescence are specialized risks.
- Availability is limited because the sector is specialized. Sponsor expertise and facility quality matter heavily.

## Availability: where data center DSTs are offered

Data center DSTs appear less often than core sectors such as net-lease retail, [multifamily](multifamily-dsts-apartment-investing-for-your-1031.html), or industrial. Fewer sponsors have the expertise to acquire, operate, and structure data-center real estate, and facilities with the desired leases, tenants, and infrastructure are not always available to package into an offering. Treat the sector as available “where available,” not as something that can be sourced on demand.

That constraint is practical in a 1031 exchange. Identification is due within **45 days**, and closing is due within **180 days**. An investor who specifically wants data-center exposure should plan before the sale and ask the broker-dealer what is actually available inside the exchange window. A suitable data-center offering may be available; the better answer might instead be [a different sector DST, a diversified offering, or a combination](1031-exchange-replacement-property-options-compared.html). Flexibility is part of risk management because a specialized sector cannot be assumed to be available at a particular moment.

## Evaluating a data center DST

Start with the tenants.

1. **Tenant credit.** Assess the financial strength of the cloud provider, enterprise, or colocation operator.
2. **Tenant concentration.** Measure how much rent depends on the largest tenant or two.
3. **Lease length.** Review remaining term, the expiration calendar, and the implications of renewal or re-leasing.
4. **Facility and market.** Consider infrastructure quality and age, reliable and affordable power, cooling and energy costs, and the location's support for data-center demand.
5. **Sponsor expertise.** Confirm specific operating experience in data-center real estate, not merely general DST experience.
6. **Ordinary DST terms.** Review debt—including the amount and terms of any non-recourse financing—fees, projected hold period, and projected distributions.

Strong credit, manageable concentration, and long remaining leases may support a more predictable income profile. They do not guarantee it. Projected distributions are estimates. Because these interests are securities, a broker-dealer's suitability review considers financial condition, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance to determine whether the offering fits the investor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a data center DST?

It is a Delaware Statutory Trust that owns income-producing data-center facilities leased to cloud providers, enterprises, or colocation operators. Accredited investors hold fractional beneficial interests that may qualify as like-kind property under Rev. Rul. 2004-86, allowing a 1031 investor to defer capital-gains tax while gaining passive sector exposure. The trust collects rent, pays expenses and debt service, and distributes net cash flow. It remains illiquid, fee-bearing, trustee-restricted, and generally has a **five-to-seven-year** hold horizon. Availability varies.

### Why are data centers in demand?

Cloud migration moves enterprise computing capacity from private server rooms to large providers, and AI adds compute-intensive demand for high-power facilities. Those structural trends have supported tenant interest and long leases in some markets because moving infrastructure is expensive and disruptive. They do not guarantee occupancy, rent, distributions, or returns.

### How do data center DSTs work?

The trust owns the property; the sponsor structures the offering, financing, and management; and accredited investors buy 1031-eligible fractional interests. The DST receives rent, pays expenses and debt service, and typically distributes net cash flow monthly or quarterly. Trustee restrictions, illiquidity, fees, and a **five-to-seven-year** hold apply. The sector's capital intensity makes sponsor, tenant, and facility quality especially important.

### Who are the tenants in a data center DST?

They are commonly hyperscale cloud providers, enterprises with substantial computing needs, or colocation operators that lease space to many customers. Because the facility can be essential and hard to move, such tenants may be sticky and commit to long leases. Their credit quality is central, but reliance on one or a few tenants also creates concentration risk.

### Are data center DST leases long?

They can be—sometimes a decade or more—because moving the infrastructure is costly and disruptive. Long leases with creditworthy tenants can provide income visibility. At expiry, however, a specialized building can be difficult to re-lease if a new tenant requires different power, cooling, or configuration. A long lease does not assure a particular distribution or return.

### What is tenant concentration risk in a data center DST?

It is the risk that much of the DST's rental income depends on one tenant or a small group. A default, financial problem, or non-renewal can then have a much larger effect than it would in a property with many smaller tenants. Review the rent share of the largest tenant or two alongside credit, lease term, and re-leasing prospects. The DST structure does not eliminate this risk.

### What are the specialized risks of data center DSTs?

They include power and cooling needs, continuing capital expenditure, tenant concentration, and technological obsolescence, in addition to ordinary DST illiquidity, fees, and trustee restrictions. A power-constrained or costly market, one tenant's departure, unexpected capital needs, or a facility that cannot meet future specifications can weaken income. These risks require specialized sponsor expertise and do not guarantee any outcome.

### Why are data center DSTs less common than other sectors?

Data centers are specialized and capital-intensive. Fewer sponsors have the necessary experience, and suitable facilities with the right tenants, leases, and infrastructure are not always available. For a 1031 exchange, this matters because **45-day** identification and **180-day** closing deadlines are strict. Plan ahead, confirm live availability with the broker-dealer, and remain flexible if another option fits better.

### Can I use a data center DST in a 1031 exchange?

Yes, where a suitable offering is available. Under Rev. Rul. 2004-86, a properly structured DST beneficial interest is treated as like-kind real property. The investor must use a qualified intermediary, identify replacement property within **45 days**, close within **180 days**, reinvest proceeds, and replace debt to seek full deferral. DST interests are securities for accredited investors after a suitability review. Confirm exchange eligibility and mechanics with the investor's tax advisor and qualified intermediary.

### What is technological obsolescence risk in data centers?

It is the possibility that a facility becomes less competitive or needs expensive upgrades as computing changes. Power density, cooling capacity, and configuration are designed for a particular era. New workloads, including AI training, can require far more power and cooling than conventional computing. Facility age, quality, and the sponsor's plan to maintain competitiveness therefore matter.

### Are distributions from a data center DST guaranteed?

No. Offering distribution figures are projections based on rent, occupancy, expenses, and financing. Tenant non-renewal or default, energy-cost increases, capital needs, power and cooling costs, or technological obsolescence can reduce cash flow. Past performance does not guarantee future results. Size an investment for the possibility that income varies or is interrupted.

### How do I evaluate a data center DST?

Evaluate tenant credit, concentration, and lease length first. Then assess facility condition and age, power and cooling, energy costs, and market strength. Review the sponsor's data-center experience, debt and non-recourse financing terms, fees, projected hold period, and projected distributions. Complete the broker-dealer suitability review; it is the process for deciding whether an illiquid specialized DST fits the investor.

### Why does power matter so much for data center DSTs?

Servers consume substantial electricity and create heat, so cooling is also energy intensive. Reliable, affordable power is consequently a core element of a facility's competitiveness and cost profile. Constrained capacity, an unreliable grid, or high energy costs can create operating and competitive pressure. AI workloads make power and cooling even more important. Review the facility's location, power arrangements, and ability to manage those costs.

### Who can invest in a data center DST?

These are Regulation D securities generally offered to accredited investors. Accreditation can arise from the applicable income or net-worth tests, often based on income for the previous two years or net worth excluding a primary residence, or from certain professional credentials. A broker-dealer also performs a suitability review of financial position, objectives, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. Data-center DSTs are not offered to the general public.

## Glossary

- **Data Center DST:** A DST owning data-center facilities leased to cloud or enterprise tenants.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in 1031-eligible fractional beneficial interests.
- **Hyperscale Tenant:** A large cloud provider operating major-scale data-center capacity.
- **Colocation Operator:** A tenant that leases data-center space to many customers.
- **Beneficial Interest:** A DST investor's fractional ownership treated as real property.
- **Rev Rul 2004-86:** The IRS ruling that makes eligible DST interests like-kind property for 1031 purposes.
- **Tenant Credit:** The financial strength of a data center's tenants.
- **Tenant Concentration:** Reliance on one or a few tenants for most rent.
- **Lease Length:** The period during which a data-center tenant is contracted to pay rent.
- **Power Intensity:** A data center's heavy electricity requirement for servers and cooling.
- **Capital Expenditure:** Continuing investment needed to build and maintain a facility.
- **Technological Obsolescence:** The risk that a facility becomes outdated as computing needs change.
- **Cloud Computing:** Renting computing capacity housed in data centers.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** Financing repaid only from the property, not from investors personally.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting the applicable income, net-worth, or credential standards for a Regulation D offering.
- **Suitability Review:** A broker-dealer's assessment of whether a DST fits the investor.
