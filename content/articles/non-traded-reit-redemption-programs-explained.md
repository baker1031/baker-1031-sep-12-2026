# Non-Traded REIT Redemption Programs Explained

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing a redemption feature treated as a promise of liquidity. It is not. A request to redeem is a request made under a set of limits, and those limits matter most when an investor is under pressure to use them.

A non-traded REIT’s redemption program is the main route to capital before a liquidity event. This guide explains how it works, common quarterly and annual caps, when requests are gated or suspended, how redeemed shares are priced, and how to plan liquidity around an illiquid allocation.

First-order thinking is “there is a redemption program, so I can get out.” Second-order thinking asks how much can be redeemed, when the request window occurs, which NAV applies, whether an early-exit discount applies, and what happens if market stress produces more requests than the program can satisfy. ## How Redemption Programs Work

A redemption program, also called a share repurchase program, is a non-traded REIT’s periodic, rules-based method of offering limited liquidity before a [final liquidity event (like a listing or sale of the portfolio)](non-traded-reit-liquidity-events-ipo-merger.html). Because the shares are not exchange-listed, an investor cannot sell into an open market. Instead, the investor submits a request and the REIT repurchases shares under its program.

Programs often use monthly or quarterly submission and processing windows. The prospectus and plan state the redemption frequency, caps, price, holding-period rules, early-redemption discounts, and the board’s power to change the program. This is a contractual feature with terms that vary by REIT, not an open market. Reading those terms is the central step in understanding when and whether capital can be returned.

## Quarterly Caps and Gating

The central limitation is the cap. A REIT cannot meet unlimited redemptions without potentially selling illiquid real estate at disadvantageous prices. A common program limits repurchases to roughly **5%** of NAV per quarter and about **20%** of NAV per year in aggregate, although the actual terms vary by REIT.

When requests exceed the cap, the program is gated: it prorates requests. An investor receives only a portion, while the balance stays invested and may have to be requested again in a later period. Gating is an intentional protection for the REIT and remaining shareholders against a forced sale. It is not, by itself, a malfunction. For the individual investor, however, it means even an open program does not assure full liquidity.

## When Redemptions Get Suspended

The board generally can reduce, modify, gate, or fully suspend redemption activity. That is a live risk, not merely a theoretical contractual clause. In market stress, many investors may seek cash at the same time that selling properties would be costly. A board can halt redemptions to avoid a forced sale and protect remaining shareholders. The result can be capital locked up until the program is reopened, for an uncertain period.

This is one of the [most important risks to understand about non-traded REITs](non-traded-reit-liquidity-fees-and-risks.html). Liquidity is often most constrained in the downturn when an investor wants it most. Caps, gating, and suspension authority make redemptions discretionary and limited, rather than a right to exit on demand.

## Pricing of Redeemed Shares

When a REIT does repurchase shares, it generally uses periodic NAV rather than an exchange price. NAV is usually calculated monthly or quarterly through appraisals and valuation methods, and a program will specify whether the applicable price is the most recent NAV or the NAV at the time of processing. It is an estimate of per-share underlying value, not a live market quote.

Programs can also apply an early-redemption discount in the first year or two, sometimes on a schedule that declines after a holding period. Some impose a holding-period restriction before eligibility. These rules discourage short-term exits and can compensate the REIT and remaining shareholders for costs associated with early withdrawals. The redemption value depends on the applicable NAV, holding period, and the plan’s discount rules.

## Key Takeaways

- A redemption program is a non-traded REIT’s primary liquidity feature: a periodic, rules-based way to request that the REIT buy back shares.
- Repurchases are commonly capped at roughly **5%** of NAV per quarter and about **20%** per year; requests exceeding a cap can be gated and prorated.
- The board can reduce or fully suspend redemptions, and has done so in market stress. Liquidity is limited and not guaranteed.
- Redeemed shares are generally priced at periodic NAV, often with an early-redemption discount in the first year or two. Plan liquidity before investing.

## Reading the Redemption Terms

The phrase “there is a redemption program” says very little. The prospectus and share repurchase plan determine actual liquidity: monthly or quarterly frequency, submission windows, quarterly and annual caps, holding-period requirements, early-redemption discounts and phase-outs, NAV pricing methodology, and the board’s authority to amend, gate, suspend, or terminate the program.

Pay particular attention to board discretion. Programs commonly give the board broad authority, sometimes with limited notice. That authority is the basis for gating and suspension. A financial professional can help interpret the terms in relation to a particular liquidity need, but the investor should understand that the feature is conditional, not a contractual assurance that a full exit will be available on demand.

## Planning Your Liquidity

Treat a non-traded REIT as long-term, illiquid capital. Invest only money that can remain committed for years and through a full holding period without relying on a redemption request for early access. If there is a real chance the capital will be needed, the structure is likely not the right place for it.

Size the allocation so illiquid holdings do not become over-concentrated. Maintain separate liquid reserves for emergencies and near-term obligations. If the position is in a traditional IRA, make sure required minimum distributions can be met without depending on a REIT redemption. The goal is simple: a gate or suspension should be an inconvenience, not a crisis.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a non-traded REIT redemption program?

It is also called a share repurchase program. It lets a non-traded REIT offer limited periodic liquidity before a listing or portfolio sale. An investor submits a request; the REIT may repurchase shares under terms in the offering documents. Monthly or quarterly schedules are common. The plan sets frequency, caps, NAV pricing, holding-period and early-discount rules, and the board’s ability to modify or suspend the program. It is a rules-based liquidity feature, not an open market.

### How much can I redeem from a non-traded REIT each quarter?

The specific terms control, but a common cap is roughly **5%** of NAV per quarter and around **20%** per year in aggregate. The REIT limits redemptions so it does not have to sell real estate at unfavorable prices. If requests exceed the cap, the program gates them and prorates each request. An investor may receive only a portion and may be unable to redeem a full position quickly. Check the offering documents.

### Can a non-traded REIT stop redemptions?

Yes. The board generally may reduce, modify, or fully suspend the program, and this has occurred in practice during market stress. When redemptions are suspended, investors cannot redeem until the board reopens the program; capital can remain locked up for an extended and uncertain period. That is often the same moment investors most want liquidity. Treat the program as discretionary, not guaranteed.

### What does it mean for a redemption program to be gated?

Gating occurs when requests exceed the cap and the REIT prorates them. For example, with a **5%** quarterly NAV cap and investor requests totaling **10%**, the REIT might fulfill about half of each request, leaving the balance invested and often requiring a new request later. Gating protects the REIT and remaining shareholders from forced property sales. It also shows why a redemption request cannot be assumed to produce full cash access.

### How are redeemed non-traded REIT shares priced?

They are usually priced at the applicable periodic NAV, not a market price, because there is no exchange. NAV is calculated, often monthly or quarterly, using appraisals and valuation methods. An investor may receive less than full NAV when an early-redemption discount applies in the first year or two, and may be unable to redeem until a holding period has passed. The actual proceeds depend on NAV, holding period, and the plan’s terms.

### What is an early-redemption discount?

It is a reduction from NAV when an investor redeems in an initial holding period, commonly the first year or two. A schedule may impose a larger discount in year one, then reduce or eliminate it after a period of time. The feature discourages short-term exits and can help compensate the REIT and remaining investors for early-exit costs. Some plans bar a redemption altogether until a minimum holding period has elapsed. Review the schedule in the prospectus and plan.

### Why do non-traded REITs limit redemptions?

The underlying real estate is illiquid. Unlimited withdrawals could force sales at a discount, particularly during a downturn, damaging remaining shareholders and value. Caps, proration, and suspension authority align redemptions with the illiquid assets and guard against a fire sale. They are protections, not arbitrary obstacles. The trade-off is that an investor’s own liquidity is restricted, which is why the structure is for capital that does not need ready access.

### Are non-traded REIT redemptions guaranteed?

No. A program may allow a request, but the amount is capped, requests can be prorated, and the board can reduce, modify, or suspend the program. An investor might receive only a part of a request, receive nothing in a period, or find the program unavailable during market stress. Non-traded REITs are illiquid, long-term investments, and liquidity planning should not depend on full redemption availability.

### What happens to redemptions during a market downturn?

Requests can increase just as property sales become more difficult. To avoid a forced sale, the REIT may gate a larger part of requests or suspend the program until conditions improve. Real-world non-traded REITs have limited or halted redemptions during stressed markets. Plan so a gate or suspension is an inconvenience rather than a crisis, and do not assume a redemption will be available in a downturn.

### How often can I redeem from a non-traded REIT?

It depends on the REIT, but programs commonly operate monthly or quarterly with set request and processing windows. A holding period can delay eligibility, and an early-redemption discount can apply in the first year or two. Frequency does not overcome caps, potential gating, or suspension authority. The prospectus states the precise timing and restrictions.

### What is NAV and how does it affect redemptions?

NAV is net asset value: a periodic estimate of the REIT’s property and other assets less liabilities, stated per share. It is often calculated monthly or quarterly through appraisals and valuation methods. The REIT typically uses the applicable NAV to price a redemption, perhaps less an early-exit discount. NAV may lag market conditions and appear smoother than a live price while property values change. Read the valuation methodology because it determines what an exit may pay.

### Should I read the offering documents before investing in a non-traded REIT?

Absolutely. The prospectus and share repurchase plan set the frequency, caps, holding-period requirements, early-discount phase-outs, applicable NAV, and board authority to modify, gate, or suspend. Those terms determine actual liquidity far more than a general description of a redemption program. Read them, focus on the board’s discretion, and confirm the limits fit the investor’s liquidity needs before committing capital.

### How should I plan my liquidity around a non-traded REIT?

Use only capital that can stay committed for years and through a full hold period. Size the position so illiquid assets are not over-concentrated, retain separate liquid reserves, and, in a traditional IRA, ensure required minimum distributions can be met without a redemption. A non-traded REIT is not appropriate if the capital may be needed soon. Confirm fit through a suitability review.

### Can I sell a non-traded REIT to another investor instead of redeeming?

In most cases, no. There is no public exchange or ready pool of buyers, so it is generally not possible to find a transparent-price counterparty as with a listed stock. Limited secondary markets, where available, can be thin, opaque, and at significant discounts to NAV. Transfers can also require REIT consent and face offering-document restrictions. In practice, the options are the capped, gated, potentially suspended program or a final liquidity event. Review transfer restrictions before investing.

## Glossary

- **Non-Traded REIT:** An SEC-registered REIT not listed on a stock exchange.
- **Redemption Program:** A non-traded REIT's periodic share-repurchase liquidity feature.
- **Share Repurchase Plan:** Another name for a non-traded REIT redemption program.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The periodic per-share value used to price redemptions.
- **Redemption Cap:** The limit on how much can be redeemed per period.
- **Gating:** Prorating redemption requests when they exceed the cap.
- **Suspension:** The board's halting of redemptions, often in market stress.
- **Early-Redemption Discount:** A reduced price for redeeming within an initial holding period.
- **Holding Period:** A minimum time before shares become eligible for redemption.
- **Proration:** Reducing each investor's redemption to fit within the cap.
- **Liquidity Event:** A listing or sale that returns capital to investors.
- **Illiquidity:** The inability to readily sell a non-traded REIT interest.
- **Offering Documents:** The prospectus and plan detailing redemption terms.
- **Board Discretion:** The board's authority to modify or suspend redemptions.
- **Market Stress:** Conditions that often trigger gating or suspensions.
- **Suitability Review:** Assessing whether an illiquid non-traded REIT fits the investor.
