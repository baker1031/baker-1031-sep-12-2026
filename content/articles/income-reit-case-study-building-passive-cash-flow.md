## Selecting Income REITs

With the goal defined, the illustrative next step is selecting income-oriented REITs across complementary sectors. The composite investor leans toward sectors historically associated with steadier distributions: net-lease REITs, which own single-tenant properties under long leases where tenants cover most operating costs; healthcare REITs, including medical office, senior housing, and related property tied to long-term demographic demand; and residential REITs, including apartments and rental housing. The point is to combine sectors with different demand drivers so the income stream does not depend on one part of the economy.

Working with an advisor, she considers what bears on distribution sustainability instead of simply pursuing the highest headline yield. That includes funds from operations (FFO) and adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) relative to the dividend — a rough check on whether distributions are covered by cash flow — a [REIT's payout ratio](reit-payout-ratios-explained.html), balance-sheet leverage, occupancy, and lease durability. A very high yield can signal elevated risk rather than a bargain. Yield is one input, not the objective. This is illustrative methodology, not a recommendation of a specific REIT or a claim that any approach produces a particular result.

The selection logic is therefore complementary income sectors — here, net-lease, healthcare, and residential — paired with FFO/AFFO coverage, payout ratio, leverage, and occupancy. It favors distribution sustainability over headline yield. ## Distribution Results

To make the math concrete, assume an illustrative sample portfolio allocates roughly one-third to net-lease, one-third to healthcare, and one-third to residential income sectors. Suppose the net-lease sleeve yields about 5%, healthcare about 5.5%, and residential about 4%. Blended, that would produce an illustrative portfolio yield in the neighborhood of 4.5% to 5%. These numbers are invented for demonstration. They are not current market yields, a forecast, or a promise; actual yields vary widely and change constantly.

On an illustrative $300,000 allocation, a blended ~4.8% yield would generate roughly $14,400 of annual distributions, or about $1,200 a month. That is illustrative only. The point is not the dollar figure; it is the shape of the result: a diversified set of income REITs can, in principle, produce a regular monthly or quarterly cash-flow stream from rents across different property types. The income is not fixed. Distributions could rise if REITs grow payouts or fall if a [REIT cuts its distribution](why-reits-cut-dividends.html), and the underlying share prices fluctuate.

So the hypothetical result is a diversified, monthly-or-quarterly cash-flow stream from income REITs. The invented 4.5%–5% blended yield and the example of about $1,200 a month on $300,000 show mechanics, not a forecast. Actual results vary, and none of the figures is guaranteed.

## Tax Treatment of the Income

The after-tax result matters as much as headline yield. Most of a REIT's ordinary dividends are taxed as ordinary income, rather than at lower qualified-dividend rates, because the REIT itself paid no corporate tax. The offset is a 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends, which lowers the effective federal rate and was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. The dividends remain ordinary income, but the 199A deduction softens the rate.

Some distributions in this illustration could be classified as return of capital rather than current income. Return of capital is not taxed when received; it reduces cost basis and defers tax until shares are sold, when the lower basis means a larger gain. A portion might also be characterized as capital-gain distributions, taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the breakdown on Form 1099-DIV each year. Given the ordinary-income character, the investor and advisor might consider holding some REITs in a tax-advantaged account such as an IRA, where ordinary-income dividends are not currently taxed, while keeping the 199A benefit in mind for taxable holdings. Actual treatment depends on the REITs and the investor's situation.

The illustration's tax treatment is therefore mostly ordinary-income dividends softened by the 20% Section 199A deduction, often some return of capital that defers tax by reducing basis, and possible capital-gain components. All are reported on Form 1099-DIV. Account location can matter. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice, and treatment depends on the facts.

For an income-focused investor, the after-tax result is what counts: mostly ordinary-income REIT dividends, softened by the 20% Section 199A deduction and often including return of capital that defers tax by reducing basis.

## Lessons & Takeaways

The illustrative numbers support a few lessons. First, diversify: spreading an income-REIT allocation across complementary sectors with different demand drivers reduces reliance on any single property type and can smooth the income stream. Second, check sustainability, not just yield: a high headline yield can signal risk, so FFO/AFFO coverage, payout ratios, leverage, and occupancy matter more than reaching for the largest number. Third, mind taxes: because REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, the after-tax result and the account where REITs are held can affect what remains.

Fourth, keep expectations grounded. REIT distributions are not guaranteed, share prices fluctuate, and the sector is interest-rate-sensitive. An income-REIT stream belongs in a diversified plan, not in the mental category of a fixed annuity. The composite investor accepts that the illustrative ~4.5%–5% blended yield could be higher or lower in reality, that a REIT could cut its distribution, and that principal could move up or down. Passive cash flow through REITs can be realistic for many investors, but only when paired with realistic expectations and an understanding that these figures are illustrative, not promised.

## Risks and Caveats to Keep in View

The case study presents passive income, so the risks need to stay alongside it. REIT distributions are not guaranteed: a REIT can reduce or suspend its dividend if property income falls, occupancy drops, or financing conditions tighten. REITs are interest-rate-sensitive, so rising rates can pressure share prices and, for some REITs, distribution capacity. Publicly traded REIT prices fluctuate with the market and can decline even when underlying real estate is performing. Many REITs use leverage, which amplifies gains and losses. These are inherent real-estate risks, not hypothetical ones.

There are structural caveats as well. If some allocation were in non-traded REITs, that portion would be illiquid, with capped, suspendable redemptions and historically higher fees; it is not capital an investor can count on accessing quickly. The illustrative blended yield assumes distributions continue at assumed levels, which markets do not promise. Tax treatment, even softened by the 199A deduction, still leans toward ordinary income. The upside shown is illustrative; the risks are real. A suitable allocation reflects both.

### What is an income REIT?

An income REIT is not a separate legal category. It describes a REIT, or REIT allocation, held primarily for dividend income rather than growth. Investors seeking income often favor equity REITs in sectors historically associated with steadier distributions, including net-lease single-tenant properties under long leases, healthcare properties such as medical office and senior housing, and certain residential REITs. The 90% distribution rule requires REITs to distribute most taxable income, which is why REITs are commonly used for income. But an income REIT does not mean guaranteed income: distributions can be cut and share prices fluctuate. ### How are income REIT distributions taxed?

Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at lower qualified-dividend rates because the REIT paid no corporate tax. Qualified REIT dividends receive the 20% Section 199A deduction, made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. Some distributions are return of capital, which is not taxed on receipt but reduces basis and defers tax until sale; some may be capital-gain distributions taxed at capital-gains rates. The breakdown appears on Form 1099-DIV. The ordinary-income character leads many investors to consider tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs, though the 199A deduction applies only in taxable accounts. Verify treatment with a tax advisor.

### What is return of capital in a REIT distribution?

Return of capital is a portion of a REIT distribution not treated as current taxable income. It reduces cost basis, so tax is not paid now, but a lower basis means a larger taxable gain when shares are sold. It often arises because REIT depreciation deductions can shelter part of cash distributions. It can defer tax, but is not free money: tax comes due on sale. The return-of-capital portion appears on Form 1099-DIV. Confirm specifics with a tax advisor.

### Why diversify across REIT sectors for income?

Diversification reduces reliance on one property type and can smooth income. Net-lease income depends on long single-tenant leases and tenant credit; healthcare demand is tied to long-term demographic trends; residential demand reflects household formation, job growth, and housing affordability. Those drivers do not all move together, so weakness in a soft apartment market may be offset by stability elsewhere. Concentration exposes the whole income stream to a sector's specific risks. Diversification does not remove risk: REITs remain rate-sensitive and subject to market swings, and it does not eliminate distribution cuts or price declines.

### What metrics show whether a REIT's dividend is sustainable?

FFO and AFFO are REIT-specific measures of cash earnings. Comparing the dividend with FFO or AFFO — the payout ratio — indicates whether distributions are covered by recurring cash flow or stretching beyond it. A payout ratio comfortably below 100% of AFFO suggests more cushion; near or above 100% can signal risk. Balance-sheet leverage, occupancy, and lease durability matter too. None guarantees a result, but together they indicate whether a distribution may be supportable. Look at AFFO coverage and payout ratio, leverage, occupancy, and lease quality, not only headline yield.

### Should income REITs go in a taxable or retirement account?

It depends. Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, so a taxable account can produce a relatively high tax bill. In a traditional IRA, ordinary-income dividends are not currently taxed; in a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals can be tax-free. The counterpoint is that the 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends applies only in taxable accounts. Account space, other holdings, and goals all matter. Account location has no universal answer; discuss it with a tax advisor.

### What are the risks of building income with REITs?

Distributions can be cut or suspended if property income falls. Rising rates can pressure share prices and distribution capacity. Public REIT prices fluctuate and can decline even when properties perform. Leverage amplifies gains and losses. Non-traded REIT portions are illiquid, with capped, suspendable redemptions and historically higher fees. Income is mostly ordinary income. The upside in this case study is illustrative; those risks are real. A suitable allocation reflects potential income and the genuine downsides within a diversified plan, not as a guaranteed annuity.

### How much of a portfolio should income REITs be?

There is no universal percentage. The right allocation depends on goals, horizon, income needs, other holdings, and risk tolerance. REITs can provide income and diversification, but carry market, interest-rate, and for non-traded REITs, illiquidity risk. They are generally one building block, not a dominant position. Size REIT exposure so a distribution cut or price decline would not derail the overall plan, and diversify within REITs across sectors. The case-study figures are illustrative, not a recommended allocation.

### Can I build passive income with REITs instead of rental property?

For many investors, yes. REITs can provide real-estate income without owning and managing rental property. Rental owners handle tenants, maintenance, financing, and the concentration risk of one or a few buildings. Income REIT investors own shares in professionally managed portfolios, receive dividends, diversify across properties and sectors, often have liquidity when REITs are publicly traded, and can start at a lower entry cost. The trade-offs are loss of direct control and some direct tax benefits such as property depreciation; REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, and REIT shares are not 1031-eligible. The choice depends on goals.

## Glossary

### Income REIT

A REIT held primarily for its dividend income stream.

### Passive Cash Flow

Regular income earned without active management.

### Blended Yield

The weighted-average yield across a portfolio of REITs.

### Net-Lease REIT

A REIT owning single-tenant properties under long leases.

### Healthcare REIT

A REIT owning medical office, senior housing, and related property.

### Residential REIT

A REIT owning apartments and rental housing.

### FFO

Funds from operations — a REIT's cash-earnings measure.

### AFFO

Adjusted funds from operations — a refined cash-earnings measure.

### Payout Ratio

The share of FFO/AFFO paid out as distributions.

### 90% Distribution Rule

The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

### Section 199A Deduction

The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

### Return of Capital

A distribution reducing basis rather than being taxed currently.

### Capital-Gain Distribution

A REIT distribution taxed at capital-gains rates.

### Form 1099-DIV

The form reporting REIT dividends and their tax character.

### Distribution Sustainability

Whether a dividend is supported by recurring cash flow.

### Illustrative / Hypothetical

Invented for education — not a prediction or promise of results.
