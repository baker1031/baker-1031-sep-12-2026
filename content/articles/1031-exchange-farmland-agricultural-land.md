# 1031 Exchange for Farmland & Agricultural Land

I keep returning to the same fact with farmland: a family can own a piece of ground for decades, watch it appreciate far beyond its basis, and then discover that selling it is not a single decision. It is a land decision, a tax decision, a water-rights decision, an equipment decision, and sometimes an estate decision.

That is what makes a farmland exchange different. First-order thinking is that it is only acreage changing hands. Second-order thinking recognizes a bundle of assets with different tax character, a large four-layer tax at stake, and a choice between continuing to farm, repositioning the capital, or moving to passive ownership. A 1031 exchange can defer the gain, but the components must be understood before the sale starts the clock.

Farmland and agricultural land are strong candidates for an exchange because they are generally real property held for productive business use or investment. Land, qualifying improvements, and sometimes water rights can be included. This guide explains the boundaries and the planning work that makes them usable.

## Does Farmland Qualify?

Farmland and agricultural land qualify for a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) when held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. Land used to grow crops, raise livestock, or conduct an agricultural business meets the productive-use standard. Land held for appreciation or rented to a farmer meets the investment standard. Either can make the typical farm or agricultural parcel eligible as like-kind real property.

The real-estate like-kind rule is broad. Farmland may be exchanged for more farmland, [commercial property](1031-exchange-for-commercial-property.html), residential rentals, [raw land slated for development](1031-exchange-raw-land-development.html), or passive holdings such as DSTs. The agricultural character of the property sold does not require the replacement to be agricultural. The relevant question is whether both sides are investment or business real estate.

The important exception is purpose. A working farm or investment-held agricultural parcel generally qualifies. A rural parcel held principally for personal recreation, such as a personally used hunting retreat, may not—just as a personal vacation home may not. For most working farms and leased farmland, qualification is clear. The difficult work is usually sorting the property’s components.

## Land, Improvements, and Water Rights

A farm is not one asset. The land is real property and forms the core of the exchange. Barns, sheds, grain bins, fencing, permanent irrigation infrastructure, and similar fixtures are generally real-property improvements as well. Land and those qualifying improvements can be exchanged together as like-kind real estate and can be part of full deferral.

Since the **2017** tax law limited Section 1031 to real property, personal property no longer qualifies. Most permanent structures, permanent irrigation systems, and fences are real property. Movable equipment—tractors, combines, portable irrigation equipment, and livestock—is personal property. If it is included in a farm sale, its value must be carved out and handled separately, often with tax. The exchange should focus on the land and fixtures.

Water rights require their own analysis. Rights that are real property under the relevant state law, including appurtenant rights running with the land or perpetual interests in water, generally qualify as like-kind real property. Water rights that are personal property or contractual rights may not. In many agricultural areas, qualifying water rights can be very valuable, sometimes rivaling the land itself. Their character should be confirmed with a tax adviser familiar with the state’s water law before the exchange is structured.

The concise version is worth keeping in view: land and real-property improvements qualify; equipment does not; water rights qualify only when they are real property under state law.

## Turning Farmland Into Passive Property

One common reason to exchange farmland is retirement from farming. A farmer ready to step back, or a family that inherited land it does not want to operate, may exchange appreciated land into passive replacement property: DSTs, net-lease real estate, or other hands-off real estate. The exchange can defer the tax while moving the capital from active agricultural management to passive ownership.

For an aging owner, the benefit can be more than tax deferral. Farmland often represents decades of appreciation and a low basis, while farming demands labor, capital, and tolerance for operating risk. A properly chosen passive replacement can keep the full value working and provide retirement income without the management burden of farming.

DSTs can be a fit for this purpose. A farmland owner may exchange into a diversified set of DSTs holding institutional apartments, industrial properties, or medical office properties across several markets. The ownership is passive and professionally managed. If the assets are held until death, a step-up in basis may affect the deferred gain for heirs. For several heirs who inherit farmland together, DST interests can also give each heir an independent, divisible interest rather than requiring them to jointly operate the farm.

DSTs are securities. They are offered through a broker-dealer and require a suitability review. They are not automatically better than direct ownership; the replacement must be suitable on its property, debt, price, cash-flow, and liquidity terms. But for an owner moving from active farming to passive income, the transition can be useful.

## Conservation Easements, CRP, and Other Arrangements

Conservation easements and similar arrangements deserve separate attention. A conservation easement is a restriction on land—often donated or sold to a land trust or government—that limits development to preserve agricultural or natural value in exchange for tax benefits or payment. Donating an easement is a charitable-contribution transaction, not the same thing as exchanging the land. Selling an easement can have its own tax treatment.

Land subject to a conservation easement can generally still be exchanged. The easement restricts use and affects value, but the underlying land remains real property. Granting or selling the easement is a separate, technical transaction that may or may not fit into an exchange depending on its structure, so it warrants tax advice.

Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) enrollment, agricultural conservation easements, and other government-program participation can also affect the land’s value, income, and character. Land in a conservation program is generally still eligible real property, but the terms, payments, and restrictions must be accounted for. These arrangements usually do not prevent an exchange; they are facts that need to be handled deliberately.

## Planning a Farmland Exchange

The standard 1031 framework still applies. Engage a [qualified intermediary](insights.html) before the sale closes, identify replacement property within **45 days**, and close within **180 days**. To fully defer, reinvest proceeds in equal-or-greater-value replacement property and replace debt. The agricultural work sits on top of those core rules.

The key pre-sale task is component characterization. With a tax adviser, separate the real property—land, fixtures, and qualifying water rights—from personal property such as equipment and livestock. Confirm the water-rights classification under state law and allocate value among the components. That allocation establishes what is exchanged, what is separately taxable, and what replacement value is required.

Valuation is equally important. Farmland and water rights can be harder to value than a conventional building. A defensible appraisal by someone experienced in agricultural land, and water rights where relevant, supports the sale and the equal-or-greater-value calculation.

Then set the replacement goal before selling. It may be more farmland, other real estate, or passive property for retirement. Build the replacement list, including a fast-closing backup. Farm families should also coordinate the exchange with estate planning, particularly where several heirs are involved or a step-up at death is part of the long-term plan. The team normally includes the QI, CPA, tax adviser, and an advisor. Early planning with professionals familiar with agricultural property is what converts a complex farm asset into a fully deferred exchange.

## Key Takeaways

- Farmland is like-kind to any qualifying investment real estate, not just other farmland, when it is held for business or investment.
- Land and real-property improvements generally qualify. Equipment and livestock do not; water rights qualify only when they are real property under state law.
- A farmland-to-DST exchange can help an owner retire from farming while deferring the tax on appreciated land.
- Characterize the components, account for conservation or easement arrangements, and work with an agricultural tax adviser before the sale.

## Common Farmland Exchange Scenarios

The **retiring farmer** exchanges a working farm into passive DSTs, deferring the tax and converting decades of appreciated, labor-intensive land into passive retirement income.

The **inheriting family** exchanges farmland it does not want to operate into DSTs, allowing each heir an independent passive interest instead of a shared farm-management obligation. Because inheritance can reset basis, the important calculation is often the gain since inheritance.

The **repositioning investor** exchanges investment-held farmland into commercial or residential real estate. The **water-rights-rich owner** includes qualifying water rights as real property in the exchange. The **consolidating or trading-up farmer** exchanges smaller or less productive parcels into better farmland and uses deferred tax as added buying power.

These cases can overlap. A family may be retiring from farming, managing water rights, and planning for several heirs at the same time. The value of the exchange comes from recognizing the real goal—retirement, repositioning, consolidation, or succession—and treating each property component accordingly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does farmland qualify for a 1031 exchange?

Yes. Farmland and agricultural land qualify when held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. Crops, livestock, appreciation, and rental use can satisfy the test. It is like-kind to commercial, residential, and passive real estate as well as other farmland.

### Can I exchange farmland for non-agricultural property?

Yes. Farmland is like-kind to virtually any qualifying investment real property, including commercial buildings, residential rentals, raw land, and DSTs. Agricultural use does not limit the replacement to farmland.

### Do farm improvements qualify in the exchange?

Generally, yes. Barns, grain bins, permanent irrigation systems, and fencing are normally real-property improvements. Tractors, combines, portable irrigation equipment, and livestock are personal property, do not qualify since **2017**, and must be handled separately.

### Do water rights qualify for a 1031 exchange?

It depends on state law and the right itself. Appurtenant, perpetual water interests that are real property generally qualify; personal-property or contractual water rights may not. Use an adviser familiar with the state’s water law.

### Can I retire from farming using a 1031?

Yes. Owners commonly exchange appreciated farmland into passive DSTs, net-lease property, or other hands-off real estate. That can defer the tax, keep the full value invested, and turn management-intensive land into passive retirement income while preserving the possibility of a step-up at death.

### What happens to farm equipment in the exchange?

Movable equipment, including tractors, combines, portable irrigation, and livestock, is personal property and does not qualify. Its value is carved out and handled separately, often with tax and depreciation recapture. Land and fixtures remain the exchange property.

### Can land with a conservation easement be exchanged?

Generally, yes. The easement restricts land use and affects value, but the underlying land can remain like-kind real property. Granting or selling an easement is a separate transaction with separate tax treatment and should be reviewed with a tax adviser.

### Does CRP enrollment affect a farmland exchange?

CRP or similar enrollment generally does not prevent an exchange. Program terms, payments, and restrictions can affect value, income, and characterization, so they should be reviewed by an agricultural tax adviser.

### How do multiple heirs handle inherited farmland?

An exchange into DSTs can give each heir a divisible passive interest instead of a joint farm operation. The basis step-up at inheritance means the analysis should focus on post-inheritance gain, with a CPA quantifying that result.

### How do I value farmland for the exchange?

Use a professional appraisal. Farmland and water rights can be harder to value than buildings, and a defensible value supports both the sale and the equal-or-greater-value calculation. Component allocation also determines the deferred and taxable portions.

### What is the first step in planning a farmland exchange?

Characterize the components: land, fixtures, and qualifying water rights versus equipment and livestock. Confirm water rights under state law and establish the value allocation before engaging the QI and setting the replacement strategy.

### Should farm families coordinate the exchange with estate planning?

Yes, especially with several heirs or a desired step-up at death. A CPA, estate attorney, and 1031 advisor can align the exchange, DST divisibility, and the family’s succession goals.

### Can I exchange leased farmland I do not farm myself?

Yes. Farmland leased for cash rent or a crop share is held for investment. The owner need not farm it personally; rental income or appreciation can satisfy the qualifying purpose.

### Are mineral rights under my farmland part of the exchange?

They can be if the owner holds perpetual mineral or royalty interests that are real property. Whether to include them depends on the goals and structure. Characterization and valuation should be performed with advice from someone familiar with land and minerals.

### Does a farm’s primary-residence farmhouse complicate the exchange?

It can. A farmhouse used as a personal residence is personal-use property, not 1031-eligible. It must be carved out and may qualify separately for the Section 121 residence exclusion. The farmland and agricultural improvements are exchanged, and the CPA allocates value between them.

### How is a farmland exchange valued when it includes water rights?

Value both land and water rights professionally. Water rights can rival land value and are often harder to value. A qualified agricultural and water-rights appraiser supports the allocation, sale, equal-or-greater-value calculation, and property characterization.

## Glossary

- **Farmland:** Agricultural land held for crops, livestock, or a farming business; it can qualify as business or investment property.
- **Agricultural Land:** Land used or held for agricultural purposes, generally eligible real property.
- **Held for Productive Use:** The qualifying business purpose for land used in a trade or business.
- **Real-Property Improvements:** Fixtures such as barns, grain bins, and permanent irrigation that qualify as real property.
- **Personal Property:** Movable equipment and livestock that no longer qualify for 1031 since **2017**.
- **Water Rights:** Rights to use water that qualify when real property under state law, including appurtenant or perpetual interests.
- **Appurtenant:** Attached to and running with the land, a feature of some qualifying water rights.
- **Conservation Easement:** A development restriction to preserve land, with separate tax treatment.
- **Conservation Reserve Program (CRP):** A program that pays owners to conserve land; participation is a farmland-exchange factor.
- **Like-Kind:** The standard requiring real property held for investment or business use.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive, diversified real-property interest that can let owners retire from farming.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of basis at death, relevant to farmland succession.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, which can apply to carved-out farm equipment.
- **Component Characterization:** Separating a farm’s real property from personal property for the exchange.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party holding proceeds so the seller avoids constructive receipt.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** The requirement to acquire replacement value at least equal to the net sale price for full deferral.
