# Common DST Myths and Misconceptions

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › Common DST Myths and Misconceptions

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep coming back to the gap between a projected distribution and a promised payment. That gap is where a great deal of DST confusion starts. Delaware Statutory Trusts are widely misunderstood: investors can mistakenly expect guaranteed income, easy resale, ultra-wealthy-only access, or interchangeable sponsors.

First-order thinking treats a DST as passive income with a tax feature. Second-order thinking asks what sits behind the income, how long the capital is committed, who controls the decisions, and whether the sponsor, property, debt, and fees deserve the risk. The four myths are false: distributions are projections; DSTs are illiquid and generally held to full cycle; accredited status is not the same as being ultra-wealthy; and sponsors vary enormously in quality.

DST interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice; verify current rules with a tax advisor, and regard distributions as projections rather than guarantees. [Our complete 2026 guide to Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html) provides the underlying structure before these misconceptions are considered.

## Myth: DSTs Are Guaranteed Income

They are not. DST distributions are projections based on expected net rental income, not promises. They can be reduced or suspended if a property underperforms. A DST is real estate, not a fixed-income product such as a bond or CD, so income depends on the property's performance during the hold.

Tenant default or vacancy, declining occupancy, higher operating expenses, and higher debt service on a floating-rate loan can each reduce cash available for distribution. The annual yield shown in an offering may be a sponsor's reasonable estimate, but it carries no guarantee; the PPM risk factors state that plainly.

Treating a DST as a bond and counting on a fixed payment invites disappointment when the property encounters a rough patch. Income can be real, but it is variable and stands behind real-estate risk. The useful correction is simple: a headline yield is a projection, not a payment anyone promised to make.

## Myth: You Can Sell Anytime

Generally, an investor cannot. DST interests are illiquid, with no meaningful secondary market that works like a stock exchange. Once invested, an owner is normally committed until the sponsor sells the property at full cycle, commonly after five to seven or more years.

An informal resale market may occasionally exist, but it is thin and unreliable. There may be no buyer; if one appears, the seller may need to accept a meaningful discount to value. This is not a temporary inconvenience. It is a structural feature of a passive, packaged real-estate investment, disclosed in the offering documents.

Capital committed to a DST should not be capital needed during the hold. The correct question is not whether an early exit might materialize, but whether the investor can afford for it not to. A DST is a multi-year illiquid commitment, not a liquid holding that can be exited at will.

## Myth: DSTs Are Only for the Wealthy

DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to [accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html), so a financial threshold is real. It is not the same as ultra-wealth. The standard is generally income over $200,000 individually, or $300,000 jointly, in each of the last two years; alternatively, net worth over $1 million excluding a primary residence.

Many long-time real-estate owners meet the net-worth test through appreciation and loan paydown even if they would not describe themselves as wealthy in everyday terms. DST minimums are also often around $25,000 to $100,000, not the multimillion-dollar entry point some people imagine. A person can be accredited without being part of the wealthy elite.

DSTs are not open to everyone, and accreditation needs confirmation. Still, they are commonly used by everyday property owners completing a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), not only by ultra-rich investors.

## Key Takeaways

- Myth: DSTs are guaranteed income—false. Distributions are projections based on property performance and may be reduced or suspended.
- Myth: You can sell anytime—false. DSTs are illiquid, lack a real secondary market, and are generally held to full cycle, often five to seven-plus years.
- Myth: DSTs are only for the wealthy—false. They require accredited status, which many real-estate owners with substantial equity meet.
- Myth: All sponsors are the same—false. Track record, fees, and quality differ widely, so due diligence is essential.

## Myth: All Sponsors Are the Same

This is a costly belief. DST sponsors vary in track record, financial strength, full-cycle history, fees, underwriting conservatism, property quality, and transparency. Because the sponsor controls decisions in a passive DST, sponsor quality is a major determinant of outcome.

Some sponsors have decades of experience, managed billions across several market cycles, and full-cycle results that performed in line with or close to projections. Others are newer, more thinly capitalized, or have launched offerings only in favorable markets, leaving little full-cycle history to assess. Fees and disclosure practices differ. So do underwriting discipline and property selection.

A stronger sponsor may better support a property through a soft period, [fund reserves](dst-reserves-and-capital-expenditures.html), and avoid forced decisions. A weaker sponsor can overpay, over-leverage, or manage poorly. In a passive investment, treating sponsors as interchangeable discards the factor diligence can most directly examine. Vetting the sponsor is the most important due-diligence step.

## A Few More Myths to Avoid

A DST does not normally issue a complicated partnership K-1. Because it is [a grantor trust](how-dsts-work-structure-beneficial-interests-and-trustees.html), an investor generally receives a substitute Form 1099 and grantor-trust statement. The investor reports a proportionate share of income and depreciation much as with directly owned property.

A DST is not a REIT. A DST interest is 1031-eligible like-kind real property, while a REIT share is a security and is not 1031-eligible. That distinction is central to tax deferral. Some investors later move from a DST into a REIT through a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), but that is a different transaction with its own considerations.

Passivity is not safety. A DST retains market, tenant, and financing risk, plus [illiquidity, sponsor risk, and fees](dst-pros-and-cons-for-1031-investors.html). Principal can be lost. Professional management changes who makes decisions; it does not remove the underlying real-estate risks.

Deferral also is not unlimited and consequence-free. Investors may chain exchanges, including DST-to-DST exchanges and a DST-to-REIT 721 transaction. A [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death may eliminate deferred gain for heirs under current law. But the rules, deadlines, and tax and estate treatment are technical and may change. Work with a tax advisor.

## The Reality Investors Should Know

A DST is a passive, illiquid, fractional real-estate investment that can fit the right 1031 investor. It is not guaranteed, liquid, or risk-free. Distributions depend on performance; capital stays committed until full cycle; it requires accredited status that many property owners meet; and outcome depends materially on property and sponsor quality.

That reality is not an indictment. It is the information an investor needs to decide clearly. Treat projected distributions as estimates. Commit only capital that can remain invested for years. Confirm accredited status and suitability. Diligence the sponsor, property, debt, and fees rather than assuming offerings are interchangeable or safe.

Approached with that discipline, a DST can provide passive, diversified, tax-deferred real estate without direct-management burdens and without surprises created by myths. It should fit the investor on its investment merits, not merely serve as a way to defer tax.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are DST distributions guaranteed?

No. They are projections based on expected net rental income and may be reduced or suspended if the property underperforms. Tenant defaults or vacancies, declining occupancy, rising operating expenses, or higher floating-rate debt service can reduce distributable cash flow. The PPM risk factors say projected yields are not guarantees. A DST is real estate, not a bond or CD; its income is real but variable and subject to real-estate risk.

### Can I sell a DST interest whenever I want?

No. A DST interest generally cannot be sold on demand because no meaningful secondary market exists. The investor is ordinarily committed until the sponsor sells the property at full cycle, commonly after five to seven or more years. A limited informal resale market can be thin, unreliable, and available only at a meaningful discount, if at all. Do not invest capital needed during the hold or assume an early exit will be available.

### Are DSTs only for wealthy investors?

No. They are limited to accredited investors under Regulation D, but accreditation is not synonymous with ultra-wealth. The standard is generally $200,000 individual income or $300,000 joint income in each of the prior two years, or net worth above $1 million excluding a primary residence. Many property owners satisfy the latter through accumulated equity. Typical $25,000 to $100,000 minimums are well below the multimillion-dollar entry people often assume. DSTs are commonly used by everyday accredited owners completing a 1031 exchange.

### Are all DST sponsors the same?

No. Sponsors differ in financial strength, experience, full-cycle history, fees, underwriting, property quality, and transparency. Some have decades of experience, assets managed in the billions, and full-cycle history across market cycles; others may be newer, thinner capitalized, or untested outside favorable markets. In a passive DST, the sponsor controls decisions. Stronger sponsors can support a property through a soft period and fund reserves; weak sponsors can overpay, over-leverage, or manage poorly. Sponsor track record and full-cycle history deserve close diligence.

### Does a DST issue a K-1?

Generally no. A DST typically issues a substitute Form 1099 and grantor-trust statement, not a Schedule K-1. Grantor-trust treatment treats the beneficial owner as directly owning a proportionate interest in property income, expenses, and depreciation, so reporting resembles direct real-estate ownership. Partnerships issue K-1s to partners. The treatment helps a DST interest qualify as like-kind real property in a 1031 exchange and allows an owner to report depreciation that may shelter part of distributions. Confirm current tax treatment with a CPA.

### Is a DST the same as a REIT?

No. A DST interest is treated as a direct interest in like-kind real property and can qualify as 1031 replacement property. A REIT share is a security and is not 1031-eligible, so an investor cannot directly exchange into a REIT and defer gain. A publicly traded REIT may offer daily liquidity and a larger portfolio, while a DST may hold a specific property or small portfolio and is illiquid. A DST may suit a 1031 investor seeking deferral; a REIT may suit a person needing liquidity without 1031 treatment. Some investors eventually use a 721 exchange to move from a DST into a REIT.

### Are DSTs low-risk because they are passive?

No. Passive management is not safety. A DST has the same market, tenant, and financing risks as real estate, plus illiquidity, sponsor risk, and fees, and principal can be lost. Property values can decline; tenants can default or leave; occupancy can fall; leverage can amplify loss; and debt can be difficult to refinance at maturity. Distributions may be reduced or suspended. Illiquidity usually prevents an early exit to limit loss. Diligence can reduce risk but cannot eliminate it, so position size and diversification matter.

### Can I keep deferring taxes with DSTs forever?

Long-term deferral is possible but not automatic or consequence-free. An investor may chain 1031 exchanges from one DST to another or move from a DST into a REIT through a 721 exchange. Under current law, heirs who receive qualifying investment property at death generally receive a step-up in basis to fair market value that can eliminate deferred gain. Every 1031 exchange must meet its requirements and deadlines, every 721 exchange has its own rules, and tax and estate laws can change. Work with a CPA and attorney rather than assuming a permanent result.

### Do DSTs have high minimums?

No. Minimums are often $25,000 to $100,000, well below the price of most direct replacement properties and the multimillion-dollar entry some assume. Those relatively low minimums can help an investor split an exchange among several sponsors, property types, and geographies. The practical gate is accreditation, not necessarily a huge minimum. A modest minimum does not remove the need for suitability, diligence, or sensible position sizing.

### Is a DST a good way to avoid taxes?

A DST defers taxes; it does not simply avoid them. Used as 1031 replacement property, it can defer capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture that would otherwise follow a relinquished-property sale, keeping full equity invested in real estate. Continued exchanges and a qualifying step-up at death can, under current law, eliminate deferred gain for heirs, but that is an estate-planning outcome and not a guarantee supplied by the DST itself. The tool should be part of a sound investment and estate plan, not a pure tax-avoidance scheme. Verify the technical rules with a CPA.

### Why do these DST myths persist?

DSTs are complex securities, and complexity invites oversimplification. Marketing sometimes emphasizes passive, tax-deferred, or attractive projected yields more than risk. Investors can also learn secondhand without reading the PPM, where projections, illiquidity, lack of control, and risk factors are plainly disclosed. Accreditation can wrongly imply that DSTs are exclusive ultra-wealthy products. Read the PPM, ask questions, use a representative who offers a balanced view, and evaluate a DST as real estate.

### What is the single most important thing to know about DSTs?

A DST is a real-estate investment with real risk, not a guaranteed, liquid, or risk-free product. Its outcome depends heavily on its property and sponsor. That insight resolves the central myths: distributions are performance-dependent projections, capital can be committed for years, and property and sponsor quality require diligence. Accreditation is a meaningful but broadly met eligibility threshold. For the right 1031 investor, a DST can still be passive, diversified, and tax-deferred; the discipline is to treat projections as estimates, preserve liquidity outside the trust, confirm suitability, and conduct due diligence.

### Should I avoid DSTs because of these risks?

Not necessarily. The purpose of correcting myths is realistic decision-making, not discouragement. DSTs carry illiquidity, sponsor, market, tenant, fee, and distribution risk, but those trade-offs may suit the right investor. A DST can offer passive, diversified, tax-deferred real estate without direct management and can provide debt replacement without personal qualification. The investor should evaluate whether a particular trust from a quality sponsor fits goals, preference for passivity, and liquidity needs. Suitability review and diligent work on sponsor, property, debt, and fees help turn risks into known trade-offs rather than surprises.

### How can I tell DST fact from marketing hype?

Go to the source documents and ask hard questions. Read the PPM risk factors for statements about projected—not guaranteed—distributions, illiquidity, no control, and potential principal loss. Compare a headline projected yield with the tenants, lease terms, occupancy, and market that support income, as well as fees and leverage. Scrutinize actual sponsor track record and full-cycle history rather than marketing claims. Be skeptical of language promising safety, guarantees, or easy liquidity. A representative should give a balanced picture and answer questions candidly.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)**  
A trust holding income-producing real estate, with 1031-eligible beneficial interests.

**Distribution**  
An investor's share of DST net rental income—a projection, not a guarantee.

**Projection**  
An estimate of expected returns, not a promised outcome.

**Illiquidity**  
The inability to readily sell a DST interest before full cycle.

**Secondary Market**  
A market to resell interests—thin and unreliable for DSTs.

**Full Cycle**  
When the sponsor sells the DST property, ending the investment.

**Accredited Investor**  
An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Regulation D offerings.

**Regulation D**  
The SEC exemption under which DST interests are offered.

**Low Minimum**  
A typical DST entry point, often around $25,000 to $100,000.

**Sponsor**  
The company that acquires, structures, and manages a DST.

**Sponsor Risk**  
The risk that the controlling sponsor performs poorly.

**Grantor Trust**  
The tax status under which a DST issues a 1099, not a K-1.

**REIT**  
A real-estate company whose shares are not 1031-eligible.

**721 Exchange**  
Contributing property to a REIT for OP units, preserving deferral.

**Step-Up in Basis**  
The basis reset to fair market value for heirs at death.

**Suitability Review**  
Assessing whether a DST fits the investor's situation.
