# Can You 1031 Exchange Mineral Rights? Eligibility Rules Explained

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Mineral & Royalty Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 8 min read

I keep seeing mineral owners focus first on the sale price and the chance to defer gain. That is understandable. But the more consequential question arrives before either: what, exactly, is the legal interest being sold?

First-order thinking asks whether minerals are real estate. Second-order thinking asks whether this particular interest is perpetual real property under the applicable state law, rather than a finite claim on production or payments. That distinction decides whether the intended deferral has a foundation.

Mineral rights are the niche's highest-volume question. They can qualify for a like-kind exchange — but only when they are treated as a perpetual interest in real property. Here's where the line falls.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">It is the most-searched question in the niche, and the answer is a qualified yes: mineral rights can be exchanged under Section 1031 — but only when they are treated as a <strong>perpetual interest in real property</strong>. The qualification is the whole story, and getting it wrong can turn an intended tax deferral into a taxable sale.</p>
<p>For the complete eligibility picture and every step that follows, see <a href="mineral-rights-1031-guide.html">our in-depth mineral and royalty exchange guide</a>.</p>
<h2 id="qualify">Do Mineral Rights Qualify for a 1031 Exchange?</h2>
<p>In most producing states, mineral rights are classified as real property, and federal tax law follows that treatment for like-kind exchange purposes. So as a general rule, a fee mineral interest — your ownership of the oil, gas, and minerals in place — can be sold and exchanged for other like-kind real estate with the capital gain deferred.</p>
<p>The qualification has two parts. First, the interest must actually be real property under the law of the state where it sits. Second, since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act limited Section 1031 to real property only, the interest cannot be something the Code reclassifies as personal property or as a financing. Most ordinary mineral and royalty ownership clears both tests; the trouble comes with unusual, time-limited, or carved-out interests.</p>
<h2 id="real-vs-personal">Real Property vs. Personal Property Treatment</h2>
<p>The real-versus-personal line is where exchanges live or die. An interest in the minerals <em>in place</em> — still in the ground, conveyed for the life of the deposit — is real property. Once minerals are severed and produced at the surface, they become personal property (and ordinary income), which is why you exchange the interest, not the barrels.</p>
<p>This is also why a <a href="1031-exchange-guide.html">1031 exchange</a> of minerals is documented like any real estate deal — by deed, with title work, division orders, and a <a href="what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html">qualified intermediary</a> holding the funds — rather than like a sale of equipment. If an instrument looks more like a contract right to a stream of payments than ownership of property in the ground, it is at risk of being treated as personal property and disqualified.</p>
<h2 id="perpetual">The Perpetual Interest Requirement</h2>
<p>The single most important test is duration. To be like-kind real property, the interest generally must be <strong>perpetual</strong> — lasting for the economic life of the reserve — not limited to a term of years or a fixed volume of production. The classic authorities turn on exactly this point: in Rev. Rul. 68-331 a leasehold extending <em>until the deposit was exhausted</em> qualified, precisely because it was a continuing interest.</p>
<p>Flip the duration and the interest fails. <a href="term-vs-perpetual-royalties-1031-like-kind.html">A royalty carved out for ten years</a>, or a right to a set number of barrels, is a finite interest the IRS is likely to treat as something other than a perpetual real-property interest. <a href="do-production-payments-qualify-1031-exchange.html">Production payments are the cleanest example</a>: IRC §636 treats them as loans, not property, so they generally cannot anchor a 1031 exchange.</p>
<div class="pullquote"><p>Duration is the test. A perpetual interest in the minerals in place is real property; a term-limited slice of production usually is not.</p><cite>Jerry Baker</cite></div>
<h2 id="when">When Mineral Rights Are — and Aren't — Eligible</h2>
<p>Eligible, as a general matter: fee mineral interests, perpetual royalty interests, overriding royalty interests tied to a continuing estate, and working interests that run until exhaustion. These convey a continuing interest in real property and have a long history of like-kind treatment.</p>
<p>At risk or ineligible: term mineral or royalty interests, production payments, net profits interests in some forms, and any interest whose state-law character is personal property. The safest path when your interest is unusual is to confirm its character with counsel before you market it — and, if a direct replacement is hard to source inside 45 days, to keep an exchange-eligible <a href="oil-and-gas-1031-exchange-mineral-royalty-owners.html">mineral or real estate DST</a> identified as a backup.</p>
<div class="takeaways"><div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>Mineral rights generally qualify for a 1031 exchange when state law treats them as real property and the interest is perpetual.</li>
<li>The perpetual-interest rule is decisive: term interests and production payments (loans under §636) usually fail.</li>
<li>Document the exchange like real estate — deed, title, division orders, qualified intermediary — and confirm unusual interests with counsel first.</li>
</ul></div>
<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq">
<details open="">
<summary>Are mineral rights eligible for a 1031 exchange?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Generally yes, when the mineral rights are treated as real property under the law of the state where they are located and the interest is perpetual rather than limited to a term of years. Fee mineral interests and perpetual royalties typically qualify; term interests and production payments usually do not.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What is the perpetual interest rule?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>To be like-kind real property, an oil and gas interest generally must last for the economic life of the reserve rather than for a fixed term or a set quantity of production. A continuing interest in the minerals in place qualifies; a finite, time-limited interest typically does not.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Are mineral rights real property or personal property?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>An interest in the minerals still in the ground is generally real property. Once minerals are produced and severed at the surface they become personal property. Section 1031 exchanges involve the in-place real-property interest, not the produced product.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I exchange mineral rights for other real estate?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Yes. A qualifying perpetual mineral or royalty interest is like-kind to most U.S. investment real estate, so it can be exchanged for multifamily, net-lease, land, or a DST interest, and vice versa, with the gain deferred when the standard 1031 rules are met.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Do term royalties or production payments qualify?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Usually not. A royalty limited to a term of years or a fixed volume is a finite interest at risk of disqualification, and a production payment is treated as a loan under IRC §636 rather than a real-property interest. Both generally fall outside Section 1031.</p></div>
</details>
</div>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>Mineral Rights</dt><dd>Ownership of the oil, gas, and minerals in place beneath a tract, including the right to develop them or lease that right. Generally real property in producing states.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Perpetual Interest</dt><dd>An interest that continues for the economic life of the reserve rather than a fixed term. Perpetual duration is the key test for like-kind real-property treatment.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Fee Mineral Interest</dt><dd>Full ownership of the mineral estate, separate from the surface. Typically the clearest real-property interest for 1031 purposes.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Real Property</dt><dd>For Section 1031 after 2017, U.S. real estate held for investment or business. An interest in minerals in place generally qualifies; produced minerals do not.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Production Payment</dt><dd>A right to a fixed quantity or value of future production. Treated as a loan under IRC §636, so it generally cannot support a 1031 exchange.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Division Order</dt><dd>A document directing how production revenue is divided among interest owners. Part of the paperwork a qualified intermediary handles in a mineral exchange.</dd></div>
</dl>
