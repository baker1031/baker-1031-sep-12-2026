# 1031 Exchanges for LLCs & Partnerships

I keep seeing entity charts treated as an administrative detail just as a sale is coming together. They are not. When property is held in an LLC, partnership, or another entity, the structure can determine who is even allowed to complete the exchange.

Most real-estate investors use entities for liability protection, management, or estate planning. Those are sensible reasons. But a 1031 exchange follows the taxpayer, not the ownership diagram. The entity’s tax classification therefore comes first: it tells us who sold the relinquished property, who must buy the replacement, and whether owners can pursue different paths.

First-order thinking is that an LLC label supplies flexibility. Second-order thinking is that the tax classification behind that label may either preserve flexibility or make a later separation difficult. Get that answer before the sale, not after the 45-day clock has begun.

## Who Is the Taxpayer in an Entity?

The foundational question is simple: who is the taxpayer? The same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer that sells the relinquished property to acquire the replacement property. The answer depends on how the entity is treated for tax purposes.

A single-member LLC is generally a **disregarded entity**. It is not treated as separate from its one owner for federal tax purposes: the owner reports the income and is treated as owning the property directly. In that case, the owner is the taxpayer.

A multi-member LLC or partnership is different. It is generally taxed as a partnership and is its own taxpayer for the exchange. The entity owns the property, sells it, and must acquire the replacement. Its individual members are not the taxpayer that owned the relinquished property.

This is why the distinction changes the result. A single owner is effectively carrying out an individual exchange, with the titling flexibility that comes with it. Partners cannot simply divide an entity-owned property sale and each conduct an independent exchange; the partnership was the seller.

Other entities have their own treatment. A C corporation and an S corporation are separate taxpayers, so a corporation-owned property is exchanged by the corporation. A trust’s treatment depends on the type of trust. For the structures most often used in real estate, however, the important first split is single-member LLC versus multi-member LLC or partnership.

## Single-Member LLCs and 1031

For a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), a single-member LLC is generally the cleanest entity structure. The LLC offers liability protection, while its disregarded tax treatment means the owner remains the taxpayer. The exchange is, in substance, the owner’s exchange.

That creates useful room to title the two legs of the transaction. An owner may relinquish a property held in one single-member LLC and acquire replacement property in the owner’s individual name or through another single-member LLC that the owner owns. The same-taxpayer rule can still be satisfied because the taxpayer has not changed.

This also allows a practical liability strategy. An investor may use a separate single-member LLC for each property, then form a new single-member LLC for the replacement. The LLC isolates liability, but the owner remains the consistent taxpayer. Single-member LLCs are popular for investment real estate for exactly this combination of liability protection and exchange simplicity.

> A single-member LLC is generally disregarded for tax. The owner is the taxpayer, which can pair entity-level liability protection with the exchange simplicity of individual ownership.

## Multi-Member Partnership Issues

Multi-member LLCs and partnerships require more care because the entity is the separate taxpayer. The partnership must sell the property and acquire the replacement property. That arrangement works when the owners want to continue owning a replacement together.

The trouble begins when their aims diverge. One partner may want to defer gain, another may want cash, and another may want a different replacement property. The partnership can generally complete one exchange into one replacement, but it cannot let each partner run a separate exchange from the entity’s property. That is the familiar issue that arises [when a partnership sells and the partners diverge](insights.html).

Two techniques are often discussed in this setting:

- **Drop-and-swap:** The partnership distributes property interests to its partners before the sale, potentially allowing each partner to exchange directly.
- **Swap-and-drop:** The partnership completes an exchange and then distributes interests afterward.

Neither phrase is a shortcut around the rule. Each carries timing, documentation, holding-period, and IRS-scrutiny concerns. See the related [holding-period discussion](1031-exchange-holding-period-how-long.html). Experienced tax counsel should evaluate the facts well in advance. When partners are aligned, an entity-level exchange may be straightforward. When they are separating, planning becomes the central task.

## The Same-Taxpayer Requirement

The same-taxpayer requirement is the connective rule behind these issues. The taxpayer that sells the relinquished property must be the taxpayer that acquires the replacement. Deferred gain and the carried basis belong to that taxpayer, so a change between the two legs generally breaks the exchange.

For a partnership, that means the same partnership must acquire the replacement. For an individual, it means that same individual must acquire it, whether directly or through a disregarded single-member LLC. A partnership cannot relinquish the property and have its individual partners acquire the replacement separately; that substitutes new taxpayers for the entity that made the sale.

The disregarded-entity point is the important nuance. Moving property between an owner’s individual name and the owner’s single-member LLC, or between two of that owner’s single-member LLCs, does not ordinarily change the taxpayer. A multi-member partnership has no comparable freedom to substitute its partners or an unrelated entity.

## Planning Entity Exchanges

Start by confirming the entity’s tax classification and identifying the taxpayer. For a single-member LLC, planning generally follows the individual-owner path, while still allowing thoughtful liability-oriented titling.

For a multi-member entity, ask one question early: are the partners staying together? If yes, the partnership may relinquish and acquire as the same partnership through an entity-level exchange. If no, the discussion turns to a drop-and-swap or swap-and-drop, along with their timing and documentation requirements. That work belongs with experienced counsel, not a last-minute closing instruction.

Sometimes owners who expect to go their separate ways choose to restructure in advance, perhaps through single-member LLCs or a tenant-in-common structure. That may make a future separate exchange easier, but it requires foresight and its own tax analysis. A CPA should confirm classification and tax consequences; an attorney should address entity structure and titling; experienced tax counsel is especially important when partnership owners are separating.

### Key Takeaways

- The taxpayer depends on the entity: a single-member LLC is generally disregarded, while a multi-member LLC or partnership is generally its own taxpayer.
- A single-member LLC can combine liability protection with relatively simple same-taxpayer continuity.
- Partners in a multi-member entity cannot each run a separate exchange without specialized approaches such as a drop-and-swap or swap-and-drop.
- Continuity of the taxpayer on both legs is the rule to protect. Plan with a CPA and, where partners may separate, experienced tax counsel.

## Titling and Structuring the Replacement

Titling is where the tax rule meets the investor’s liability and management goals. A single-member LLC owner may title the replacement in the owner’s own name, in the same LLC, or in a new single-member LLC the owner forms. Because the owner is still the taxpayer, the entity choice can support liability isolation without changing taxpayer identity.

The partnership case is narrower. A partnership conducting an entity-level exchange must title the replacement in the same partnership, or in a disregarded single-member LLC wholly owned by that partnership. It cannot put the replacement in the individual partners’ names or in a different entity and still assume continuity is intact.

The practical answer is often a disregarded single-member LLC owned by the same taxpayer that relinquished the property. That taxpayer may be an individual or a partnership. It can provide a separate liability wrapper while preserving the ownership continuity the exchange requires. A CPA and attorney should structure and document the titling for the particular facts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who is the taxpayer in an LLC or partnership exchange?

It depends on classification. A single-member LLC is generally disregarded for tax, so its owner is the taxpayer. A multi-member LLC or partnership is generally a separate taxpayer, so the entity is the taxpayer. The distinction matters because the selling taxpayer must acquire the replacement.

### Can a single-member LLC do a 1031 exchange?

Yes. It is generally disregarded for tax, so the owner’s exchange can be conducted through the LLC. The LLC can provide liability protection without complicating the exchange, as long as the same owner remains the taxpayer on both legs.

### Why are multi-member partnerships more complicated?

The partnership must sell and acquire the replacement; partners cannot each separately exchange their share of entity-owned property. That is manageable when they remain together. If they want different outcomes, special techniques may be considered, with timing, holding-period, and IRS-scrutiny concerns.

### What is the same-taxpayer requirement?

It is the rule that the taxpayer selling the relinquished property must acquire the replacement. The deferred gain and basis follow that taxpayer. A change of taxpayer between the sale and acquisition generally fails the exchange.

### Can I hold the replacement in a different LLC?

Yes, when the new entity is a single-member LLC owned by the same taxpayer. A partnership may likewise use a single-member LLC it wholly owns. In each case, the taxpayer—not merely the legal title—must remain consistent.

### Can partners in a multi-member LLC exchange separately?

Not directly from the partnership’s sale. A drop-and-swap distributes property interests before the sale; a swap-and-drop distributes after an entity-level exchange. Both require experienced counsel, careful timing and documentation, and attention to IRS scrutiny.

### Does an entity exchange satisfy the same-taxpayer rule automatically?

Only if the same entity or disregarded owner is on both legs. A partnership must relinquish and acquire as the partnership. A partnership sale followed by individual partners acquiring replacements changes the taxpayer and generally breaks the rule.

### How should I title the replacement property?

Title it through the consistent taxpayer while meeting liability objectives. A single-member LLC owner may use the owner’s name, the existing LLC, or a new single-member LLC. A partnership generally uses the same partnership or a wholly owned disregarded LLC. Have a CPA and attorney confirm the structure.

### Can I get liability protection and still do a 1031?

Yes. A disregarded single-member LLC can isolate liability while remaining the same taxpayer as its individual or partnership owner. A replacement may be held in a new LLC if the taxpayer that made the relinquishment owns it.

### Should I restructure my entity before an exchange?

Sometimes. If partners expect to exchange separately, advance restructuring into single-member LLCs or a TIC structure may help. It requires foresight. A conversion or distribution may have its own tax consequences, so work with a CPA and attorney before acting.

### What entity is best for holding 1031 property?

Single-member LLCs are common where one owner wants liability protection and simple exchange treatment. Multi-member entities can work for joint ownership but make a later partner separation harder. The right choice depends on ownership goals, liability, estate planning, and anticipated exchanges.

### Who should I work with for an entity exchange?

Use a CPA to confirm tax classification and tax treatment, an attorney for entity structure and title, and experienced tax counsel if partners may separate. An advisor can coordinate the exchange and replacement-property process.

### Is an LLC taxed as an S-corp treated like a partnership for 1031?

An LLC that elected S-corporation treatment is a separate corporate taxpayer, so the corporation conducts the exchange. It shares the feature of having one taxpayer, but S-corporation distributions and available approaches differ from partnerships. Partnership drop-and-swap techniques are not S-corporation techniques; confirm the approach with CPA and counsel.

### Can a husband-and-wife LLC be disregarded?

In community-property states, a husband-and-wife LLC can sometimes be treated as a disregarded entity through a qualified joint venture or community-property arrangement. In other states, a two-member spousal LLC is generally a partnership. State law and elections determine the treatment, so confirm it with your CPA.

### Does converting my entity before an exchange trigger tax?

It can. Some conversions and distributions are tax-free, while others can trigger gain. Analyze a partnership conversion or a distribution to members with a CPA and attorney before using it to position an exchange.

### Can I move property from my name into an LLC and still exchange?

Moving property into your own disregarded single-member LLC generally keeps you as the taxpayer, so it generally does not interrupt a future exchange. Timing and the held-for-investment standard still matter. Confirm the treatment with your CPA.

## Glossary

- **Disregarded Entity:** An entity, such as a single-member LLC, ignored as separate from its owner for tax; the owner is the taxpayer.
- **Single-Member LLC:** An LLC with one owner, generally disregarded for tax and therefore easier to use in a 1031 exchange.
- **Multi-Member LLC:** An LLC with multiple owners, generally taxed as a partnership and treated as a separate taxpayer.
- **Partnership:** An entity that is its own taxpayer, which complicates separate exchanges by individual partners.
- **Same-Taxpayer Requirement:** The seller of relinquished property must acquire the replacement as the same taxpayer.
- **Taxpayer:** The person or entity treated as owner for tax; for a partnership it is the entity, while for a disregarded LLC it is the owner.
- **Entity-Level Exchange:** An exchange completed by the partnership itself, keeping partners together in the replacement.
- **Drop-and-Swap:** A partnership distribution before sale that can give partners direct property interests to exchange.
- **Swap-and-Drop:** An entity-level exchange followed by a distribution to partners.
- **Tenant-in-Common (TIC):** A direct fractional ownership interest in property, into which partnership interests may be converted.
- **Liability Protection:** The shield from liabilities provided by an LLC; it can be compatible with 1031 treatment through disregarded entities.
- **Titling:** The way ownership of the replacement property is held, which must satisfy the same-taxpayer rule.
- **Tax Classification:** The tax treatment—disregarded, partnership, or corporation—that identifies the taxpayer.
- **Pass-Through Entity:** An LLC or partnership whose income passes to owners; exchange treatment depends on its member count and classification.
- **Partner Separation:** Partners pursuing different exchange outcomes, which requires specialized planning.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party holding proceeds so the taxpayer does not take constructive receipt.
