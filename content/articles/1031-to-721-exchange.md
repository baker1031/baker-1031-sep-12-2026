# The Two-Step 1031-to-721 Exchange: DST Now, UPREIT Later

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › The Two-Step 1031-to-721 Exchange: DST Now, UPREIT Later

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read**

I keep seeing the same question behind a sale: can an owner put the landlord work down now without permanently closing every door tomorrow? The two-step strategy is built for that question. It can defer tax today, preserve flexibility for a period, and later move an investor into a diversified, semi-liquid REIT position without a taxable event between the two steps.

First-order thinking calls that a tax strategy. Second-order thinking asks when the flexibility is actually surrendered, what liquidity really means, and which future decision brings the tax bill back into view. This memo explains how the two steps fit together, when tax can come due, and where the strategy can disappoint.

## Key Takeaways

- Step one is a 1031 exchange into a DST, which defers tax and keeps the investor in fractional real estate that could still be exchanged again.
- Step two is a 721 exchange at the DST's full-cycle, contributing the property to a REIT's operating partnership for OP units.
- The sequence defers tax through both steps; tax is triggered only later, when OP units are converted to shares or redeemed for cash.
- It is a one-way path: once in OP units, an investor generally cannot 1031 again. Its value is preserving flexibility until the investor deliberately gives it up.

## Why the two-step strategy exists

Each tool fills a gap in the other. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) preserves flexibility, but it leaves the investor owning real estate—directly or fractionally through a DST—that is illiquid and, in a single DST, fairly concentrated. A [721 exchange](721-exchange-vs-1031-exchange.html) provides diversification and eventual liquidity inside a REIT, but it is a one-way door that an investor may not want to enter too early.

The two-step puts the tools in sequence: 1031 flexibility first, 721 diversification later, and tax deferral throughout. It is commonly used by investors winding down active ownership gradually rather than all at once: sell a building, place the proceeds in passive real estate, and eventually consolidate into a REIT for simplicity and an estate that is easier to divide. It is a glide path from active landlord to passive, diversified investor, with the tax bill deferred across the whole transition.

## Step one: the 1031 into a DST

The first step is a conventional 1031 exchange into a DST. The investor sells property, the qualified intermediary holds the proceeds, the replacement property is identified within **45** days, and the exchange closes within **180** days. The investor then owns a fractional beneficial interest in a professionally managed, institutional-quality property.

IRS Revenue Ruling **2004-86** treats a properly structured DST interest as direct real-estate ownership. That treatment can fully defer capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture, just as a direct-property 1031 would. [Our memo on the 1031 into a DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) covers that step in detail.

At this point, the investor has gained passivity and, if capital is split among several DSTs, diversification while retaining 1031 optionality. At full-cycle, the DST interest could be exchanged into other real estate. The two-step has not committed the investor to the REIT; it has established the option.

## Step two: the 721 into the REIT

The second step occurs at the DST's full-cycle. Many DSTs are structured to be “UPREIT-eligible,” meaning the sponsor's affiliated REIT has the option to acquire the DST property and offer investors a 721 exchange. Rather than cashing out or completing another 1031, the investor contributes the interest to the REIT's operating partnership and receives **OP units**.

The contribution is generally tax-free under Section **721**, so the deferral established in step one carries forward—now inside a diversified REIT rather than a single DST. OP units pay distributions that track the REIT's dividends. After a holding period, the units can usually be converted into REIT shares or redeemed for cash. That is where tax can eventually come due; the move from DST to REIT itself is designed to be a non-taxable continuation of the deferral.

## The tax timing across both steps

Neither step is intended to trigger tax. The 1031 into the DST defers tax; the 721 into the REIT defers it again. The original gain and the depreciation recapture continue in the investor's basis through both transactions.

Tax is generally triggered only by a later, separate event: converting OP units into REIT shares, redeeming them for cash, or, depending on the structure, a REIT sale of the contributed property that passes through built-in gain. Each may recognize some or all of the deferred gain. If the investor holds OP units until death, heirs may receive a stepped-up basis that can eliminate the deferred gain altogether—the same estate-planning endgame offered through a 1031, reached through the REIT.

The point is practical: identify the future action that could trigger the bill, and plan conversions deliberately rather than drifting into a taxable redemption.

## The benefits of the sequence

Stacked together, the two steps offer immediate tax deferral on a property sale; passivity when the investor enters the DST; diversification that can expand from one DST to an entire REIT portfolio; eventual liquidity through OP-unit conversion, which neither a directly owned building nor a single DST supplies; and an estate-friendly end state. OP units are easier to divide among heirs than a building and can be converted to shares as needed.

For the right investor, it is flexible early, simpler and more diversified later, and tax-deferred throughout. That is why UPREIT-eligible DSTs have become a mainstream destination for 1031 proceeds rather than a niche.

## The trade-offs and risks

The cost is real. Most important is the **one-way door**: once step two is complete and the investor holds OP units, the investor generally cannot 1031 back into real estate. The flexibility preserved in step one has been spent.

There is also **REIT dependence**. The outcome moves from a specific property to the performance and management of the whole REIT. OP-unit “liquidity,” especially in a non-traded REIT, runs through limited redemption programs and a taxable conversion, not a free public market.

The ordinary risks of both stages remain. Step one brings DST illiquidity and fees. Step two brings the REIT's leverage, valuation, and distribution-sustainability risk. This does not argue against the two-step; it argues for using it deliberately, with clear eyes, and only when the eventual move into the REIT is a result the investor actually wants. [Our memo on 721 exchange downsides](721-exchange-downsides.html) catalogues the second-step risks.

## Who the two-step fits

The sequence can fit an investor transitioning out of active real estate over time, seeking tax deferral now while preserving options, and expecting ultimately to value diversification and liquidity more than control. That can include an investor approaching or in retirement or simplifying an estate.

It fits poorly for someone who wants to exchange real estate indefinitely, for whom serial 1031 exchanges are better, or for someone who may need full liquidity before the DST and REIT timelines allow. Because the route is deliberate and one-directional, each step should be a decision rather than a default: enter the DST knowing the REIT is the likely destination, and take the 721 only when ready to make the transition permanent.

## A worked example

Consider an illustrative investor nearing retirement who sells a long-held rental with a large gain. **Figures hypothetical.** In step one, she 1031s the proceeds into two DSTs—an apartment portfolio and an industrial offering—deferring all tax and immediately ending landlord duties. She holds for several years, collects distributions, and retains the option to exchange again.

When the apartment DST reaches full-cycle, the sponsor's REIT offers a 721 exchange. Ready to consolidate, she contributes her interest for OP units and continues the deferral inside a diversified REIT. She lives on the distributions and, because she plans to hold the units for life, plans to pass them to her children with a stepped-up basis—potentially erasing the gain deferred since the original sale. In this illustration, the two steps deliver their intended sequence: flexibility first, permanence later, and no tax in between.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the two-step 1031-to-721 exchange?

It is a sequence: first, a 1031 exchange into a DST to defer tax and remain flexible; then, at the DST's full-cycle, a 721 exchange that contributes the property to a REIT's operating partnership for OP units and continues the deferral.

### Does the two-step trigger any tax?

Neither step does: the 1031 and the 721 both defer. Tax is generally triggered later when OP units are converted to REIT shares or redeemed for cash, or potentially when the REIT sells the contributed property.

### Can I still 1031 after the 721 step?

Generally, no. Once an investor holds OP units, the investor cannot 1031 back into real estate. That is why the two-step keeps flexibility through step one and requires commitment only at step two.

### Why not just 721 directly?

A direct 721 commits the investor immediately, and the REIT would usually need to want the specific property. The DST step can defer tax now, keep the investor passive and diversified, and preserve 1031 optionality until the investor is ready to make the move permanent.

### Who is the two-step best for?

It can fit investors moving out of active real estate over time who want tax deferral now, flexibility, and eventual diversification and liquidity over control—often those approaching retirement or simplifying an estate.

## Glossary

**UPREIT-Eligible DST**  
A DST structured so the sponsor's REIT can acquire its property and offer investors a 721 exchange at full-cycle.

**721 Exchange**  
A tax-deferred contribution of property to a REIT's operating partnership for OP units.

**OP Units**  
Operating-partnership units that pay distributions tracking REIT dividends and can later be converted to shares (taxably).

**Full-Cycle**  
The point at which a DST's property is sold or contributed and the program concludes.

**Step-Up in Basis**  
The reset of an asset's basis to fair market value at death, which can eliminate deferred gain for heirs.
