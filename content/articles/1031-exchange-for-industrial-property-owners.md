# 1031 Exchange for Industrial Property Owners

I keep seeing industrial owners face the same pleasant but demanding problem: a property that has appreciated so much that holding, selling, and exchanging all look reasonable. The gain is real. So is the risk of letting a strong sector story substitute for careful replacement underwriting.

Industrial real estate—warehouses, distribution centers, fulfillment sites, and logistics facilities—has benefited from e-commerce, logistics, and supply-chain investment. That has produced appreciation and rent growth for many owners. A 1031 exchange can let an owner capture the value of that appreciation while deferring tax, whether the goal is a larger asset, a better location, diversification, or a more passive form of ownership.

First-order thinking is, “Industrial has been strong, so I should stay in industrial.” Second-order thinking is, “Does this particular building work for today’s users, is the tenant and lease sound, how much local supply is coming, and does a direct property or DST actually fit my need for control and liquidity?” The exchange can preserve capital. It should not remove the need to earn the next investment decision.

## The industrial sector and exchanges

E-commerce and modern logistics have increased demand for the physical infrastructure behind online retail and supply chains: warehouses, distribution centers, fulfillment facilities, and last-mile delivery locations. That demand has supported appreciation and rent growth in industrial property and has drawn more investor attention to the sector.

For an owner of appreciated industrial property, the decision is often whether to hold, sell, or exchange. Holding preserves current exposure. A taxable sale captures gains but can create a meaningful tax bill. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) lets the owner reposition the capital in qualifying real estate without current recognition of the qualifying gain.

Industrial is also a common replacement target for exchangers leaving other sectors. They may be looking for growth, creditworthy national logistics, distribution, or manufacturing tenants, and the relatively passive income profile that can come with long or net leases. Industrial therefore shows up on both sides of the exchange: as appreciated property owners sell and as replacement property others seek.

## Why industrial owners exchange

The first reason is tax-deferred capture of appreciation. An owner with substantial embedded gain can redeploy full qualifying equity rather than the amount remaining after a taxable sale.

The second is repositioning. An owner may exchange an older or smaller facility for a newer, larger, or better-located distribution asset. They may diversify a single industrial holding across more properties or sectors, or move toward stronger logistics markets.

The third is passivity. Owners who want less management work or who want to convert industrial gains into a passive form can exchange into industrial DSTs or other passive real-estate holdings. The exchange can support scaling, optimization, or a step back from active ownership while keeping capital invested.

The exchange lets industrial owners capture strong sector appreciation while deferring tax, repositioning, upgrading, or going passive without the tax drag of a sale.

## Trade up in industrial

Trading up is a common strategy. An owner of a smaller or older industrial building can use the equity preserved through deferral to acquire a larger, newer, or better-located facility. Strong appreciation can create the buying power for a meaningful trade-up into a modern logistics or distribution asset.

Modern specifications matter. Older buildings with low clear height, limited loading, or weak locations may be less competitive with facilities that offer high clear heights, ample loading docks, trailer parking, and prime logistics-corridor access. The exchange can be used to move from obsolete or constrained space into a building that better fits today’s logistics users.

Trading up can also mean scale: one facility becomes a larger one or a portfolio. Repeated exchanges can allow an investor to compound industrial holdings over time. The tax deferral is useful, but the new building’s functionality and tenant demand still have to justify the higher price.

## Industrial as replacement property

Industrial can be an attractive replacement for owners coming from many real-estate sectors. The appeal often includes strong sector fundamentals, frequent net-lease or long-lease structures, and tenants that may include national logistics, distribution, e-commerce, and manufacturing companies.

The underwriting is specific. Consider:

- **Location:** access to highways, ports, rail, airports, population centers, and logistics corridors.
- **Building functionality:** clear height, loading, truck courts, configuration, floor capacity, columns, and power for modern logistics use.
- **Tenant and lease:** tenant credit, lease term, rent structure, and the distinction between net and gross obligations.
- **Local supply and demand:** current and proposed supply, vacancy, re-leasing prospects, and whether the submarket serves a real logistics need.

Modern, well-located facilities with strong tenants and long leases can have a different risk profile from older, poorly located, or speculative buildings. Industrial’s general reputation does not eliminate property-level work.

Within exchange deadlines, direct assets can be sourced through brokers and marketplaces. Industrial DST sponsors can provide another route. Because industrial is popular, it is prudent to have candidates and a backup, such as an industrial DST, identified before the deadline pressure becomes acute.

## Industrial DSTs

An industrial DST may own warehouses, distribution centers, or logistics facilities, sometimes as a portfolio across properties and markets. It gives the investor fractional, passive exposure to institutional-quality industrial real estate without the work of finding and managing an individual building.

The potential advantages are access, diversification, and passivity. A portfolio DST can spread exposure among properties, tenants, and markets. It may hold modern distribution centers or logistics facilities leased to national tenants—assets an individual investor may not easily acquire alone. The sponsor handles management.

The trade-offs are important. Industrial DSTs are securities, are sold to accredited investors after a suitability review, offer no investor control, and are illiquid. For an owner moving from active industrial ownership, that may be an acceptable exchange for less work and broader exposure. It remains a decision about capital, control, and liquidity, not merely a way to meet an exchange deadline.

## Key takeaways

- Industrial real estate has generated substantial gains for many warehouse, distribution, and logistics owners.
- Owners use exchanges to capture appreciation tax-deferred, upgrade and scale facilities, diversify, or transition to passive ownership.
- Industrial attracts replacement buyers for its fundamentals, long- or net-lease structures, and potentially creditworthy tenants.
- Building function, location, tenant and lease, and local supply-demand conditions determine the quality of a specific replacement.
- Industrial DSTs can provide passive, diversified institutional exposure, but they are illiquid, no-control securities for accredited investors.

## Tax considerations

Industrial sales face the standard four-layer tax stack: capital gains, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), [net investment income tax (NIIT)](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. Strong industrial appreciation can make the amount deferred through a qualifying exchange particularly meaningful.

Commercial industrial real property is depreciated over **39 years**. [Cost segregation](cost-segregation.html) is common in industrial ownership. It can accelerate depreciation during the holding period, but it can also increase recapture exposure at sale. A properly structured exchange defers recapture associated with qualifying real property as well as the qualifying gain.

There is a property-composition issue, too. Some facilities contain specialized machinery, racking, or other personal property. Since the **2017** TCJA, personal property does not qualify for like-kind exchange; only real property does. The CPA should allocate any significant personal-property value from exchangeable real property and determine the resulting tax treatment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is industrial a popular sector for exchanges?

Warehouses, distribution facilities, and logistics property have been strong commercial sectors, supported by e-commerce and logistics demand. That has created gains for owners and made industrial appealing as a replacement for investors seeking sector strength, passive-income structures, and potentially creditworthy tenants.

### Why do industrial owners use 1031 exchanges?

They use them to capture appreciation while deferring qualifying tax, trade up into modern or better-located facilities, diversify, change markets, or move into passive DST interests.

### What does trading up in industrial mean?

It means exchanging a smaller or older facility for a larger, newer, or better-located one. It can involve high clear heights, ample loading, trailer parking, and logistics-corridor access, or scaling a single facility into a larger asset or portfolio.

### What makes a good industrial replacement property?

Evaluate transportation and population access, the building’s clear height, loading and configuration, the tenant and lease, and local supply-demand conditions. Modern, well-located properties with strong tenants and long leases can be less risky than obsolete, poorly located, or speculative industrial property.

### What is an industrial DST?

It is a DST that owns industrial property such as warehouses, distribution centers, or logistics facilities, sometimes in a diversified multi-property portfolio. It offers passive exposure without direct sourcing or management, but requires accredited-investor status, suitability review, and acceptance of illiquidity and no control.

### Do industrial properties have personal-property issues in exchanges?

Sometimes. Specialized machinery, racking, and other equipment may be personal property. Since **2017**, only real property qualifies for a 1031 exchange, so the CPA should allocate significant personal-property value from the exchangeable real-property portion.

### How does depreciation recapture affect industrial owners?

Industrial real property is depreciated over **39 years**, and cost segregation can accelerate depreciation. Both can create recapture exposure in a taxable sale. A qualifying exchange defers real-property recapture along with qualifying gain, so owners should review the cost-segregation history with their CPA.

### Can I exchange industrial for other property types?

Yes. Like-kind treatment for investment real estate is broad. Industrial can be exchanged for multifamily, retail, net-lease, and other qualifying investment real estate, and other real-estate types can be exchanged into industrial.

### Is now a good time to exchange industrial?

That depends on current market conditions and the investor’s goals. The source does not predict markets. Owners may consider exchanging when they have substantial gains and want to capture them, upgrade, reposition, or become passive, but current information and advisors should guide the decision.

### Can I transition from active industrial to passive ownership?

Yes. An industrial DST or another passive holding can replace directly managed industrial property. The investor can retain industrial exposure or diversify while shedding management work and preserving qualifying exchange deferral.

### Why are industrial tenants considered strong?

Industrial tenants are often national logistics, distribution, e-commerce, or manufacturing companies on long-term, sometimes net, leases. That can support relatively passive income, but individual tenant credit and lease terms still require review.

### Should I choose direct industrial or an industrial DST?

Direct ownership provides control of a specific property but requires sourcing and management and concentrates risk. A DST provides diversified, sponsor-managed exposure but is illiquid and gives the investor no control. The choice turns on control versus diversification and passivity.

### What building features matter most in industrial?

Clear height, dock and drive-in loading, trailer parking, truck court depth, floor-load capacity, column spacing, and power capacity. Modern logistical function—especially clear height and loading—can matter as much as location.

### Why does location matter so much for industrial?

Industrial value depends heavily on access to highways, ports, rail, airports, population centers, and logistics corridors. A connected location can be more valuable and re-leasable than an isolated one, particularly for last-mile or distribution use.

### Is industrial overbuilt or still a good investment?

Conditions vary by submarket and over time. Some markets may have substantial new supply while others are tight. Assess current supply-demand conditions for the specific market and property rather than relying on the sector’s broad reputation.

### Can I exchange land or a different property into industrial?

Yes. Land, retail, multifamily, and other investment real estate can be exchanged into industrial property held for investment or productive use, subject to the usual exchange rules.

### What lease structures are common in industrial?

Net and triple-net structures are common, with tenants paying some or all of taxes, insurance, and maintenance. Single-tenant industrial often uses longer net leases; multi-tenant property may have shorter leases and more active leasing. Lease structure, term, and tenant determine how passive and reliable the income may be.

## Glossary

- **Industrial Real Estate:** Warehouses, distribution centers, and logistics facilities.
- **Distribution Center:** A facility for storing and distributing goods.
- **Logistics Corridor:** A prime industrial location near transportation and population.
- **Clear Height:** Warehouse ceiling height; higher clearance can suit modern logistics.
- **Last-Mile Facility:** Industrial property used for final delivery, often tied to e-commerce.
- **Trading Up:** Exchanging into a larger, newer, or better-located industrial facility.
- **39-Year Depreciation:** The recovery period for commercial, including industrial, real property.
- **Cost Segregation:** Accelerating depreciation in a way that can increase recapture exposure.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, deferred through a qualifying exchange.
- **Personal Property:** Equipment or machinery not eligible for exchange under the TCJA rule.
- **Real-vs-Personal Allocation:** Dividing industrial value into exchangeable real property and non-exchangeable personal property.
- **Industrial DST:** A DST holding industrial property for passive sector exposure.
- **Net Lease:** A lease structure common in industrial that can provide passive income.
- **Creditworthy Tenant:** A strong logistics or distribution tenant that can support reliable income.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax—the taxes a qualifying exchange can defer.
- **Replacement Property:** The property acquired in an exchange; industrial can be a sought-after choice.
