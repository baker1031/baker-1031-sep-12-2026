# State-Specific 1031 Rules: California Clawback & More

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep coming back to a simple problem in cross-state exchanges: a closing can look clean, the federal deferral can be sound, and the state-tax work can still be waiting years down the road. The paperwork at sale is visible. The state consequences often are not.

The first-order view is that a 1031 exchange is one federal rulebook. The second-order reality is that the states can have their own income-tax rates, reporting obligations, withholding procedures, and claims to deferred gain. That is why a cross-state exchange should be planned as a federal and state transaction together, not as a federal transaction with a state footnote.

## Why State Rules Matter

The federal rules are the same everywhere. The deadlines, the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) requirement, and the like-kind standard are set by the Internal Revenue Code. Most states conform to the federal deferral, so they also defer state tax on gain at the exchange.

But conformity is not the whole answer. States differ in the tax rate that would otherwise apply, the reports they require, and whether they preserve a future claim to gain from in-state property after that property is exchanged out of state. Those differences matter most when the source state has an income tax and when the exchange moves capital across borders.

An exchange within one conforming state is usually more straightforward. A move from a high-tax state into another state raises a different set of questions: which state may tax the gain, when can it do so, and does a state retain a claim after the replacement property leaves its borders? The general federal mechanics are explained in this [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html), but state treatment calls for its own review.

California is the familiar example. A federal exchange can be valid and federal gain can remain deferred, yet California can tax its originally deferred California-source gain years later when an out-of-state replacement is sold. That delayed result is exactly why investors should map the facts with a CPA before completing [a 1031 exchange across state lines](1031-exchange-across-state-lines.html).

## California's Clawback Provision

California has the best-known clawback rule. If a California taxpayer, or an owner of California-source property, exchanges into replacement property outside California, the state generally conforms at the exchange: California tax is not due at that point. California does not, however, lose track of the deferred gain.

The taxpayer must file an annual information return, FTB Form 3840, for as long as the out-of-state replacement is held. The filing reports the deferred California-source gain and keeps that gain on California's record.

The trigger is a later taxable sale of the out-of-state replacement, not another qualifying exchange. On that sale, California taxes the originally deferred California-source gain even if the replacement property is outside California and the taxpayer no longer has another California connection. California defers; it does not forgive.

That is a material planning fact. The owner must file FTB 3840 every year, and a failure to file can cause California to assess the deferred gain. A further exchange can continue deferral, but moving the replacement out of state does not eliminate California's later claim. Investors leaving California property should make the eventual California tax, the annual filing, and the possibility of another exchange part of a long-term plan with a CPA familiar with California rules.

## Other Clawback and Reporting States

California is prominent, but it is not the only state with a reason to track gain that originated inside its borders. Massachusetts, Montana, and Oregon, among others, have used clawback-style rules or special reporting requirements for 1031 exchanges involving out-of-state replacement property. The policy concern is familiar: without a continuing claim, a state can lose tax on gain from in-state real estate after an owner exchanges out of state.

The mechanics are not uniform. One state may require annual reporting similar to FTB 3840. Another may recover deferred gain on a later out-of-state sale, or apply a distinct threshold or procedure. Rules and the list of affected states can evolve. The answer depends on the particular source state, the replacement state, and the facts of the exchange.

The practical lesson is not that every cross-state exchange produces a clawback. It is that any exchange leaving an income-tax state deserves a state-specific check. A CPA familiar with the relevant states can identify filings, help avoid missed-filing penalties, and estimate the longer-term state-tax exposure. California is the headline; it is not the entire story.

## No-Income-Tax State Considerations

States such as Texas, Florida, Washington, Nevada, Wyoming, and South Dakota, along with a few others, do not levy a state income tax. Property located there does not create a state-level gain tax or a state clawback in that jurisdiction. An exchange of property in a no-income-tax state therefore has no state-income-tax layer from that state, although federal rules still apply.

That can simplify a destination, but it does not erase a source state's retained claim. A California owner who exchanges California property into a no-income-tax state still faces California clawback when the replacement is sold in a taxable transaction. The destination simply does not add a new state income tax of its own on future replacement income or sale.

The reverse is also worth seeing: an owner from a no-income-tax state can acquire replacement property in a high-tax state and take on that replacement state's future exposure. And the taxpayer's residence state may tax its residents on income from all sources, often allowing a credit for tax paid to a source state. A property in a no-income-tax state can therefore still produce residence-state tax for an owner who lives in a taxing state.

## Planning Cross-State Exchanges

The durable way to analyze a cross-state exchange is to map three places before the sale: the source state where the relinquished property sits, the residence state where the taxpayer lives, and the replacement state where the new property will sit. The source state may have clawback or annual-reporting rules. The residence state may tax the gain with a credit. The replacement state affects future income and sale tax.

If the source state has a clawback, plan for annual reporting such as FTB 3840 and for the later tax on a taxable sale, or for continued exchanges if further deferral is the intent. If the replacement is moving into a high-tax state, include its future tax treatment. If it is moving into a no-income-tax state, recognize the source-state claim may remain even though the destination adds no state-income-tax layer.

Federal deferral and state treatment should be understood together. A replacement location is not just a real-estate choice; it can be part of the after-tax plan. The useful question is not merely whether an exchange can close. It is whether the investor understands the compliance trail and future tax that follows the capital.

## Key Takeaways

- Federal 1031 rules are uniform, while state income tax, reporting, withholding, and clawback rules vary.
- California requires annual FTB 3840 reporting of deferred California-source gain in an out-of-state replacement and can tax that gain on a later taxable sale.
- Massachusetts, Montana, Oregon, and other states may have their own reporting or clawback approaches. No-income-tax states remove their own state-income-tax layer but not another source state's claim.
- Before proceeding, map the source state, residence state, and replacement state with a CPA.

## Ongoing Compliance After the Exchange

The state work can continue long after the exchange closes. California's FTB 3840 is an annual return for every year the out-of-state replacement acquired with California-source gain is held. It is not a one-time closing document. A missed filing may lead California to treat the deferred gain as recognized, accelerating the clawback and potentially adding penalties.

If the owner makes another qualifying exchange instead of selling, the deferral and the reporting obligation continue. If the owner sells in a taxable transaction, California tax comes due on the deferred California-source gain. The exchange is therefore not truly finished from the state's perspective until the deferred gain has been resolved.

Chains of exchanges can make this more involved. An owner can have deferred gains from multiple source states moving through several replacement properties. Continuing CPA support is useful because it keeps the record of state-specific deferred gain, annual filings, and future tax obligations from disappearing after the original closing.

## State Withholding on the Sale

Clawback is not the only state-level issue. Some states, including California, require the buyer or closing agent to withhold a percentage of a real-estate sale price for state tax. That withholding can apply on the sale leg even when an owner is attempting a 1031 exchange and does not expect current tax.

States with withholding often provide an exemption or exception for a 1031 exchange, but the exemption must be claimed with the appropriate closing paperwork, usually a certification that the transaction is like-kind. If it is not properly claimed, the state can retain part of the sale proceeds. That can create an avoidable cash-flow problem and potentially produce [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html) if the withheld money does not reach the exchange.

The qualified intermediary, closing agent, and CPA should coordinate any applicable withholding exemption before closing. This does not alter federal eligibility, but it can affect whether the full proceeds are available to acquire the replacement property. It is a small procedural detail with an outsized effect if missed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do 1031 exchange rules vary by state?

Federal deadlines, the qualified intermediary requirement, and the like-kind standard are uniform. State income-tax treatment is not. Most states conform to the federal deferral, but the rate, reports, and treatment of deferred gain on out-of-state replacements can differ. Those differences are most important in cross-state exchanges.

### What is California's 1031 clawback?

When California property is exchanged into out-of-state property, California generally defers the gain at the exchange, requires annual FTB Form 3840 reporting, and taxes the deferred California-source gain when the replacement is later sold in a taxable transaction. California tracks the gain and can reclaim the tax later.

### What is FTB Form 3840?

FTB Form 3840 is California's annual information return for deferred gain from an exchange of California property into out-of-state replacement property. It must be filed every year for as long as the replacement is held. A failure to file can lead California to treat the gain as recognized and accelerate the clawback.

### When does California's clawback actually tax me?

It applies when the out-of-state replacement is sold in a taxable transaction, rather than through another qualifying exchange. The originally deferred California-source gain can then be taxable to California even though the property is out of state. Continued exchanges continue deferral; a taxable cash-out is the trigger.

### Do other states have clawback rules?

Yes. California is the most visible example, but Massachusetts, Montana, Oregon, and other states may have clawback or special reporting rules. The details vary, so the source state's current requirements should be checked for every cross-state exchange.

### Which states have no income tax?

Texas, Florida, Washington, Nevada, Wyoming, and South Dakota are examples of states with no state income tax. They do not impose state-level gain tax or a state clawback on property there. That simplifies their state layer, although federal rules and another state's source claim can still matter.

### Does moving to a no-income-tax state avoid the clawback?

No. If an owner exchanges out of a clawback state such as California, the source state's claim can remain when the replacement is later sold. The no-income-tax destination may avoid adding a new tax of its own, but it does not cancel California's deferred-gain claim.

### Does my state of residence tax the gain?

It can. Most states tax residents on income from all sources and commonly provide a credit for source-state tax to avoid double taxation. An investor who lives in a taxing state may therefore have residence-state tax exposure even if the property is in a no-income-tax state. A CPA should map the interaction.

### How do I plan a cross-state 1031 exchange?

Start with a CPA-led map of the source state, residence state, and replacement state. Identify clawback, annual reporting, withholding, credits, and future state tax before the sale. The federal timetable remains important, but a complete plan includes the state consequences through the replacement's life.

### What happens if I do not file FTB 3840?

California may treat the failure as recognition of the deferred gain, assess the tax, and potentially impose penalties. The return is annual for the entire out-of-state replacement hold, which is why it belongs on an ongoing compliance calendar rather than in a closing folder.

### Do state rules change whether I should exchange?

Usually they shape the after-tax result and long-term plan rather than remove the value of federal deferral. Clawback states still defer state tax at the exchange; they may reclaim it later. The decision should be made with that later obligation visible rather than with the expectation that crossing a border makes it disappear.

### Can I defer a state clawback indefinitely?

Continued qualifying exchanges can continue the deferral, because a clawback such as California's generally applies on a taxable sale of the replacement. A step-up in basis at death can erase deferred gain, including clawback exposure. The tax is deferred for as long as the exchange chain continues, subject to the investor's facts and applicable law.

### Is state tax withheld when I sell in a 1031 exchange?

Some states, including California, require withholding of a percentage of sale price for state tax. They generally provide a 1031 exemption, but it must be properly claimed at closing. Without it, proceeds can be withheld, creating cash-flow pressure and potential boot issues.

### How do I claim a state withholding exemption for my exchange?

Provide the state-required certification that the transaction is a like-kind exchange at closing. The form differs by state. The QI, closing agent, and CPA should coordinate the documentation so available proceeds flow into the exchange rather than to a withholding account.

### Does state withholding affect my exchange's cash flow?

Yes. If an exemption is not claimed, the withheld amount is held by the state instead of funding the replacement acquisition. That can impair the cash available for the exchange and can cause boot complications. Proper documentation at closing protects the flow of proceeds.

### Do all states have clawback or withholding rules?

No. Some states have both, some have one or neither, and no-income-tax states have no state-level gain tax. Requirements differ and can change, so state-specific CPA advice is needed for the states actually involved.

## Glossary

**Clawback Provision:** A state rule taxing previously deferred gain when an out-of-state replacement is later sold.

**FTB Form 3840:** California's annual information return reporting deferred gain on out-of-state 1031 replacements.

**Source State:** The state where the relinquished property is located and which may have clawback or reporting rules.

**Residence State:** The taxpayer's home state, which may tax the gain with a credit for source-state tax.

**Conformity:** A state's adoption of the federal 1031 deferral for state income-tax purposes.

**No-Income-Tax State:** A state without an income tax, such as Texas or Florida, and therefore without state-level gain tax or clawback.

**Cross-State Exchange:** An exchange that relinquishes property in one state and acquires it in another.

**Double Taxation Credit:** A credit for tax paid to one state against another state's tax on the same gain.

**California-Source Gain:** Gain attributable to California property that California tracks and can claw back.

**Annual Reporting Requirement:** The obligation to file, such as FTB 3840, each year a deferred out-of-state replacement is held.

**Deferred Gain:** Gain not recognized at the exchange and tracked by a clawback state for later taxation.

**Recognition Trigger:** An event, such as a taxable sale or a missed filing, that causes deferred gain to be taxed.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of basis at death that can erase deferred gain, including state clawback exposure.

**Like-Kind:** The federal standard requiring exchanged property to be investment real property.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that facilitates the federal exchange uniformly across states.

**Multi-State Analysis:** A CPA's map of the source, residence, and replacement state tax consequences.
