# DST Liquidity: The Secondary Market Reality

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep finding that liquidity gets treated as a footnote until someone needs money. With a DST, it is a primary investment term: the capital is committed until the sponsor sells the underlying property.

Delaware Statutory Trust interests are illiquid by design. Unlike a publicly traded stock or REIT, an investor cannot readily sell a DST interest on demand. The intended hold normally runs five to seven years or more through the property sale. There is no robust, established secondary market. Occasional secondary sales may occur, but they are not assured and typically require a discount to underlying value. DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after suitability review; distributions and returns are never guaranteed, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

Liquidity is one of the trade-offs discussed in [our comprehensive Delaware Statutory Trust primer](dst-guide.html).

## Why DSTs are illiquid

A DST holds one or a few particular properties through a defined life. The sponsor acquires and manages the real estate for a multi-year period, then sells and returns capital to investors. The interest is a fractional beneficial interest in finite-life property, not a continuously traded share with an exchange where it can be sold whenever an investor wants.

The illiquidity also supports the structure’s 1031 tax treatment. A DST used in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) operates under restrictions often called the seven deadly sins, including limits on raising new capital or actively reworking the investment. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 supports its 1031-eligible treatment. DST interests are private securities offered to [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html)s, not listed securities. The hold-to-sale design is therefore a built-in characteristic, not a temporary market problem.

## Is there a DST secondary market?

There is no established, robust secondary market for DST interests. Publicly traded stocks and REITs have exchanges, continuous pricing, and active buyers and sellers. DST interests have no central marketplace, no continuous pricing, and no assurance of a buyer at the time an investor wants to exit.

Occasional secondary sales can occur. A sponsor or third party may help an investor who has changed circumstances find a buyer before full cycle. Such transactions are sporadic, depend on a willing buyer and acceptable price, and cannot be treated as a reliable source of liquidity.

First-order thinking is that an occasional secondary sale means the interest can be sold if necessary. Second-order thinking recognizes that availability, price, timing, buyer demand, and terms are all uncertain. The rational planning assumption is that the secondary market does not exist for the investor’s liquidity plan.

> Treat the DST secondary market as if it does not exist. An occasional sale is not a dependable exit mechanism.

## Discounts on secondary sales

When an early sale does occur, the seller often accepts a discount to the interest’s paper or underlying value. There is limited demand for used DST interests, the interest may be difficult to value without a current property appraisal, and the buyer accepts the remaining lock-up. A seller who needs out has limited negotiating leverage.

The discount is not predictable. It depends on the property, the particular interest, remaining hold, buyer appetite, and the urgency of the seller. A motivated seller may need to take a meaningful haircut just to find a buyer. That can lock in a loss compared with holding to full cycle. An early exit is thus both uncertain and potentially costly.

## Planning for the full hold period

The appropriate investment plan assumes the full hold, usually five to seven years or more, until the sponsor completes the sale. Only patient capital—funds an investor does not expect to need—belongs in a DST. Living expenses, emergencies, and known near-term obligations need liquid savings or other accessible investments.

The projected hold is not a guarantee. A sponsor may sell earlier if circumstances are favorable, or may hold longer to avoid selling in a weak market. The capital can remain committed beyond the original expectation. Illiquid DSTs should therefore be a measured portion of overall assets, rather than a dominant allocation.

## Key Takeaways

- DSTs are finite-life, private real-estate investments with strict structural limits and no exchange for on-demand sale.
- There is no robust DST secondary market. Occasional secondary sales are sporadic, unreliable, and not a liquidity plan.
- An early secondary sale typically occurs at an unpredictable discount to underlying value and can lock in a loss.
- Plan for a full 5-7+ year hold, possibly longer; use only patient capital and maintain liquidity elsewhere.

## Liquidity alternatives to consider

The first alternative is separate liquid reserves outside the DST allocation: emergency savings and accessible liquid investments that can cover unexpected needs. Liquidity should come from elsewhere in the portfolio, not from the DST interest.

The second is laddering. Spreading DST capital across trusts with staggered projected exit dates can have portions of capital expected to return at intervals rather than at one date. The dates remain projections, but laddering can spread reinvestment and expected capital-return timing.

The third is allocation sizing. Keep the DST sleeve measured so the remainder of the portfolio is sufficiently liquid and flexible. Liquid reserves, laddering, and appropriate sizing work together to manage the structural lock-up without relying on a secondary sale.

> The answer to DST illiquidity is not a hoped-for buyer. It is separate reserves, staggered expected exits, and an allocation small enough to leave untouched.

## Setting realistic liquidity expectations

An investor in a DST commits capital for the full hold. They should not expect to sell on demand, rely on a secondary market, or assume an early sale would be near paper value. If the money may be required before sponsor sale, a DST is not appropriate for those funds.

The expected hold itself is an estimate. A sponsor may extend it when selling into a weak market would be unfavorable. The investor should plan for the stated period plus flexibility beyond it. DSTs may provide 1031 deferral, passive income, diversification, and estate-planning benefits, but those features come with an actual lock-up. The appropriate expectation is clear-eyed patient capital, not temporary inconvenience.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are DSTs liquid?

No. DSTs are finite-life investments in one or a few properties, typically held for five to seven years or more until sponsor sale. They are private securities with no exchange, no central marketplace, no continuous pricing, and no assured early buyer. Their structural limits preserve 1031 treatment and reinforce the buy-and-hold design. If capital may be needed during the hold, DSTs are not the appropriate place for it.

### Is there a secondary market for DST interests?

There is no robust, established secondary market. Occasional sales may be facilitated by a sponsor or third party, but they are sporadic, require a willing buyer, and are not assured at the time of need. Unlike public REITs or stocks, DST interests do not have continuous trading or price discovery. Plan as though a secondary sale is unavailable.

### Can I sell my DST interest early?

An investor cannot count on it. An occasional buyer may be found, but availability is uncertain and an early sale often requires a discount to underlying value. The buyer assumes the remaining illiquidity and the seller may be motivated. The responsible approach is to invest only capital that can remain committed through full cycle and keep liquidity elsewhere.

### Why are DST secondary sales at a discount?

Limited buyer demand, lack of a liquid market, lack of a current appraisal, remaining hold-period risk, and the seller’s weak negotiating position all push price below paper value. The discount varies by interest, property, remaining term, buyer appetite, and seller urgency. It can be meaningful and can lock in a loss relative to holding to the planned sale.

### How long is a DST hold period?

The typical projection is roughly five to seven years, though holds can be shorter or longer. The sponsor manages the property and sells it at full cycle. The projected window is not a guaranteed end date: a favorable opportunity can lead to an earlier sale, while a weak market can lead the sponsor to hold longer. Treat the hold as a minimum expected commitment and confirm the expected period in each offering document.

### Should I plan to hold a DST to full cycle?

Yes. Full cycle means remaining invested until sponsor sale. With no robust secondary market and likely discount on any rare early sale, it is the only dependable exit. Commit only funds that can stay untouched through the entire expected hold and beyond it if needed; keep liquidity outside the DST allocation.

### What liquidity alternatives should I consider with DSTs?

Maintain separate liquid reserves for emergencies and near-term needs; ladder multiple DSTs with staggered projected exits; and keep illiquid DSTs to a measured share of the portfolio. Those choices provide liquidity outside the trusts and can spread expected capital-return timing. They do not make a DST itself liquid.

### What is laddering DSTs?

Laddering spreads capital across DSTs with different projected exit dates. Expected property sales and capital returns then occur at intervals rather than one time, creating opportunities to reinvest, spend proceeds, or rebalance. It also spreads reinvestment and market-timing exposure. Actual holds can differ from projections, so laddering does not create true liquidity and works best with separate reserves and appropriate sizing.

### What happens if I need my money before the DST sells?

There may be no dependable solution. The investor can try to find a buyer through an occasional secondary transaction, which is not guaranteed and may require a meaningful discount, or wait for full cycle. A change in circumstances does not create DST liquidity. Keep emergency reserves and accessible capital outside DSTs, avoid overcommitting to illiquid investments, and do not invest funds that might be needed before the hold ends.

### Are DSTs a good investment despite the illiquidity?

They can be suitable for investors with patient capital who value 1031 deferral, passive income, diversification, low minimums relative to whole property, non-recourse debt replacement, and estate-planning features. The lock-up is a genuine trade-off, not a hidden flaw. DSTs fit capital that can remain committed and do not fit capital that needs ready access. Suitability review assesses that match; past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Does the suitability review consider liquidity?

Yes. A broker-dealer suitability review considers financial situation, goals, risk tolerance, and liquidity needs. It asks whether the investor can leave the capital committed for the entire hold, especially if the expected five-to-seven-year period extends. Significant near-term needs or limited outside reserves can make a DST unsuitable. Liquidity is a core consideration, not an afterthought.

### How much of my portfolio should be in illiquid DSTs?

There is no universal amount. It depends on total assets, liquid reserves, time horizon, obligations, and risk tolerance. The governing principle is that DSTs should be a measured portion, leaving enough liquid assets elsewhere for expenses, emergencies, and known needs. Diversifying the DST sleeve and laddering exits can further manage concentration, but do not replace liquid capital. A financial advisor and suitability review can help determine an appropriate allocation.

### Can a DST hold last longer than expected?

Yes. The expected five-to-seven-year window is a projection. A sponsor may extend the hold to avoid an unfavorable weak-market sale, tying up capital longer, or may sell sooner if an opportunity arises. The illiquidity remains until actual sale. Plan for the full expected period and flexibility beyond it, not a fixed return-of-capital date.

### How are DSTs different from publicly traded REITs on liquidity?

A publicly traded REIT has exchange-listed shares, continuous pricing, and the ability to buy or sell quickly at the market price. A DST has no exchange, no continuous pricing, and no robust secondary market, and usually requires five to seven years or more until sale. A DST may be 1031-eligible like-kind real property, while a public REIT share is a security that does not provide 1031 eligibility. The choice is between ready liquidity without 1031 deferral and passive 1031-eligible real estate with a genuine lock-up.

## Glossary

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible real estate.

**Illiquidity:** The inability to readily sell a DST interest for cash.

**Liquidity:** The ability to access your capital readily when needed.

**Full Cycle:** A DST's hold from acquisition to the planned sale.

**Hold Period:** The multi-year span (often ~5-7+ years) before sale.

**Secondary Market:** A marketplace for resale — robust ones don't exist for DSTs.

**Secondary Sale:** An occasional, unreliable early sale of a DST interest.

**Discount:** The reduced price an early DST seller typically accepts.

**Underlying Value:** An interest's paper value before a secondary discount.

**Liquid Reserves:** Accessible savings kept outside illiquid DSTs.

**Laddering:** Staggering DST exits so capital returns at different times.

**Allocation Sizing:** Keeping illiquid DSTs a measured portion of a portfolio.

**Patient Capital:** Funds you can leave committed for the full hold.

**Seven Deadly Sins:** DST restrictions that preserve its 1031 tax treatment.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a DST's illiquidity fits the investor.
