# Student Housing DSTs: Pros, Cons & Risks

*Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I have been thinking about how quickly an attractive student-housing headline can obscure the work underneath it. A full property near a strong university can look reassuring. The more useful question is how it got full, how durable that demand is, and what happens if the next pre-leasing season misses its target.

The first-order view is that student housing is just multifamily with a dependable renter base. The second-order reality is that it is a specialized operating business: enrollment, campus proximity, by-the-bed leasing, annual turnover, and one concentrated leasing season all carry unusual weight. A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can give a 1031 investor passive exposure to that work, but passive ownership does not remove the operating and liquidity risks.

Student-housing DSTs hold purpose-built communities near colleges and universities. Investors own fractional beneficial interests that can qualify as like-kind real property in a 1031 exchange, while a sponsor and operator handle leasing, maintenance, and management. The appeal is university-anchored demand and a leasing model that can support income. The trade-offs are turnover, seasonality, dependence on one university, sponsor execution, financing, fees, and illiquidity.

DST interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after a suitability review. Distributions are projected, never guaranteed, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. The broader DST structure is covered in [our 2026 DST guide](dst-guide.html).

## The Student Housing Thesis

At large universities, on-campus housing often does not meet the number of students who want or need to live near campus. Purpose-built student housing fills that gap between dorm capacity and the broader rental market. These communities are designed for students, often with roommate-friendly unit layouts, amenities, and study spaces, and they are commonly located close to campus.

The renter base refreshes every year as new students arrive. That makes demand more closely tied to the institution than to the local job market. At a large, established university with stable or growing enrollment, strong applicant demand, and a national draw, that can create a recurring source of housing demand.

Leases are often by-the-bed: each student signs for an individual bedroom and shares common areas. Parents frequently co-sign or guarantee those leases. The structure can lift total revenue per unit and can limit the effect of one roommate's default on other leases. It does not make the cash flow certain. It makes the underwriting more dependent on pre-leasing and capable operations.

The thesis, then, is education-driven demand served by purpose-built communities near campus. It tends to be strongest where a durable university and a walkable property reinforce each other. It weakens where the school is shrinking, the property is distant, or the operator fails to execute through the leasing cycle.

## Enrollment & University Proximity

Enrollment and proximity to campus are the two central demand drivers. A student-housing property's renter base comes from a specific institution, so the institution's enrollment level and direction matter directly. Investors should study applicant demand, selectivity, financial health, and growth trajectory as closely as the physical property.

The stronger markets often surround large, well-established public universities with stable or rising enrollment and a national draw. The reverse is just as important. A decline in enrollment, an erosion in a school's appeal, or financial stress at the institution can soften housing demand with few alternative sources to offset it. This is single-university dependence in practical form.

Location is equally consequential. Pedestrian-to-campus properties, where students can walk to campus in minutes, tend to command premium demand and rents because convenience, safety, and proximity to classes matter. They can maintain occupancy and pricing better and may be more insulated from nearby competition. A property requiring a car, shuttle, or bus generally faces more alternatives and softer demand.

The useful underwriting combination is not simply “near a university.” It is a university with durable enrollment paired with a property whose location gives students a reason to choose it over the next available bed.

## Turnover and Seasonality Risks

Student housing has two operating risks that conventional apartments spread out more gradually: near-total annual turnover and a concentrated leasing season. Academic-year leases end, students graduate, transfer, study abroad, or move, and the property must often re-lease nearly its entire resident base each cycle.

That creates heavy marketing, leasing, unit make-ready, and maintenance work in a short period. Conventional apartments usually have staggered move-in dates and longer average tenancy. Student housing does not get that same cushion.

Seasonality compounds the issue. Most leases are signed in a pre-leasing period before the fall term, and the property must largely fill itself for the coming academic year in that window. If pre-leasing is behind target as the term approaches, vacant beds can remain vacant through the year with limited opportunity to backfill. Pre-leasing velocity—how quickly the property is filling compared with the same point in earlier cycles—is therefore a critical leading indicator.

The sector also carries the usual DST risks: sponsor, market, financing, fee, and illiquidity risk. The student-housing-specific risk is that operational execution cannot be deferred. A missed leasing season may determine a full year of results.

## Income Potential

The income case is real, but it is not effortless. By-the-bed leasing can produce a higher total revenue per unit than a comparable whole-unit apartment lease because the sum of individual bed rents can exceed what one household would pay. Parental guarantees can support collections if a student defaults.

At a strong, growing university with limited on-campus supply, a well-located and well-managed property can sustain high occupancy. Annual lease cycles can also let the operator reset rents to market each year, providing some inflation responsiveness. Those are meaningful features for current-income underwriting.

But the higher revenue potential is tied to successful pre-leasing and strong management. A weak enrollment trend, a distant location, missed leasing targets, rising turnover costs, or debt service can change the outcome quickly. Projected distributions are estimates, not promises.

## Key Takeaways

- Demand is tied to university enrollment and the property's proximity to campus. Pedestrian-to-campus locations are especially valued.
- By-the-bed leases and parental guarantees can lift revenue per unit and support collections.
- Near-total annual turnover and a concentrated pre-leasing window are defining risks. Missing the window can leave vacancy for the academic year.
- Income potential rests on the university's strength, location, pre-leasing velocity, and sponsor execution—not on a headline yield alone.

## Sample Student Housing Offerings

It can help to picture the usual shape of an offering, without treating an illustration as a security or a return promise. A representative student-housing DST might hold one purpose-built community, or a small portfolio, near a large public university selected for enrollment and supply characteristics. A pedestrian-to-campus location is generally preferred.

The offering materials normally describe bed and unit counts, distance to campus, amenities, the university served, and recent occupancy and pre-leasing history. They should also explain the sponsor's student-housing experience, because this property type has a different operating rhythm from conventional multifamily.

The minimum investment is commonly in the range of roughly $25,000 to $100,000. The trust may be all-cash, or it may use a pre-arranged non-recourse loan at the trust level, a structure investors can compare with relinquished debt using an [LTV calculator for matching 1031 debt](ltv-calculator-1031-debt-matching.html). Materials also describe projected distributions, fee and load structure, an expected hold period often around 5 to 7 years, and a business plan such as improving pre-leasing or raising rents before an eventual sale.

Those are characteristics to weigh: a stated minimum, capital structure, projected distributions, disclosed fees, a multi-year hold, and an operating plan. They are not guarantees of income, sale timing, or return.

## Evaluating a Student Housing DST

Start with the university. Review enrollment levels and trends, applicant demand, selectivity, financial health, and growth outlook. A large, well-established public institution with stable or rising enrollment is a more durable demand base than a smaller or shrinking school, but no institution is free of risk. Single-university dependence deserves explicit weight.

Then assess the property. Is it truly pedestrian-to-campus, or does it require a drive or shuttle? The answer can influence occupancy, rent resilience, and competitive position. Review pre-leasing velocity against the same point in prior cycles; it is often the clearest near-term signal of whether the property is filling for the next academic year.

Finally, evaluate the sponsor's actual student-housing operating experience, as well as projected distributions, fees, leverage, and the hold period. These factors interact. A sponsor, university, location, and capital structure should be judged as one underwriting problem, not as isolated strengths.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a student housing DST?

A student housing DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust that holds one or more purpose-built communities for college students, usually near a university. Investors own fractional beneficial interests. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured DST interest can be treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, allowing an investor to reinvest appreciated real-estate proceeds and defer capital-gains tax. A sponsor and operator manage leasing, maintenance, and the property; investors receive a pro-rata share of net rental income. The attraction is passive exposure to university-anchored demand and a by-the-bed model, alongside real turnover and seasonality risks. DST interests are broker-dealer securities for accredited investors after suitability review, and distributions are projected, not guaranteed.

### What is the investment thesis for student housing?

The thesis is that demand can be anchored to a university rather than the local employment base. On-campus supply often falls short, purpose-built communities near campus fill the gap, and a new class of renters arrives each year. By-the-bed leases can improve revenue per unit, while parental guarantees can support collections. The thesis is strongest at large public flagships or other established institutions with stable or rising enrollment and pedestrian-to-campus locations. It is weaker at smaller or shrinking schools and at properties far from campus. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Why does university enrollment matter so much?

Student-housing demand is directly linked to enrollment. Unlike conventional apartments, which depend more broadly on employment and population, the property draws from one institution. Investors should review enrollment history and direction, applicant demand, selectivity, financial health, and growth trajectory. Large, established universities with stable or growing enrollment can provide a more durable renter base. The other side is concentrated risk: if the university loses students, appeal, or financial stability, nearby housing demand can weaken with few alternate renters to replace it.

### Why is proximity to campus important?

Students value proximity for convenience, safety, and campus life. A pedestrian-to-campus property that students can reach on foot in minutes can command premium demand and rents, maintain occupancy and pricing more effectively, and be more insulated from competition. A property that requires a car, bus, or shuttle often faces more competing options and may have softer demand. Strong enrollment and a walkable location are the desirable combination; distance from campus is a risk to weigh.

### What is by-the-bed leasing?

In by-the-bed leasing, each student signs an individual lease for a bedroom and shares the kitchen and living area with roommates. It differs from a single household signing one whole-unit lease. Individual bed rents can add up to more revenue than a comparable whole-unit rent, and one roommate's default does not generally affect the others' leases. Parental guarantees often support collections. The trade is more leasing complexity because the operator must fill many individual beds every cycle.

### What are the main risks of a student housing DST?

The defining sector risks are high turnover, seasonality, and single-university dependence. The property often re-leases nearly all residents annually, and pre-leasing must largely succeed within a short period before fall. A shortfall can lock in vacancy for the academic year. A decline in university enrollment can weaken the demand base. Investors also face sponsor, market, financing, fee and load, and illiquidity risks common to DSTs. University quality, location, pre-leasing velocity, and specialized sponsor experience are core due-diligence items.

### Why is turnover higher in student housing?

Students are a naturally transient renter base. Academic-year leases end, and students can graduate, transfer, study abroad, or move. A student-housing property may re-lease nearly its entire resident base each year, requiring large-scale marketing, leasing, make-ready work, and maintenance. Conventional apartments have more staggered move-ins and longer average tenure, so their re-leasing burden is more spread out. The concentration of work makes capable management and an experienced sponsor particularly important.

### What is seasonality risk in student housing?

Leasing follows the academic calendar rather than happening evenly all year. Most leases are signed in a concentrated pre-leasing season before the fall term, so the property has essentially one annual chance to fill itself. If pre-leasing trails target as the term approaches, vacant beds can remain vacant for the full academic year, with limited opportunity to recover until the next cycle. Pre-leasing velocity compared with prior years is therefore a leading indicator of performance. Strong operations focused on pre-leasing are essential.

### What is the income potential of student housing?

By-the-bed leasing can generate higher total revenue per unit than a comparable conventional apartment. Parental guarantees can support collections. At a strong and growing university with limited on-campus supply, a well-located, well-managed community can maintain occupancy and reset rents annually, offering some inflation responsiveness and current-income potential. The income depends on successful pre-leasing and capable management, however, and comes with turnover and seasonality burden. Projected DST distributions are estimates, never guaranteed, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Can I use a student housing DST for a 1031 exchange?

Yes, subject to the structure and the investor's facts. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured DST interest can be treated as real property for 1031 purposes. An investor can sell appreciated investment real estate, work through a qualified intermediary, and reinvest into a student-housing DST. Full deferral generally calls for equal or greater equity and debt replacement, identifying the DST within 45 days and closing within 180 days. The structure can move an owner from active property work into passive management, but the QI and tax advisor should confirm the specific mechanics. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How long is the hold period for a student housing DST?

The hold is generally multi-year, commonly 5 to 7 years, though it varies by offering. During the hold, the sponsor operates the community, manages annual leasing, distributes income when available, and pursues the business plan, which might include improving pre-leasing, raising rents, or enhancing the property before sale. When the sponsor sells, investors receive pro-rata proceeds and may take cash or complete another 1031 exchange. A DST usually has little or no secondary market, so an investor should expect illiquidity for the hold and should not count on an early sale. Sale timing depends on the sponsor and market conditions and is not guaranteed.

### What should I look for when evaluating a student housing DST?

Begin with the university's enrollment levels and trends, applicant demand, financial health, and growth. Then assess walkability to campus and pre-leasing velocity relative to prior cycles. Review whether the sponsor has genuine student-housing experience, because the operations differ from conventional apartments. Projected distributions are estimates, so evaluate fees, leverage, and the hold period with the economics. These points reinforce or weaken each other; a thorough review considers them together before a commitment.

### Are student housing DST distributions guaranteed?

No. Offering figures are projections based on assumptions about pre-leasing, occupancy, rents, expenses, and market conditions, and actual results may be higher or lower. A pre-leasing shortfall can produce academic-year vacancy. Enrollment declines, higher turnover costs, and debt service can also reduce distributions. There is no promise to return capital or pay a set yield, and past performance does not guarantee future results. Treat projected income as an assumption to test and size a DST allocation within the investor's overall liquidity and risk plan.

### How does student housing compare to conventional apartments?

Both are multifamily, but the underwriting differs. Student housing is tied to a single university's enrollment; conventional apartments draw more from local employment and population. Student housing can be more insulated from ordinary economic cycles but more exposed to one institution's fortunes. It often uses by-the-bed leases and parental guarantees rather than whole-unit leases, and it faces annual turnover and one concentrated leasing period rather than staggered renewals and year-round leasing. By-bed revenue can create upside, but operational burden, seasonality, and single-university dependence are higher. It can be one part of a diversified DST allocation.

## Glossary

**Student Housing DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding purpose-built student housing.

**Purpose-Built Student Housing:** Housing designed specifically for college students.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in which investors own fractional interests.

**Beneficial Interest:** An investor's fractional, 1031-eligible ownership in a DST.

**By-the-Bed Lease:** A lease where each student signs for an individual bedroom.

**Parental Guarantee:** A parent co-signing or guaranteeing a student's lease.

**Pedestrian-to-Campus:** A property students can walk to campus from in minutes.

**Enrollment:** The number of students attending a university; it drives demand.

**Pre-Leasing:** Signing leases ahead of the fall term for the coming year.

**Pre-Leasing Velocity:** How quickly a property fills for the next academic year.

**Turnover:** The annual re-leasing of nearly the entire resident base.

**Seasonality:** Leasing concentrated in the academic-calendar cycle.

**Sponsor:** The firm that structures and operates the DST.

**1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange deferring capital-gains tax.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.

**Single-University Dependence:** Reliance on one institution's enrollment for demand.
