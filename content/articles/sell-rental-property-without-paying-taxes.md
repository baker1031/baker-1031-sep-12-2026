# Sell Rental Property Without Paying Taxes? What's Actually Possible

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read*

I keep hearing the same question from rental owners: can I sell, take the cash, and not pay tax? It is a useful question because the language around it is often much looser than the rules.

The first-order view is that the answer is found by locating a tax trick. The second-order reality is that legitimate choices ask something in return: reinvestment, a stream of future payments, or a decision not to cash out during life. There is no legal mechanism that makes a real, recognized gain disappear while the seller pockets the proceeds. Anyone promising that is overselling.

The useful tools are a 1031 exchange, which reinvests proceeds in like-kind real property, including a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), and defers gain and depreciation recapture; an installment sale under IRC §453, which spreads gain over the years payments arrive; and the step-up in basis at death under IRC §1014, which can erase deferred gain for heirs. A 1031 into a DST held until death can combine two of those tools. DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after a suitability review. This is educational information, not tax advice, so confirm the facts with your CPA.

## The Honest Answer: Defer, Don't “Avoid”

Selling an appreciated rental generally produces capital-gains tax on the appreciation and tax on [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) for depreciation claimed while the property was rented. The seller may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax and state tax.

That changes the question. The practical issue is not how to make tax vanish, but whether it can be legally deferred while equity stays invested—and, in the best case, whether it may never become due. A legitimate strategy either reinvests the proceeds through a 1031, receives sale payments over time through an installment sale, or leaves the property uncashed during life so the step-up rules may apply. Understanding what each route does, and what it requires, is the honest starting point.

## The 1031 Exchange: Defer by Reinvesting

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-rules-requirements.html), under IRC §1031, is the main deferral tool. The owner sells investment real property and reinvests the proceeds in like-kind replacement *real property*, deferring capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture. The rules are strict: a qualified intermediary must hold the proceeds, the seller may never take receipt, replacement property must be identified within 45 days, and closing must occur within 180 days. When the exchange is completed correctly, gain is not recognized at the sale; it carries into the replacement property.

For an owner who wants to stop managing a hands-on rental, the replacement can be a passive DST. The IRS confirmed in Revenue Ruling 2004-86 that a DST interest is like-kind real property. That makes it possible to exchange into professionally managed DST real estate, defer tax, and receive passive income. See our [guide to exchanging into a DST](1031-exchange-into-a-dst-passive-option.html). Section 1031 applies only to investment real property: not stocks, not an operating business, and not personal-use property.

> A 1031 does not erase gain. It moves it forward into the next property, where it remains deferred until a non-exchange sale—or, potentially, until the property is held through death.

## The Installment Sale: Spread the Gain Over Time

An [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) under IRC §453 arises when a seller finances the buyer and receives payment across more than one tax year. Rather than reporting all gain in the year of sale, the seller reports it proportionally as each payment is received. That does not avoid tax. It can spread gain over multiple years, potentially keeping the seller in lower brackets and easing the cash-flow burden.

Depreciation recapture is generally still taxed in the year of sale. There is also a real risk that the buyer defaults on the note. This is a different trade from a 1031: the seller gives up reinvestment of all proceeds in exchange for a payment stream and a smoother tax bill.

## The Step-Up at Death: Where Deferral Becomes Permanent

The [step-up in basis at death](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) under IRC §1014 is why deferral can, in some cases, become permanent. At death, heirs generally receive a basis equal to the property’s fair market value on the date of death. Gain deferred through one or more 1031 exchanges can be wiped out by that step-up, leaving heirs with the stepped-up basis and deferred capital-gains tax that may never be paid.

This is the “swap till you drop” approach: continue exchanging, potentially into passive DSTs rather than actively managed property in later years, and hold until death. It is the closest practical route to selling a rental “without paying taxes,” but the condition is that the owner does not cash out during life. Estate, basis, and timing rules are fact-specific, so confirm them with a CPA and estate attorney.

## Key Takeaways

- You generally cannot avoid capital-gains tax on a rental sale while cashing out. The honest goal is deferral.
- A 1031 exchange, including an exchange into a DST, defers gain and depreciation recapture by reinvesting in like-kind real property. Section 1031 is for real property only.
- An installment sale under IRC §453 spreads gain over years as payments are received; it does not eliminate the tax.
- The IRC §1014 step-up in basis at death can erase deferred gain for heirs, potentially turning deferral into permanent avoidance.

## A Note on Former Homes and the Section 121 Exclusion

If the property was a primary residence, IRC §121 may be relevant. It can exclude up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for married couples filing jointly, when the owner both owned and used the property as a main home for at least two of the last five years. A pure rental does not qualify. A conversion from rental to residence, or the reverse, has more nuanced rules.

This is genuine tax avoidance on the capped amount, but it applies to homes rather than investment rentals. Most landlords considering a sale will be evaluating deferral tools instead. Confirm Section 121 eligibility with your CPA.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I really sell a rental property without paying any taxes?

Not by simply cashing out. A real, recognized gain is generally taxable. What can be done is to defer the tax: a 1031 exchange can reinvest proceeds in like-kind real property, including a passive DST, and defer capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture; an installment sale can spread gain over years; and property or a replacement property held until death may receive an IRC §1014 step-up that erases the deferred gain. Each has a trade-off. A 1031 requires real-estate reinvestment within strict deadlines, an installment sale requires accepting payments over time, and a step-up strategy requires no lifetime cash-out. Confirm the specific facts with your CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How does a 1031 exchange defer the tax on a rental sale?

Under IRC §1031, an owner sells investment real property and reinvests proceeds in like-kind replacement real property, deferring capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture. A qualified intermediary must hold proceeds, the owner may not possess them, replacement property must be identified within 45 days, and closing must happen within 180 days. Gain carries forward into the replacement. A DST can be a passive replacement because Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a properly structured DST interest as like-kind real property. Stocks, operating businesses, and personal-use property are not qualifying replacement property. Work with a CPA and qualified intermediary.

### What is an installment sale and does it avoid the tax?

An IRC §453 installment sale is a sale with payments over more than one tax year, such as seller financing. Gain is reported proportionally as payments arrive rather than entirely in the sale year. It does not avoid tax, but can spread it across years, potentially smoothing cash flow and keeping the seller in lower brackets. Depreciation recapture is generally taxed in the year of sale, and the buyer can default on the note. The seller trades immediate reinvestment for a payment stream and remains exposed to the buyer’s creditworthiness. CPA confirmation matters because exceptions apply.

### How does the step-up in basis make deferral permanent?

When an owner dies, IRC §1014 generally gives heirs a basis equal to fair market value as of the date of death. If gain was deferred through one or more exchanges and the property, including a DST replacement, is held until death, that deferred gain can be erased. This is the swap-till-you-drop strategy: keep exchanging into qualifying property, potentially passive DSTs, then hold. The critical limitation is no lifetime cash-out. Estate, basis, and timing rules should be addressed with a CPA and estate attorney.

### Can I 1031 a rental into stocks or a business to avoid the tax?

No. A 1031 exchange is limited to like-kind real property held for trade, business, or investment. It cannot exchange a rental into stocks, bonds, a partnership interest, an operating business, or personal-use property. An owner who wants to move outside real estate generally sells, pays tax, and reinvests after-tax proceeds. A separate program, IRC §1400Z-2, allows certain capital gains from many sources, including stocks, to be invested in Qualified Opportunity Funds, but it has different rules and is not a 1031 exchange. For a real-estate gain, the like-kind options are other investment real estate, including passive DSTs. Confirm the suitable path with a CPA.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate under IRC §1031.

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible fractional real estate.

**Capital-Gains Tax:** Tax on a property’s appreciation when it is sold.

**Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on depreciation previously claimed on a rental.

**Installment Sale:** A sale reported over multiple years as payments are received under IRC §453.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The IRC §1014 basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain.

**Section 121 Exclusion:** An exclusion of up to $250,000/$500,000 of gain on a qualifying primary residence, also commonly expressed as $250k/$500k.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds 1031 proceeds so the seller never receives them.

**45-Day Identification:** The window after sale to identify replacement property.

**180-Day Closing:** The deadline to close on replacement property in a 1031 exchange.
