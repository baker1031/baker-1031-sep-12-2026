# Return of Capital in REIT Distributions

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing investors assume every dollar of a REIT distribution is taxed alike. It is not. Return of capital, or ROC, is the portion treated as a return of the investor’s own capital rather than current income. It is usually not taxed in the year received, but it reduces cost basis and moves tax to a later sale. This guide explains ROC, why depreciation produces it, cost-basis effects, tax deferral, and the records an investor needs to keep. It is educational information, not tax advice; verify current rules with a tax advisor.

First-order thinking treats a non-taxable distribution as free money. Second-order thinking follows the basis reduction, the point at which basis reaches zero, the eventual gain on sale, and whether the distribution is supported by the REIT’s underlying cash flow.

## Key Takeaways

- Return of capital is the nondividend part of a REIT distribution, reported in Box 3 of Form 1099-DIV, and is generally not currently taxed.
- Depreciation can reduce taxable income below distributable cash, making ROC a normal REIT tax feature rather than a warning sign by itself.
- ROC reduces cost basis dollar for dollar. Tax generally appears at sale, and further ROC is capital gain once basis reaches zero.
- The benefit is deferral and possible conversion from ordinary-rate income to long-term capital gain; the responsibility is accurate basis tracking.

Return of capital appears across REIT structures. Our [2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html) explains how structures handle distributions.

## What Return of Capital Means

Return of capital, often abbreviated ROC, is a portion of a distribution treated as giving back part of the investor’s original investment rather than paying income. It is sometimes called a nondividend distribution. The amount appears in Box 3 of Form 1099-DIV.

The critical issue is timing. ROC is not permanently tax-free. It is tax-deferred: the amount reduces cost basis in the shares, creating a larger taxable gain—or a smaller loss—when the investor sells. ROC can shift current ordinary-dividend treatment toward capital gain at sale. It is a timing and tax-character feature, not free money.

## Why REIT Dividends Include It

ROC commonly arises from depreciation. Real estate is depreciable, and a REIT deducts building depreciation each year. Depreciation is a non-cash expense: it lowers taxable income without reducing rent collected. Because a REIT must distribute most earnings under [the 90% distribution rule that governs how REITs pay dividends](how-reits-pay-dividends-the-90-percent-distribution-rule.html), it can distribute more cash than it has taxable earnings. The cash distributed above taxable earnings may be classified as return of capital instead of a taxable dividend.

That is a normal structural feature of depreciable real estate, not proof of trouble. A healthy REIT with substantial property can generate large depreciation deductions and a meaningful ROC component. Still, [REIT payout ratios are best read against cash flow, not just reported earnings](reit-payout-ratios-explained.html). The percentage varies by REIT and year based on property age and basis, depreciation schedule, and earnings. After year-end, the REIT reports ordinary dividends, capital-gain distributions, and ROC on Form 1099-DIV.

> ROC is not automatically a red flag. Depreciation can allow a healthy REIT to distribute more cash than it reports as taxable income.

## Impact on Cost Basis

Cost basis begins with what the investor paid for shares. Each ROC distribution reduces it dollar for dollar. For example, a $50,000 purchase followed by $3,000 of ROC over several years leaves an adjusted basis of $47,000. The $3,000 was not taxed when received, but the lower basis sets up more gain at sale.

Basis cannot fall below zero. While it remains positive, ROC is not currently taxable and simply lowers basis. Once cumulative ROC reaches zero basis, further ROC cannot reduce basis and is instead taxed as capital gain in the year received. Long-term investors must track that threshold, including across multiple purchases and reinvested distributions.

## Tax Deferral Effect

ROC postpones tax to the sale year. The investor retains and can reinvest the cash in the meantime, and a tax paid later has a lower present-value cost than a tax paid today. A distribution containing ROC can therefore be more tax-efficient than one paid entirely as an ordinary dividend.

There can also be tax-character conversion. Ordinary REIT dividends are taxed at ordinary rates, while the gain created by ROC basis reductions at sale is typically capital gain and may qualify for lower long-term rates when shares have been held long enough. The potential combination of deferral and rate conversion is part of the tax design of depreciable real estate; it is not a loophole and is not a permanent tax exemption. Verify qualification and treatment for the investor’s own situation.

> ROC is deferral with a responsibility: the investor keeps cash now, but must carry the lower basis forward to a later sale.

## Tracking It Accurately

Maintain a running record of original cost, additional purchases, and reinvested distributions, minus cumulative ROC from Box 3 of each year’s 1099-DIV. That adjusted basis determines the taxable gain on sale. Without the record, an investor can overstate basis, understate gain, or miss the point at which basis reaches zero.

Dividend reinvestment plans create new lots with their own basis. Purchases at different times and prices create multiple lots. Brokers report basis and proceeds on Form 1099-B at sale, but investors should retain their own records, particularly for older or non-traded REIT positions where broker reporting may be less complete. ROC classification generally has no separate current tax effect inside a tax-advantaged account, because distributions are not currently taxed there. For taxable holdings, careful records and CPA support are important.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is return of capital in a REIT distribution?

ROC is the part of a distribution treated as return of the investor’s own capital, not current income. It is not generally taxed in the year received. Instead, it reduces share cost basis and defers tax until sale. It appears in Box 3 of Form 1099-DIV, sometimes as a nondividend distribution. The tax is deferred rather than eliminated because lower basis produces a larger gain at sale. Verify current rules with a tax advisor.

### Why do REIT distributions include return of capital?

Depreciation is the main reason. It is a non-cash deduction that reduces a REIT’s taxable income without reducing rental cash flow. A REIT can therefore distribute more cash than taxable earnings, and the excess is ROC. This is a normal feature of tax treatment for income-producing real estate, not a sign that a REIT is struggling. The percentage varies by REIT and year and is reported on Form 1099-DIV.

### Is return of capital taxable?

ROC is not generally taxed when received, but it is tax-deferred rather than permanently tax-free. It lowers cost basis, making the gain at sale larger or loss smaller. When cumulative ROC has already reduced basis to zero, further ROC is taxed as capital gain in the year received. Confirm the treatment with a tax advisor.

### How does return of capital affect my cost basis?

It reduces basis dollar for dollar. If an investor paid $50,000 and received $3,000 of ROC, adjusted basis is $47,000. The ROC was not currently taxable, but a lower basis increases eventual gain. Basis cannot go below zero. While it remains positive, ROC lowers basis; after it reaches zero, additional ROC is capital gain in the year received. Track it carefully across the holding period.

### What happens when my basis reaches zero?

Further ROC can no longer be subtracted from basis. While basis is positive, ROC is non-taxable and reduces that basis. Once it reaches zero, additional amounts are capital gain in the year received. This can affect long-term holders after years of ROC. It is not a problem by itself; it means the deferral has run its course for that distribution portion. A CPA can help identify the threshold.

### Is return of capital a bad sign for a REIT?

Not necessarily. Depreciation can make a healthy REIT’s taxable earnings lower than its cash distributions, producing ROC even when underlying properties are performing well. It is not automatically benign, however. A distribution persistently exceeding economic earnings can indicate the company pays out more than it sustainably generates. Read ROC in the context of cash flow, payout coverage, and the REIT’s overall financial health—not the label alone.

### How is return of capital reported on Form 1099-DIV?

ROC appears in Box 3 as a nondividend distribution. Form 1099-DIV also separates total ordinary dividends in Box 1a, qualified dividends in Box 1b, total capital-gain distributions in Box 2a, Section 199A dividends representing the QBI-eligible portion, and Box 3 ROC. The Box 3 amount reduces that year’s cost basis. The breakdown supports ordinary-rate treatment for ordinary dividends, capital-gains rates for capital-gain distributions, the 20% deduction for qualified REIT dividends, and basis reduction for ROC. Keep each year’s Box 3 figure.

### What is the tax-deferral benefit of return of capital?

ROC postpones tax until sale, letting the investor retain and potentially reinvest the cash. A later tax payment has less present-value cost than one paid today. ROC may also convert what would have been ordinary income into capital gain on sale, often at lower long-term rates if the holding period qualifies. The benefit is deferral plus possible rate conversion, not a permanent exemption.

### Does return of capital matter inside an IRA?

It generally has no separate current tax benefit in an IRA or 401(k), where distributions are not currently taxed regardless of character. In a taxable account, ordinary dividends, capital-gain distributions, and ROC are treated differently. Inside an IRA, there is no current tax to defer and no basis tracking for the same purpose; in a Roth, qualified withdrawals can be tax-free. Account type changes the analysis. Confirm the current rules with an advisor.

### Why does depreciation create return of capital?

Depreciation spreads property cost over time as a non-cash deduction. It reduces taxable income without reducing rent collected, leaving a REIT with more cash to distribute than taxable earnings. Cash paid above taxable earnings is ROC rather than a taxable dividend. Depreciation is therefore the mechanism that creates the gap between distributable cash and taxable income.

### How do I track return of capital over time?

Keep a running basis record: what was paid for shares, plus additional purchases and reinvested distributions that create new lots, less cumulative ROC in Box 3 of every 1099-DIV. The result is adjusted basis at sale. Multiple lots, dividend reinvestment, older positions, and non-traded REIT reporting can make this difficult. Form 1099-B reports basis and proceeds at sale, but retain personal records as backup. Tax-advantaged accounts sidestep the issue; taxable holdings benefit from CPA help.

### Does return of capital reduce my dividend income?

It reduces current taxable dividend income, not cash received. Ordinary dividends, qualified dividends, and capital-gain distributions are taxable in the current year; Box 3 ROC is a nondividend distribution that reduces basis instead. If a portion of total cash is ROC, taxable income from the REIT is lower that year than total cash received. The reduction is temporary because ROC resurfaces as gain at sale.

### Is return of capital the same as a dividend cut?

No. A dividend cut is a reduction in cash paid, often because income declined. ROC is a tax classification of cash still paid: it describes a non-taxable return of invested capital that lowers basis, not whether the payout increased or decreased. A REIT can maintain the same total cash distribution while part is ROC year after year because depreciation keeps taxable income below distributable cash. Box 3 does not itself mean a dividend was cut.

## Glossary

### Return of Capital (ROC)

The nondividend portion of a distribution that reduces basis and is not currently taxed.

### Nondividend Distribution

Another name for ROC, reported in Box 3 of Form 1099-DIV.

### Cost Basis

The investment in shares used to determine gain at sale.

### Adjusted Basis

Cost basis after subtracting cumulative return of capital.

### Depreciation

A non-cash real-estate deduction that lowers a REIT’s taxable income.

### Box 3

The Form 1099-DIV box reporting nondividend, return-of-capital amounts.

### Tax Deferral

Postponing tax to a later year, ROC’s core effect.

### Rate Conversion

Turning ordinary-rate income into lower-rate capital gain at sale.

### Capital Gain

Gain realized at sale, increased by basis reductions.

### Zero Basis

The point at which additional ROC becomes taxable as gain.

### Form 1099-DIV

The form reporting the components of a REIT distribution.

### Form 1099-B

The form reporting basis and proceeds when shares are sold.

### Ordinary Dividend

The REIT distribution portion taxed at ordinary rates.

### Capital-Gain Distribution

A REIT payout taxed at capital-gains rates.

### Dividend Reinvestment

Reinvesting distributions, which creates new basis lots.

### Tax-Advantaged Account

An IRA or 401(k) where ROC treatment is moot.
