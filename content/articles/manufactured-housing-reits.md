# Manufactured Housing REITs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing affordable housing described as a simple defensive trade. It is not. A low monthly housing cost can create durable demand, but the thing that gives a manufactured-housing community its income stability—residents' difficulty moving—also brings political and regulatory attention.

That is where first-order and second-order thinking separate. First-order thinking sees a full community, modest lot-rent increases, and a light operating model. Second-order thinking asks what the regulatory map permits, how much rent growth is already priced in, whether the local market can absorb pressure, and how leverage, liquidity, and structure change the risk.

Manufactured-housing REITs own the land beneath affordable communities, not a collection of scattered houses. Residents generally own their manufactured home and rent the lot, or pad, below it. The model is built around affordability, constrained supply, low turnover, and lot-rent income, but it also faces growing rent-control and regulatory risk. ## What These REITs Own

A manufactured housing REIT owns manufactured-housing communities, also called land-lease communities and, in older terminology, mobile home parks. The essential split is between the home and the land. Residents typically own the home but rent the pad from the community owner. The REIT owns the land, roads, utilities, common areas, and entitlements rather than the individual dwellings.

That structure shapes the economics. The primary revenue is lot rent paid across occupied pads. Some REITs also own rental homes or receive ancillary revenue from utilities, services, or amenities. Compared with an apartment owner, the community owner generally does not maintain thousands of dwelling interiors or turn a unit after every resident moves. Larger REITs may own hundreds of communities across many states, creating geographic diversification within the sector.

The home-versus-land split is the key. It distinguishes this business from apartment ownership and explains both its low operating friction and its unusual resident relationship.

## Affordable-Housing Demand

The demand case starts with price. Manufactured housing is generally the lowest-cost unsubsidized housing option in most U.S. markets. For many households, owning a manufactured home and renting a lot costs materially less than a comparable apartment or conventional single-family home. As affordability tightens, that cost advantage can support demand for the limited number of community pads.

Supply matters as much as demand. Zoning resistance and entitlement hurdles make new manufactured-housing communities difficult to develop. The existing stock therefore grows slowly while the need for affordable housing persists. Historically, constrained new supply combined with steady demand has supported high occupancy and gradual lot-rent increases. That history is not a promise: local conditions and affordability conditions vary.

Manufactured housing is often the cheapest unsubsidized roof in a market. When housing becomes less affordable, demand for the lowest-cost option may hold up better than demand for more discretionary choices.

## High Retention & Low Turnover

The sector's distinctive operating feature is how rarely residents leave. A resident who owns a home has a reason to stay, and moving a manufactured home can cost thousands of dollars and risk damage. Selling into the same community can also mean accepting a discount. As a result, residents often remain for years even when lot rent rises modestly.

That creates high retention, low turnover, stable occupancy, and a relatively predictable stream of lot-rent income. It also leaves fewer move-outs, re-leasing costs, and vacancy gaps than an apartment portfolio may experience. Small annual lot-rent increases therefore often do not trigger a departure, giving the owner gradual pricing power. Those characteristics explain why the sector is commonly described as resilient or recession-tested.

They do not eliminate risk. Resident stickiness is an economic feature, not a guarantee of income or a license to assume future rent growth.

## Regulatory & Rent-Control Risk

The same stickiness is the sector's central risk. Residents who own their homes but cannot readily move may be viewed as captive. Advocates argue that steep or repeated lot-rent increases can feel coercive, and that concern has brought more political scrutiny in some jurisdictions.

States and localities have enacted or considered measures aimed specifically at manufactured-housing communities: caps on annual lot-rent increases, notice requirements, and rights of first refusal that allow residents or nonprofits a chance to buy a community before a sale. Where they apply, these rules can limit rent growth and affect community values. Exposure varies materially by state and locality, so the regulatory map can matter as much as the physical assets. Rules change; verify current conditions.

## Key Takeaways

- Manufactured housing REITs own land-lease communities—the land and infrastructure—and earn lot rent from residents who generally own their homes but rent the pad.
- Demand rests on affordability. Limited new supply meets a need for the lowest-cost unsubsidized housing option for many households.
- Expensive, difficult moves support high retention, low turnover, stable occupancy, and gradual lot-rent pricing power.
- Regulation is the critical counterweight. Rent caps, notice rules, and purchase rights can limit rent growth in affected jurisdictions.

## Evaluating the Sector

Start with the communities. Examine market strength, local household income, housing affordability, physical condition, amenities, and the mix of community-owned rental homes versus resident-owned homes paying lot rent. High-quality communities in supply-constrained, affordability-pressured markets have historically had the strongest occupancy and the most durable lot-rent growth.

Then examine operating results and exposure. Occupancy, the history and pace of lot-rent growth, and expense control show how a portfolio is operating. Regulatory concentration is equally important: a portfolio with substantial exposure to states or localities with existing rent controls or active proposals may have a different future rent-growth profile.

As with any REIT, look at FFO, AFFO, NAV, leverage, and whether the structure is traded or non-traded. For a non-traded offering, that analysis belongs inside a suitability review. Two practical questions often tell much of the story: how full are the communities, and how much room is there to raise lot rent? Regulation quietly governs the second answer.

## Income and Resilience Profile

Affordability-driven demand, constrained supply, and sticky residents have historically produced steady occupancy and gradual lot-rent growth. Owning land and infrastructure rather than maintaining dwelling interiors can make the operating model less management-intensive than some property types. Those are reasons some investors associate the sector with defensive, recession-tested income.

The qualifier is important. Lot-rent growth is an engine of returns, and regulation can throttle that engine. Manufactured housing REITs also carry market, interest-rate, leverage, and distribution risk. Distributions are not guaranteed, and share prices or NAVs can fluctuate. “Defensive” describes a historical tendency, not an outcome any portfolio is owed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a manufactured housing REIT?

A manufactured housing REIT is a Real Estate Investment Trust that owns manufactured-housing, land-lease communities. Residents typically own the manufactured home and rent the lot beneath it. The REIT owns the land, infrastructure, roads, utilities, common areas, and entitlements, and earns income mainly from lot rents across occupied pads. It may also own rental homes or receive ancillary revenue. Larger REITs may own hundreds of communities across states. Like other REITs, it distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders as dividends.

### How do manufactured housing REITs make money?

They earn income primarily from lot rent: the payment for the pad beneath a resident's home. A community owner collects that rent across occupied pads and may supplement it with rental homes, utilities, services, or amenities. Because residents generally own their homes, the REIT is not maintaining thousands of interiors or turning every unit as an apartment owner might. After expenses, a REIT distributes most of its income to shareholders under the usual REIT rules.

### Why is demand for manufactured housing considered durable?

Demand is rooted in affordability. Manufactured housing is generally the lowest-cost unsubsidized housing option in most U.S. markets, and a home plus lot rent can cost well below a comparable apartment or conventional house. Development is difficult because of zoning resistance and entitlement hurdles, so the community stock grows slowly. Limited new pads and persistent affordable-housing need have historically supported occupancy and gradual lot-rent growth. None of that is guaranteed; local conditions, affordability, and future results vary.

### Why is retention so high in manufactured housing communities?

Residents usually own the home and face a costly, difficult move. Relocation can cost thousands of dollars, risk damaging the home, interrupt life, or require a discounted sale into the community. That makes residents inclined to stay for years rather than leave after a modest rent increase. The resulting stable occupancy, lower turnover, fewer re-leasing costs, and smaller vacancy gaps are central to the sector's resilient or recession-tested reputation.

### What is lot rent?

Lot rent is the payment a resident makes for the lot or pad below a manufactured home. In a land-lease community, the resident generally owns the home but not the land. Lot rent may include the use of roads, utilities, common areas, and amenities. It is the primary income source for a manufactured housing REIT, and gradual lot-rent growth is an important driver of REIT returns.

### What is the main risk in the manufactured housing sector?

Regulatory and rent-control risk is the main sector-specific concern. Because residents are difficult to move, they can be viewed as captive, and steep lot-rent increases draw scrutiny. Caps, notices, and resident or nonprofit rights of first refusal can constrain the rent growth that supports returns and affect local valuations. The exposure differs widely by jurisdiction. Verify the current rules in each market.

### Are manufactured-housing communities the same as mobile home parks?

They describe essentially the same asset, although terminology has evolved. “Mobile home park” is the older term; the industry now more often uses “manufactured-housing community” or “land-lease community.” Factory-built homes constructed after June 1976 are regulated under federal HUD standards and are called manufactured homes. “Mobile home” technically refers to units built before that date. Modern homes are typically placed permanently on a pad and are not mobile in a practical sense. The underlying land-lease economics are the same.

### Do residents own their homes in a manufactured-housing community?

In most cases, yes. The resident owns the home and rents the lot. That ownership gives the resident a stake in maintaining the home and makes a move more difficult, supporting retention. Some communities also include rental homes that the REIT or community owner owns and leases directly, so a portfolio can mix resident-owned homes paying lot rent with community-owned homes paying a higher all-in rent.

### Why is it hard to build new manufactured-housing communities?

New communities face zoning resistance, local and neighboring opposition, permitting challenges, and entitlement hurdles. Obtaining approval can be slow, costly, or unavailable. Few new communities are built as a result, which limits new pad supply even as affordable-housing demand persists. That scarcity has historically supported occupancy and gradual rent growth at well-located existing communities.

### How do I evaluate a manufactured housing REIT?

Assess community quality and location: market strength, household income, housing affordability, physical condition, amenities, and the resident-owned versus community-owned home mix. Then review occupancy, lot-rent growth history and pace, expense control, and exposure to jurisdictions with rent control or active proposals. Add FFO, AFFO, NAV, leverage, and traded versus non-traded structure. A non-traded REIT should be assessed through a suitability review.

### What are FFO, AFFO, and NAV?

FFO, or funds from operations, adjusts net income by adding back real estate depreciation, a large non-cash charge, and removing gains or losses on property sales. It can show recurring property cash generation more clearly than standard earnings. AFFO, adjusted funds from operations, takes FFO further by subtracting recurring capital expenditures and other adjustments, approximating cash available to support distributions. NAV, net asset value, estimates the per-share value of underlying real estate net of debt; it helps assess a premium or discount to asset value and commonly informs non-traded REIT pricing.

### Are manufactured housing REITs recession-resistant?

“Recession-resistant” overstates it. “Defensive-leaning” is more accurate. Affordability, constrained supply, and costly moves can keep occupancy and lot-rent income steadier through cycles, but ordinary REIT risks remain: market prices, interest rates, leverage, distributions, and regulation. Share prices and NAVs can fluctuate, and distributions are not guaranteed. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Can I use a manufactured housing REIT in a 1031 exchange?

Not directly. REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property, so they are not eligible replacement property for a 1031 exchange. A possible indirect path is to exchange into a properly structured Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), which can be 1031-eligible real property; the DST's property may later enter a REIT through a 721, or UPREIT, exchange that converts the interest into operating-partnership units while preserving deferral. Confirm the specifics with your tax adviser.

### Are manufactured housing REITs available to all investors?

It depends on the structure. Publicly traded REITs are listed on exchanges and broadly available through a brokerage account, including through a REIT fund or ETF. Non-traded and private REITs are offered through a broker-dealer, may have investment minimums, and typically require accredited or otherwise suitable investors. Suitability review considers financial situation, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance because non-traded structures can be illiquid and complex.

## Glossary

**Manufactured Housing REIT:** A REIT that owns land-lease manufactured-housing communities.

**Land-Lease Community:** A community where residents own homes and rent the lot.

**Lot Rent:** The rent a resident pays for the pad beneath a home.

**Pad:** The lot or site on which a manufactured home is placed.

**Manufactured Home:** A factory-built home meeting federal HUD standards.

**Mobile Home Park:** The older term for a manufactured-housing community.

**Retention:** The rate at which residents stay rather than leave.

**Turnover:** The rate at which residents move out and are replaced.

**Occupancy:** The share of community pads that are filled.

**Rent Control:** Rules limiting how much lot rent can be raised.

**Right of First Refusal:** A resident's or nonprofit's chance to buy before a sale.

**Affordable Housing:** The lowest-cost unsubsidized housing demand driver discussed here.

**FFO:** Funds from operations, a REIT's recurring cash measure.

**AFFO:** Adjusted FFO, cash available to support distributions.

**NAV:** Net asset value, the per-share value of the real estate.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a non-traded REIT fits an investor.
