# 1031 Exchange for Commercial Property

I keep seeing commercial owners treat a 1031 exchange as a choice between keeping the same kind of building or paying a large tax bill. The rules are much broader, but the replacement still has to earn its place in the portfolio.

Commercial property is often appreciated, depreciated, and debt-financed, so a sale can produce a four-layer tax bill: capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, net investment income tax, and state tax. First-order thinking is to avoid that bill. Second-order thinking asks whether the next asset improves income quality, concentration, leverage, liquidity, and operating burden after the tax deferral is set aside.

## Commercial-to-commercial exchanges

Office, retail, industrial, multifamily, self-storage, hospitality, and other commercial investment property are like-kind to one another. An owner can sell an office building and buy a shopping center, trade a warehouse for apartments, exchange a strip mall for a passive interest in a portfolio of medical offices, or exchange one asset for a larger one without losing deferral merely because the type changes.

That freedom supports trade-ups, repositioning away from a softening market or type, and consolidation or diversification. In a trade-up, deferred tax can become additional buying power instead of tax friction that shrinks the capital available to reinvest. The mechanics remain familiar: a [qualified intermediary](insights.html) before closing, written identification by day **45**, and closing by day **180**. Commercial leases, tenants, operations, and debt make the work more involved.

## Cross-type flexibility and passive options

Commercial property can also be exchanged for residential rentals, investment land, agricultural property, or other investment real estate. An owner tired of office or retail demands might move into residential rentals or an apartment DST. Another might exchange a single office building into a mix of industrial, residential, and net-lease property, or rotate out of one sector when its fundamentals weaken and into another with stronger prospects. The decision can follow income, growth, diversification, and desired passivity rather than a like-for-like habit.

A net-lease (NNN) property has a long-term single creditworthy tenant paying taxes, insurance, and maintenance, which can reduce owner responsibility. An NNN property may be owned directly or through a DST. A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) is a securitized fractional interest in institutional commercial property, such as apartments, industrial parks, medical offices, or grocery-anchored retail. For 1031 purposes, the IRS treats the DST interest as direct real-property ownership. That gives an individual owner access to large, high-quality institutional assets that might not be available for a direct purchase, as well as professionally managed exposure and a quick close within the exchange calendar. DSTs are offered through a broker-dealer following a suitability review. Owners approaching retirement often blend NNN and DST positions to keep commercial income while shedding the operating role.

## Debt, value, diligence, and timing

Full deferral requires equal-or-greater replacement value and replacement of paid-off debt with new debt or additional cash. Paid-off debt is effectively cash-equivalent in this calculation: less replacement debt produces taxable [mortgage boot](insights.html) unless cash offsets it. An owner selling for **$5,000,000** with a **$3,000,000** mortgage needs at least **$5,000,000** of replacement value and at least **$3,000,000** of debt or offsetting cash. A leveraged DST’s known LTV and pre-arranged non-recourse debt can match the loan without personal application or guarantee.

Direct commercial diligence should start before the sale: review rent rolls, lease terms and expirations, tenant credit, operating history and expenses, capital-expenditure needs, and local/property-type fundamentals. Strong tenants on long leases are not the same asset as weak tenants or near-term expirations. A single-tenant NNN lease can simplify the review, but a multi-tenant commercial property requires evaluating the full rent roll. A DST sponsor’s prior acquisition and review may reduce the race, but a completed diligence package is not a substitute for underwriting income quality.

Commercial financing, lender approval, and closing can take longer than residential financing, while the **180-day** period continues. The **[45-day identification](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html)** window can be particularly demanding because candidates are fewer and diligence is deeper. Start lender work early. A DST identified as a backup can protect the exchange if a direct acquisition stalls: it is certain to close, has pre-arranged debt, needs no separate financing arrangement, and closes quickly. A DST route also avoids arranging a new loan and lengthy personal underwriting on the owner’s part.

## Common commercial scenarios

Owners use the same framework to trade up, rotate sectors, consolidate several properties, diversify one large asset into several replacements or DSTs, move toward retirement income, or take a partial cash-out while deferring the balance. A trade-up can mean selling a stabilized smaller asset and using its equity and deferred tax as buying power for a bigger one. A sector rotation can mean moving from a weakening office position into industrial or multifamily. A consolidation can turn several smaller commercial properties into one larger, more efficient asset. Diversifying one asset among several replacements or DSTs remains subject to the identification rules. These choices can be combined. Without the exchange, tax friction can consume roughly a third of gain at each transaction, reducing the capital available for each move. The key is to choose the desired size, type, market, leverage, and management burden before the deadline creates pressure.

## Direct property or a DST?

A direct commercial property gives the owner control, the possibility of asset-specific upside, and direct responsibility for tenant decisions, financing, capital expenditures, and management. A DST offers passivity, fractional diversification, sponsor management, and faster exchange execution, but it does not give the investor operating control and carries sponsor and offering fees. Neither structure is automatically superior. Compare the quality of the income, anticipated upside, debt, fees, liquidity, risk tolerance, and the degree of work you want to keep before choosing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I exchange commercial property, across types, or into residential?
Yes. Commercial real estate is suited to 1031 because the four-layer tax can be large. Commercial types and other investment real estate—including residential rentals, land, and agricultural property—are broadly like-kind when held for investment.

### How can I go passive?
NNN property uses a creditworthy tenant that pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance and can be held directly or through a DST. DST interests offer fractional, professionally managed institutional real estate. Both may reduce operating work, but investors must consider income quality, illiquidity, and suitability.

### Must I replace commercial debt, and can a DST help?
Yes, for full deferral: paid-off debt is cash-equivalent, so replace debt with new financing or additional cash. A leveraged DST includes pre-arranged non-recourse debt at a matching LTV, which can avoid a personal loan application or guarantee.

### What commercial diligence and timing matter?
Review rent roll, leases, tenant credit, operations, expenses, capex, and market fundamentals; for multi-tenant property, review the full rent roll. Start financing before the sale because commercial underwriting must fit the **180-day** deadline. Identify candidates by day **45**; a DST backup can protect against a stalled direct purchase because it is certain to close with no separate financing arrangement.

### What scenarios are common?
Trade-up, sector rotation, consolidation, diversification into several assets or DSTs subject to the identification rules, passive retirement income, and partial cash-out-and-defer. Commercial exchanges are more complex than many residential exchanges because of leases, diligence, debt, financing, and larger dollar amounts.

## Glossary

- **Commercial Real Estate:** Income-producing office, retail, industrial, multifamily, or self-storage property.
- **Like-Kind:** Broad investment-real-property standard.
- **Net-Lease (NNN) Property:** Tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.
- **DST:** Fractional institutional commercial real estate qualifying for 1031.
- **Rent Roll:** Schedule of leases, terms, and income.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Taxable debt shortfall.
- **LTV:** Debt-to-value ratio used in debt matching.
- **Trade-Up / Sector Rotation / Consolidation:** Moving to a larger asset with deferred tax as buying power, another type, or combining properties.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** Property-secured debt without personal liability.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** Replacement value must meet or exceed net sale price for full deferral.
- **Creditworthy Tenant / Capital Expenditure:** A financially strong tenant and a major property investment needing diligence.
- **QI / Suitability Review:** Independent proceeds holder and securities-appropriateness assessment.
