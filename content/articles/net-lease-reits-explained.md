# Net-Lease REITs Explained

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing investors treat a steady dividend as the whole investment case. With net-lease REITs, the rent may look orderly. The better question is what stands behind it: the tenant, the lease, the balance sheet, and the price paid for a long stream of income.

A net-lease REIT owns freestanding, single-tenant real estate—retail stores, industrial buildings, and offices—and leases each property to one tenant. The usual structure is a triple-net (NNN) lease, under which the tenant pays property taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to rent. Leases often run 10 to 25 years and include escalators. That can create contractual income that is largely insulated from property operating costs and is often described as bond-like.

First-order thinking sees that predictability and calls it safety. Second-order thinking asks whether the tenant can keep paying, how concentrated the portfolio is, how lease expirations are spaced, and what a higher rate environment does to the value and growth economics of long income. These are general educational observations, not investment advice or a forecast. Demand, returns, and outlook are non-promissory; past performance does not guarantee future results, and current market conditions should be verified with an adviser.

For wider context, [the 2026 REIT investing guide](reits-guide.html) compares net-lease income with the rest of the REIT universe.

## What Net-Lease REITs Own

The assets are single-tenant, freestanding properties. That can mean a pharmacy, convenience store, quick-service restaurant, dollar store, industrial or distribution building, or office property. The common feature is not the property type; it is one tenant occupying the entire building under a long net lease.

Like other equity REITs, a net-lease REIT collects rent and distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders. REITs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income. The enterprise is therefore less about actively operating a multi-tenant building and more about owning a set of long-term lease contracts secured by real estate.

Each asset is a contract with a company. The portfolio becomes a collection of those contracts, diversified across tenants, industries, geographies, and sometimes property types. Growth often comes from acquiring more properties, including through sale-leasebacks. This is a different model from actively managing apartments, shopping centers, or a multi-tenant office building.

## Triple-Net Lease Economics

Under a triple-net lease, the tenant pays the three principal property expenses—property taxes, insurance, and maintenance—on top of base rent. “Net” refers to the rent collected by the REIT after those costs sit with the tenant. The contracts are commonly long, often 10 to 25 years, and usually contain rent escalators that may be a fixed percentage or linked to inflation.

That arrangement can leave the REIT with a long, predictable, gradually growing rental stream and relatively limited operating responsibility. It is less exposed to rising property operating costs and to some of the capital demands that fall on more management-intensive real estate sectors. The landlord principally owns the asset and collects contractual rent.

This is the source of the sector’s bond-like reputation. It is not a statement that a net-lease REIT is a bond. It is an equity investment whose cash flow depends on a tenant and whose price can move materially.

## Tenant Credit Quality

The single-tenant model puts tenant credit at the center of the analysis. There is no diversification inside a property. If its tenant defaults, enters bankruptcy, or does not renew, the REIT can lose all income from that asset while it absorbs the cost and delay of re-leasing or selling it.

Review tenant credit ratings, the portion of rent coming from investment-grade tenants, industry exposure, and the degree of tenant concentration. A portfolio concentrated in financially strong tenants in stable industries can carry less credit risk than one dependent on weaker tenants or only a few large ones. Long lease terms and a mission-critical location—one important to a tenant’s operations—can make default or non-renewal less likely. They do not eliminate the risk.

The apparent steadiness of the income is only as strong as the tenants standing behind the leases.

## Interest-Rate Sensitivity

The same long-duration income that appeals to income investors also creates rate sensitivity. When rent is locked in for 10 to 25 years with only modest escalators, its present value behaves in many ways like a long-term bond: it tends to fall as rates rise and rise as rates fall. A REIT share price can come under pressure even when tenants continue paying, because a fixed long income stream is less attractive beside higher-yielding alternatives.

Rates also affect the operating business. Net-lease REITs acquire properties at cap rates, or property-purchase yields, and often use debt to finance acquisitions. When borrowing costs increase, the spread between acquisition cap rates and the cost of capital can compress, making profitable growth harder. Cap rates may rise as well, affecting property values.

Rate sensitivity is a characteristic to understand, not a prediction or a reason to avoid the sector. A REIT with strong credit, a sound balance sheet, disciplined acquisitions, and well-laddered lease maturities may handle differing rate environments better than a weaker one. Current rate conditions still need to be checked.

## Key Takeaways

- Net-lease REITs own single-tenant properties on triple-net leases, where tenants pay taxes, insurance, and maintenance. That structure can produce steady, bond-like income.
- Triple-net leases often run 10 to 25 years and include escalators, which can support growing rent and reduce exposure to operating costs.
- Tenant credit matters because a default or non-renewal can erase income from an entire property.
- Long income duration and acquisition economics make net-lease REITs sensitive to interest rates.

## Sale-Leasebacks and Diversification

A sale-leaseback is a common acquisition channel. A company sells the real estate it uses to a REIT and simultaneously signs a long-term net lease to remain in the property. The company frees capital for its business while retaining the location. The REIT acquires an asset with a built-in, long-term tenant, often the company that knows and relies on the facility.

Diversification is the main defense against the single-tenant feature of the model. Owning many properties across tenants, industries, geographies, property types, and lease maturities reduces dependence on any one tenant or sector. A well-diversified portfolio may absorb an occasional default without the same effect that it would have on a concentrated portfolio.

Lease-maturity laddering—staggering expiration dates—helps avoid a cluster of leases coming due in one year. Sale-leasebacks provide growth. Diversification and laddering manage the risk created by that growth.

## Evaluating Net-Lease REITs

Start with the tenant base: the share of rent from investment-grade tenants, the credit quality and industry exposure of the largest tenants, and concentration across tenants, industries, and geographies. Then examine remaining lease terms, escalators, and the maturity schedule. Long leases with escalators and a laddered maturity profile can support income and reduce near-term re-leasing risk.

Next, examine the acquisition engine and capital structure. The spread between acquisition cap rates and cost of capital helps determine whether growth can be profitable. Review FFO and AFFO, the cash-flow measures REIT investors commonly use instead of net income; then review the balance sheet, debt maturities, distribution coverage, leverage, and debt structure.

These are evaluation factors, not predictions. Share prices and distributions can fluctuate. Rates and tenant outcomes are uncertain. The work is to establish what is already embedded in the lease book and the capital structure, then verify current conditions before relying on the conclusion.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a net-lease REIT?

It is an equity REIT that owns freestanding, single-tenant retail, industrial, or office properties on long net leases. Its signature agreement is the triple-net lease, where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance in addition to rent. Leases often run 10 to 25 years with escalators. The REIT distributes most taxable income to shareholders because of the 90% distribution requirement and is primarily a long-term-contract owner rather than an active property operator. The income can be steady, but its quality depends on each tenant’s ability to pay.

### What is a triple-net (NNN) lease?

It is a lease under which the tenant pays property taxes, insurance, and maintenance as well as base rent. The landlord’s rent is therefore net of those expenses. Long NNN leases with escalators can create predictable, growing rent and reduce the operating-cost and capital burden that applies to more management-intensive real estate. They are the foundation of the sector’s bond-like income profile.

### Why is tenant credit quality so important for net-lease REITs?

One tenant occupies each building. A default, bankruptcy, or non-renewal can remove all income from that asset and lead to vacancy, re-leasing cost, or sale delay. Assess credit ratings, investment-grade rent share, industry exposure, and portfolio diversification. Long leases and mission-critical locations may reduce the risk, but the tenant’s financial strength remains central.

### Why are net-lease REITs sensitive to interest rates?

Their long, contractual rent resembles long-duration bond cash flow. When rates rise, its present value and REIT share prices can be pressured even if rent is paid; when rates fall, the reverse can occur. Higher borrowing costs can also compress the spread between acquisition cap rates and cost of capital, while changing cap rates affect property values. Understand the rate backdrop rather than try to forecast it.

### What is a sale-leaseback?

In a sale-leaseback, an operating company sells its real estate and immediately leases it back on a long-term basis. The company releases capital while staying in its location. The REIT acquires a property with a built-in tenant. It can be an attractive acquisition source when tenant credit is sound, the location is mission-critical, and the lease terms are strong; the structure alone does not establish quality.

### How do net-lease REITs make money?

They earn long-term rent from the sole tenants in their properties. Under triple-net leases, tenants also pay taxes, insurance, and maintenance, so rent is largely net income. Escalators can increase rent over time. Most taxable income is distributed as dividends under the 90% rule. Growth comes from acquisitions, often sale-leasebacks, at cap rates that exceed the REIT’s cost of capital.

### What property types do net-lease REITs own?

They commonly own freestanding retail—pharmacies, convenience stores, quick-service and casual restaurants, dollar stores, and auto-service locations—plus single-tenant industrial, distribution, and office properties. Some specialize; others diversify. The defining feature is the long, single-tenant net lease and the credit quality of that tenant, not a particular property type.

### Are net-lease REITs a good income investment?

They are often used for income because long triple-net leases with escalators can produce contractual, growing cash flow that is insulated from many operating costs. They also carry tenant-credit and rate risk, and distributions are not guaranteed. A tenant default can lead to a cut. They are not risk-free or bond-equivalent. Position size and diversification matter, and past performance and current yields do not guarantee future distributions.

### What should I look at when evaluating a net-lease REIT?

Focus on tenant credit, investment-grade rent, industry and tenant concentration, diversification, remaining lease terms, escalators, and maturity laddering. Then look at the cap-rate-to-cost-of-capital spread, FFO, AFFO, balance sheet, debt maturities, distribution coverage, leverage, and rate sensitivity. These factors are not predictions; share prices, distributions, rates, and tenant outcomes can change.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange into a net-lease REIT?

No. REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property, so a direct 1031 exchange into net-lease REIT shares is not available. There can be an indirect route to REIT exposure: an investor may exchange into a 1031-eligible Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) that holds net-leased property, and that DST property could later be acquired by a REIT through a 721 (UPREIT) exchange, converting the interest to operating-partnership units and eventually REIT shares while preserving deferral. The mechanics are technical and depend on the investor’s facts; confirm them with a tax adviser.

### How are net-lease REIT dividends taxed?

They are generally taxed like other REIT dividends. Most ordinary dividends are ordinary income rather than lower-rate qualified dividends because the REIT did not pay corporate tax. A 20% Section 199A deduction can apply to qualified REIT dividends. Some distributions are return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than creating current tax, or capital-gain distributions, which receive capital-gain treatment. The REIT reports the annual breakdown on Form 1099-DIV. The mix can change every year, so review it with a tax adviser.

### What does 'bond-like' income mean for net-lease REITs?

It describes long, contractual rent with escalators and tenant-paid operating expenses, which can resemble a bond’s steady coupons. It does not promise safety. A tenant can default, and the long-duration income and share price are sensitive to interest rates. A net-lease REIT remains an equity investment with credit and rate risk.

### What are the main risks of net-lease REITs?

Tenant default, bankruptcy, and non-renewal are central risks. So are rate sensitivity, higher borrowing costs, cap-rate changes, re-leasing and vacancy costs, concentration in tenants, industries, or geographies, market volatility, leverage, and distribution cuts. Strong credit, diversification, laddered maturities, and a sound balance sheet can manage these exposures but cannot eliminate them. The investment can lose value.

### How is a net-lease REIT different from other REITs?

Many other REIT sectors actively operate apartments, shopping centers, office space, or storage facilities and bear the accompanying operating costs and capital needs. A net-lease REIT owns long single-tenant NNN contracts and has less operating involvement. That can produce steadier, lower-operating-cost income, but it concentrates property risk in one tenant and can create more rate sensitivity than shorter-lease sectors. Multifamily has many short leases and more inflation-responsive rents; hotels have very short “leases” and high operating intensity.

## Glossary

**Net-Lease REIT.** A REIT owning single-tenant properties on long net leases.

**Triple-Net (NNN) Lease.** A lease where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

**Single-Tenant Property.** A building leased entirely to one tenant.

**Freestanding Property.** A standalone building common in net-lease portfolios.

**Escalator.** A built-in periodic increase in rent.

**Tenant Credit Quality.** A tenant’s financial strength and ability to pay rent.

**Investment-Grade Tenant.** A tenant with a strong credit rating.

**Sale-Leaseback.** A company sells property and leases it back long-term.

**Cap Rate.** The yield on a property purchase; a pricing measure.

**Cost of Capital.** The blended cost of a REIT’s debt and equity funding.

**Lease Maturity Laddering.** Staggering lease expirations to smooth re-leasing risk.

**Duration.** Sensitivity of long income’s value to interest rates.

**Bond-Like Income.** Steady contractual income resembling bond cash flows.

**FFO.** Funds from operations, a REIT’s core cash-flow measure.

**AFFO.** Adjusted FFO, net of recurring capital expenditures.

**90% Distribution Rule.** The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.
