# Can You 1031 Exchange a Primary Residence or Vacation Home?

I keep seeing one tax question treated as if every piece of real estate belongs in the same bucket: “Can I 1031 my house?” It does not. A home can have a large gain, and a vacation property can look a lot like a rental from the driveway. The tax treatment turns on the use of the property, not the view.

That distinction matters because Section 1031 is reserved for property held for investment or productive business use. A primary residence is personal-use property. It therefore lives under a different rule, Section 121, which can be more valuable because it can exclude qualifying gain outright rather than defer it.

First-order thinking is to reach for the famous tax tool whenever a sale creates gain. Second-order thinking is to ask what the property was actually held for, how the facts have been documented, and whether the rule you want is the rule the property can support. That is especially important with vacation homes and conversions.

## Key Takeaways

- A 1031 exchange applies to investment or business property, not a primary residence.
- A qualifying primary residence may use the Section 121 exclusion: up to **$250,000** of gain for a single filer or **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly.
- A vacation home may qualify for a 1031 only when it is held for investment and meets the safe harbor in Revenue Procedure **2008-16**.
- A conversion between personal and investment use may be possible, but it brings waiting periods, non-qualified use, and depreciation recapture into the analysis.

## The Short Answer

You generally cannot use a 1031 exchange for a primary residence. Section 1031 requires property held for productive use in a trade or business or for investment. A house you live in is held for personal use.

For most homeowners, that is not a loss of a tax benefit. Section 121 may exclude the qualifying gain rather than simply postpone it. Investment property and vacation property follow different paths. For the investment-property mechanics, see the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html).

## The Section 121 Exclusion

Section 121 is the main home-sale rule. It can exclude up to **$250,000** of gain for a single filer and **$500,000** for a married couple filing jointly if the owner both owned and used the home as a principal residence for at least **two of the five years** before sale. The exclusion generally cannot be used more than once in any **two-year** period.

That is an exclusion, not a deferral. For an owner whose qualifying gain fits within the limit, the gain can disappear from current federal taxation rather than move into a new property. Partial exclusions may also be available when a sale follows a change in employment, health, or other unforeseen circumstances even if the two-year test is not fully met.

This is why a primary residence and a rental occupy different tax categories. Deferral can be useful, but an outright exclusion is often the stronger result when Section 121 applies.

## Combining Section 121 With a 1031 Exchange

The rules can meet on a property that has genuinely had two uses. Think of a duplex where you live in one unit and rent the other, or a home with a qualifying home office or rental history. On a sale, the personal-use portion may be eligible for Section 121 and the investment portion may be eligible for 1031 deferral.

The split needs a careful allocation of basis and gain. Depreciation claimed for the rental portion remains subject to recapture. This is not a reason to force a mixed-use label onto a home; it is a reason to plan with a tax advisor well before listing when the facts already support both uses.

## Vacation Homes and the Safe Harbor

Vacation and second homes are the gray area. A vacation home must be held for **investment**, not personal enjoyment, to be exchangeable. Revenue Procedure **2008-16** provides a safe harbor. In each of the **two years** before the exchange, the property must be rented at fair market rent for at least **14 days**, while the owner’s personal use cannot exceed the greater of **14 days** or **10%** of the days the home was rented. A comparable test applies to the replacement property during the **two years** after the exchange.

Meet the safe harbor and the second home is treated as investment property for this purpose. Miss it and the property can look like personal-use property that does not qualify. The relevant facts are rental terms, actual rental use, and personal use—not the label on the property.

## Converting a Residence to a Rental—and Vice Versa

Use can change, and tax treatment can change with it. A former residence can be converted into a genuine rental and, after investment use is established, potentially exchanged under Section 1031. A property originally acquired through a 1031 can also become a residence and may eventually receive some Section 121 treatment.

The guardrails are substantial. A residence acquired through a 1031 exchange generally must be owned for at least **five years** before the Section 121 exclusion can apply. Periods of **non-qualified use**—time held as an investment rather than as a residence—reduce the exclusion. Depreciation taken during the rental period is recaptured and cannot be excluded.

The mirror conversion also needs real facts. A former home must become a bona fide investment: rented at a fair market rate, treated as a rental on the return, and held long enough to show investment intent rather than a brief effort to change the tax result. There is no bright-line holding period in the statute. A longer, cleaner record is safer; a token few months of renting before an exchange invites scrutiny.

## Worked Examples

Consider a couple that has lived in a home for a decade and sells with a **$400,000** gain. If they qualify for the **$500,000** joint Section 121 exclusion, they owe no federal tax on that gain. No 1031 is needed or available for the home.

Now consider an investor with a beach house rented at market rent for several weeks each year and used only briefly for personal purposes. If those facts satisfy the Revenue Procedure **2008-16** safe harbor, the beach house is investment property and can be exchanged into another rental to defer gain.

The asset category looks similar in both examples. The use of the property changes the answer.

## The Five-Year Rule and Non-Qualified Use, in Depth

The conversion that attracts the most attention is moving into property obtained through a 1031 exchange and later seeking a Section 121 exclusion. Section **121(d)(10)** generally requires ownership for at least **five years** before any Section 121 exclusion can apply to that 1031-acquired residence.

There is also the non-qualified-use rule. If an investor acquires a rental through a 1031, rents it for **two years**, moves in for **three** more years, and sells at the five-year mark, the ownership hurdle may be met. But roughly the portion of gain tied to the two investment years remains taxable as non-qualified use, and depreciation from the rental years is recaptured and cannot be excluded. The strategy may still work; it delivers less than the headline **$250,000/$500,000** result and requires planning for the full timeline.

## Renting Before You Sell, or Selling a Former Rental

The other direction is converting a home into a rental before an exchange. The question is intent and genuine use, not a fixed statutory number of days. Rent at a fair market rate, report and operate it as a rental, and hold it long enough for the facts to demonstrate an investment purpose.

The reverse case—converting a rental into a residence—remains subject to the five-year and non-qualified-use rules. In either direction, tax treatment follows the property’s real, demonstrable use. Let the facts change before relying on a new rule.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I 1031 exchange my primary home?

No. A 1031 exchange is limited to investment or business property. A primary residence uses the Section 121 exclusion instead, which may make a large share of gain tax-free.

### How much home-sale gain is tax-free?

Under Section 121, up to **$250,000** for a single filer and **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly, provided the home was owned and used as the main residence for **two of the last five years**.

### Can a vacation home qualify for a 1031 exchange?

Yes, if it is held for investment and meets the Revenue Procedure **2008-16** safe harbor: fair-market rental for at least **14 days** a year with limited personal use, for the **two years** before the exchange.

### Can I combine the Section 121 exclusion with a 1031 exchange?

Sometimes. On mixed-use property such as a live-in duplex, the personal-use portion may use Section 121 and the investment portion may use 1031. Depreciation on the rental portion is still recaptured.

### Can I move into a property I bought with a 1031 exchange?

Yes. To later use Section 121, you generally must own the property at least **five years**. The exclusion is reduced for the investment-use period, and prior depreciation is recaptured.

## Glossary

- **Section 121 Exclusion:** The home-sale break excluding up to **$250,000** for a single filer or **$500,000** for married couples filing jointly of qualifying primary-residence gain.
- **Revenue Procedure 2008-16:** The IRS safe harbor for when a vacation home is treated as held for investment and eligible for a 1031 exchange.
- **Non-Qualified Use:** Periods a home is used as an investment rather than as a residence; these reduce the Section 121 exclusion.
- **Mixed-Use Property:** Property serving both personal and investment purposes, where Section 121 and 1031 may each apply to a portion.
