# How Capital Gains Tax on Real Estate Works

I have been thinking about how often a property sale gets priced from the top down. The owner sees the sale price, subtracts the original purchase price, and assumes the gap is the gain. Then closing arrives and the actual tax calculation proves more complicated.

The headline capital-gains rate is only the first layer. Selling costs, adjusted basis, depreciation, a **3.8%** surtax, and state income tax can change the number materially. This guide describes the calculation as it stands in **2026** and outlines the legal ways a seller may defer or reduce the tax. It is general information, not tax advice. A CPA should run the actual figures before anything is signed.

First-order thinking is to focus on a purchase-to-sale spread and the federal rate. Second-order thinking is to calculate the full stack, ask which parts of the gain are recapture, consider state exposure and timing, then decide whether a deferral strategy actually fits the investor’s purpose and the replacement investment. Tax deferral does not turn a weak investment into a sound one.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Gain is net sale price minus adjusted basis—not sale price. Adjusted basis is original cost plus improvements less depreciation claimed.
> - More than one year generally means long-term gain at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**. A year or less is short-term gain taxed as ordinary income.
> - Two additional federal layers can apply: recapture up to **25%** on the §1250 portion and **3.8%** NIIT above **$200k** single / **$250k** joint.
> - States can tax gain again. A high-tax-state sale can lose roughly **25%** to **35%+** of gain to the combined stack.
> - Legal choices include a 1031 exchange, DST, Opportunity Zone fund, installment sale, §1033, the §121 exclusion, a charitable remainder trust, and a step-up at death.
> - Deferral is not avoidance. Most approaches move the tax to a later transaction, year, or taxpayer, and each has conditions and risks.

## How the gain is calculated

Capital-gains tax is applied to gain, not sale price. Gain is the amount realized—sale price less selling costs such as brokerage commission, title charges, and transfer taxes—minus adjusted basis. Adjusted basis starts with original cost, adds capital improvements, and subtracts depreciation claimed during ownership.

Depreciation is the part that catches people. It lowers taxable rental income every year, then reduces basis. A lower basis creates more gain when the property sells. A property bought for **$500,000**, improved with a documented **$100,000** renovation, and depreciated by **$150,000** has adjusted basis of **$450,000**, not **$600,000**. If it sells net for **$800,000**, the gain is **$350,000**, not the **$200,000** purchase-to-sale spread someone might calculate casually.

Basis should be built from records, not memory. A new roof, an addition, or a complete kitchen rebuild can increase basis; routine repairs generally do not because they were already deducted as expenses. The [capital gains tax calculator](capital-gains-tax-calculator.html) follows the same arithmetic, but a CPA needs to validate the records.

Prior ownership also matters. Property received in a 1031 exchange carries basis from the relinquished property, rather than beginning at the price paid for the replacement. That means a building bought for **$1 million** can have a much lower basis. Inherited property generally goes the other direction: basis steps up to fair-market value at the prior owner’s death. The same sale price can therefore produce very different gain depending on how the property was acquired.

## Short-term versus long-term rates

The cutoff is exactly one year. Hold a property for more than a year and the gain is long-term, taxed at the preferred federal rates of **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** according to taxable income. Hold it for a year or less and it is short-term gain, taxed at ordinary-income rates that are far above **20%** at the top. A flip that closes in eight months can fall in the high **30s** rather than the long-term **20%** category.

Most investment real estate is held for years. Still, the long-term rate is not the entire analysis. The 0/15/20% tier follows total taxable income, not the gain alone, and a large gain can push part of a return from the **15%** tier into the **20%** tier. The following are approximate **2026** figures, indexed for inflation and subject to annual change; filing status and current rules control.

| 2026 long-term rate | Single taxable income | Married filing jointly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **0%** | Up to about **$49,450** | Up to about **$98,900** |
| **15%** | About **$49,450** to **$545,500** | About **$98,900** to **$613,700** |
| **20%** | Above about **$545,500** | Above about **$613,700** |

These brackets are illustrative. Short-term gains use ordinary-income rates, not this table.

## The **3.8%** net investment income tax

NIIT adds **3.8%** to net investment income, including real-estate capital gains, once modified adjusted gross income crosses **$200,000** for a single filer or **$250,000** for married taxpayers filing jointly. It applies to the smaller of net investment income or the excess of MAGI over the threshold. A large one-time sale can therefore trigger it in full.

The thresholds are not indexed for inflation; they have not moved for more than a decade. Wage growth and a one-year gain can put a seller over the line even when ordinary income normally does not. For a high earner, NIIT takes a **20%** long-term federal rate to **23.8%** before state tax. The related [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html) memo explains the mechanics.

## Depreciation recapture

Depreciation recapture is often the unpleasant surprise. Depreciation lowered basis and provided deductions while the rental was owned. At sale, the portion of gain attributed to that depreciation—unrecaptured Section 1250 gain—can be taxed at a federal rate of up to **25%**, higher than the long-term **0%**/**15%**/**20%** rate on the remaining appreciation.

The sale is divided into pieces. In the **$350,000** example, **$150,000** of recaptured depreciation can be taxed up to **25%**, while the other **$200,000** of appreciation receives the long-term rate. NIIT can sit on top of both. Recapture can be large even if the sale price barely exceeds original cost, and it is not avoided by failing to claim depreciation: the IRS can recapture depreciation the owner was allowed to claim. See [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) for detailed edge cases.

## State income tax

Most states tax capital gain as ordinary income rather than using the federal preferential rate. Some states have no income tax and add no state layer for their residents. California and other high-tax jurisdictions can add low double digits — California’s top marginal rate exceeds 13%. The property’s location generally matters, not merely the seller’s residence, so an out-of-state rental can require another state return.

Stack the layers and the result can be heavy: long-term federal gain, recapture up to **25%** on part of the gain, the **3.8%** NIIT, and state income tax. A high-tax-state seller can lose roughly **25%** to **35%** or more of the gain. A given sale may face only some of these, depending on income, holding period, depreciation, and state. Confirm the calculation with a CPA.

| Tax layer | Applies to | Illustrative federal rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long-term capital gains | Appreciation portion of gain | **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** |
| Depreciation recapture (§1250) | Depreciation-driven portion | Up to **25%** |
| Net investment income tax | Gain after MAGI threshold | **3.8%** |
| State income tax | Full gain under state schedule | Varies from **0%** to low double digits |

## Ways to defer or reduce the tax

Real estate has more legal tools for deferring or reducing a gain than many assets. The important limitation is that most are deferral, not forgiveness: they move the tax to a later year, a later transaction, or a different taxpayer. Each has its own rules and risks.

- A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) can defer capital gains, recapture, and NIIT when proceeds are reinvested in like-kind property. Identify replacement property within **45 days**, close within **180 days**, acquire equal or greater value, and replace debt that was paid off. Miss a deadline and the deferral is lost.
- A [Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)](dst-guide.html) offers a passive way to complete a 1031 through a fractional interest in institutionally managed property. DSTs are securities, speculative and illiquid, sold only to verified accredited investors through a private placement memorandum, and principal can be lost.
- An [Opportunity Zone fund](opportunity-zones-guide.html) can take gain from any source, not only real estate. A qualified investment defers the gain; appreciation on the new investment can later be excluded if held long enough.
- An [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) under §453 spreads gain and tax across the years payments are collected, potentially keeping the seller in lower brackets. Depreciation recapture is generally taxed up front, not spread.
- A §1033 involuntary conversion can defer tax when property is lost to condemnation, casualty, or forced sale and proceeds are reinvested, on timelines more generous than a 1031.
- The §121 primary-residence exclusion can exclude up to **$250,000** for a single filer and **$500,000** for joint filers, but it is for a residence, not a rental.
- A charitable remainder trust can receive property, sell it without immediate tax inside the trust, provide an income stream, and leave the remainder to charity.
- A step-up in basis at death resets an heir’s basis to fair-market value and can eliminate built-in gain for the next generation.

Several approaches can be combined, but none guarantees a tax outcome. Compare [how to avoid capital gains tax on investment property](how-to-avoid-capital-gains-tax-investment-property.html) and [tax deferral strategies compared](tax-deferral-strategies-compared.html), then confirm the selected path with a CPA and attorney.

## A worked example

Suppose a rental was bought for **$500,000**, received **$100,000** of documented renovation, and claimed **$150,000** of depreciation. Adjusted basis is **$450,000**. It sells for **$830,000**, with **$30,000** of selling costs. Amount realized is **$800,000** and total gain is **$350,000**.

The first **$150,000** is unrecaptured §1250 gain, taxable federally at up to **25%**. The remaining **$200,000** is long-term appreciation, subject to **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** based on income, likely **15%** or **20%** in an example of this size. If MAGI clears the NIIT threshold, add **3.8%** across the gain. Then the applicable state rate applies to the full **$350,000**.

The combined federal-and-state amount can land around **$90,000** to **$120,000**, depending on bracket and state. That is why a 1031 that defers the whole bill should be considered before listing. The recapture and surtax are precisely the layers a headline rate misses. The numbers are illustrative, not a projection; actual basis, holding period, income, and state of residence determine the result.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is capital gains tax on real estate calculated?

It is calculated on gain, not sale price. Gain is net sale price—price less selling costs—minus adjusted basis: original cost plus capital improvements less depreciation. The result is then subject to the applicable rate, recapture, and surtaxes.

### What are the **2026** long-term capital-gains tax rates?

Federal long-term rates are **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by taxable income for property held more than one year. As an approximate **2026** guide, the **0%** tier is up to about **$49,450** single / **$98,900** joint, while the **20%** tier begins above about **$545,500** single / **$613,700** joint. A year or less is short-term ordinary income.

### What is the **3.8%** net investment income tax?

It is an additional **3.8%** tax on applicable net investment income, including real-estate gain, above **$200,000** MAGI for a single filer or **$250,000** for joint filers. The thresholds are not inflation indexed, and a one-time sale can trigger it.

### Why is depreciation recapture taxed higher than capital gains?

Prior depreciation reduced basis and sheltered ordinary income. At sale, the related unrecaptured Section 1250 gain can be taxed up to **25%**, higher than the long-term rate for remaining appreciation.

### How much of my gain will I actually lose to tax?

Income, state, and depreciation determine it. A high-income seller in a high-tax state can lose roughly **25%** to **35%** or more after long-term gain, recapture up to **25%**, **3.8%** NIIT, and state tax are combined. A modest-gain seller in a no-income-tax state can lose less.

### How can I defer or avoid capital gains tax on a property sale?

Legal tools include a 1031 exchange or passive DST version, Opportunity Zone fund, installment sale, §1033 involuntary conversion, §121 primary-residence exclusion, charitable remainder trust, and a hold through death for stepped-up basis. Most defer rather than eliminate tax, and each carries rules and risks.

### Does the primary-residence exclusion apply to a rental property?

No. §121 can exclude up to **$250,000** single / **$500,000** joint for a home used as a primary residence, generally for two of the last five years. A pure rental does not qualify, although a former residence has separate partial rules. Investors often evaluate a 1031 instead.

### Is a 1031 exchange the same as avoiding the tax forever?

No. A 1031 carries the tax into the replacement property’s basis until a sale without another exchange. A sequence of exchanges followed by a hold to death can lead to a step-up in basis for heirs, which is how some investors turn deferral into permanent savings.

## Glossary

- **Adjusted Basis:** Original cost plus capital improvements, less depreciation; subtracted from net sale price to find taxable gain.
- **Amount Realized:** Sale price less selling costs, including commissions, title, and transfer taxes.
- **Long-Term Capital Gain:** Gain on an asset held more than one year, federally taxed at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by income.
- **Short-Term Capital Gain:** Gain on an asset held one year or less, taxed at ordinary-income rates.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** **3.8%** surtax on applicable investment income above **$200,000** single / **$250,000** joint MAGI thresholds, which are not inflation indexed.
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain:** The depreciation-related part of real-estate gain, taxable federally at a maximum **25%** rate.
- **1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange under IRC §1031 that can defer capital gain, recapture, and NIIT when qualifying proceeds are reinvested within **45**- and **180**-day windows.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Resetting an inherited asset’s basis to fair-market value at death, which can eliminate built-in gain for heirs.
