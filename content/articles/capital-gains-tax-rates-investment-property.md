# Capital Gains Tax Rates on Investment Property

I keep seeing an investment-property sale reduced to one question: “What is the capital-gains rate?” It is a sensible first question, but it often produces an incomplete answer. A sale can bring federal long-term capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, the net investment income tax (NIIT), and state tax into the same closing.

That is the cost of not exchanging. A 1031 exchange can defer each applicable layer, rather than leaving the investor to reinvest only what remains after the bill. This is general education, not tax advice; current brackets and a particular transaction belong with a CPA.

First-order thinking is to focus on the headline rate. Second-order thinking is to calculate the combined rate on the actual gain, consider the property and taxpayer facts that drive it, and then ask whether the replacement property is worth owning even without the tax deferral. The tax benefit can be substantial. It does not make a weak investment sound.

## The four layers of an outright sale

An appreciated investment-property sale can trigger four separate taxes:

- Federal long-term capital gains on the appreciation portion of the gain.
- Depreciation recapture, generally described here as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, on depreciation taken during ownership.
- The **3.8%** NIIT for higher-income taxpayers when modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable threshold.
- State capital-gains tax, which varies with the state, and sometimes with residence, withholding, and clawback rules.

The larger the gain and the combined rate, the more tax a 1031 may defer. The rest of this guide explains how those layers work together, how adjusted basis enters the calculation, and why state facts can alter the result as much as federal ones.

## Holding period: long-term, short-term, and dealer property

Property held for more than one year may receive the long-term federal capital-gains rates of **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**, depending on taxable income—the familiar `0/15/20` framework. Property held for a year or less is generally short-term gain, taxed at ordinary-income rates that can reach **37%** federally. Most exchanges involve long-held investment property, but newer acquisitions require attention to the holding period.

Property held primarily for sale—dealer inventory or a flip—is different. It is ordinarily taxed as ordinary income and is not eligible for a 1031 exchange because it is not held for investment or business use. Intent and the holding pattern matter. An investor’s rental can be eligible where a flipper’s inventory is not; a taxpayer who has subdivided and sold lots should have a CPA address that line before relying on exchange treatment.

## Federal capital-gains brackets

The long-term bracket applies to appreciation: sale price less adjusted basis, before separating the depreciation-recapture portion. Lower-income taxpayers may fall into the **0%** or **15%** range; higher-income taxpayers can reach **20%**. The thresholds adjust periodically, so filing status and current-year figures matter.

A property gain does not stand alone. A large gain can move total income into a higher capital-gains bracket or above the NIIT threshold. That is why a model based only on the property price can be misleading.

## Depreciation recapture can be the surprise

The portion of gain tied to depreciation deductions—unrecaptured Section 1250 gain—is taxed at a maximum of **25%**. Years of deductions lower basis. On sale, that can create a recapture component that is larger than the capital-gains component, particularly for a long-held rental.

Recapture is meant to recover part of the benefit of those prior deductions. It is also recognized first when an exchange creates partial taxable gain, including through boot. Small recognized amounts can therefore cost more than an investor expects. See the related discussion of [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html).

## NIIT: the **3.8%** layer

The NIIT adds **3.8%** to applicable net investment income for higher-income taxpayers over the relevant modified-AGI thresholds. A one-time real-estate gain can put a seller over the line even when ordinary income alone would not. On a **$500,000** gain, **3.8%** is **$19,000**.

If a properly structured 1031 exchange recognizes no gain at the exchange, it defers NIIT with the other applicable layers. The mechanics and taxpayer facts still matter; the [NIIT overview](net-investment-income-tax.html) and [NIIT and 1031 resource](1031-exchange-net-investment-income-tax-niit.html) are useful companions.

## State tax is not a footnote

State treatment ranges widely. Texas, Florida, Nevada, Washington, and other no-income-tax states impose no state capital-gains tax in the way described here. California taxes gains as ordinary income, with top rates over **13%**. Many states land around **3–7%**. Georgia and Virginia are examples often around **5–6%**; New York and New Jersey can be in the high single digits to low double digits.

For a high-income seller in a high-tax state, state tax can rival or exceed federal capital-gains tax. The location of the property—and sometimes the seller’s residence—therefore changes the total amount deferred. State nonresident withholding can affect cash flow at closing, and some jurisdictions maintain clawback rules for gain deferred out of state.

## How the layers stack and how to calculate the effective rate

The layers apply to different bases but join in one tax bill. Recapture, up to **25%**, applies to the depreciated portion. Long-term capital gains, typically **15–20%** for many sellers with meaningful gain, apply to the appreciation portion. NIIT and state tax apply where the taxpayer and transaction meet their conditions.

The effective rate is total tax divided by total gain. A practical calculation is:

1. Calculate recapture: **25%** × depreciation taken, subject to the applicable rules.
2. Calculate long-term capital gains: **15%** or **20%** × appreciation.
3. Add NIIT: **3.8%** × gain, if applicable.
4. Add state tax: state rate × gain.
5. Divide the combined tax by total gain.

A heavily depreciated property in a high-tax state can put all four near their upper ranges, producing an effective rate from the mid-teens to over **35%**. A lower-income seller in a no-tax state might face **17%** or less. The [tax-deferral calculator discussion](how-much-tax-does-a-1031-exchange-defer.html) can help frame the question, but it is a planning estimate rather than a tax return.

## Adjusted basis drives the gain

Adjusted basis begins with original cost, adds capital improvements, and subtracts depreciation. A lower basis means more gain. Improvements increase basis and reduce the gain; depreciation reduces basis and increases both total gain and the recapture component.

Good records of capital improvements can materially change an eventual tax result. Accurate basis tracking becomes more important through multiple exchanges because basis carries forward. A CPA’s records and a current calculation are the right places to resolve it.

## What a 1031 changes—and what it does not

A 1031 exchange defers the applicable capital-gains, recapture, NIIT, and state-tax layers by reinvesting in like-kind property and carrying basis and deferred gain into the replacement. Instead of reinvesting after a current tax payment, the investor can keep the full exchange equity working. That deferred tax can continue compounding in the replacement property; see [how a 1031 can build long-term wealth](how-a-1031-exchange-builds-long-term-wealth.html).

Deferral is not elimination during life. Deferred gain is generally recognized if the replacement property is sold without another exchange. A final hold through death can involve a step-up in basis, but that is a tax-planning issue, not a promise. An exchange is especially consequential for sellers with substantial depreciation and a high state rate because their combined rate is steep; it still should be judged against the quality, leverage, liquidity, and suitability of the replacement investment.

## Worked examples: why the headline rate is not enough

**Example 1: high-income California seller.** A **$500,000** gain includes **$150,000** of recapture. Federal capital gains of **20%** on **$350,000** equal **$70,000**. Recapture at **25%** on **$150,000** equals **$37,500**. NIIT at **3.8%** on **$500,000** equals **$19,000**. California tax at about **13%** on **$500,000** equals **$65,000**. The combined result is about **$191,500**, or roughly **38%**.

**Example 2: moderate-income Georgia seller.** A **$300,000** gain includes **$80,000** of recapture. Federal capital gains at **15%** on **$220,000** equal **$33,000**; recapture at **25%** on **$80,000** equals **$20,000**; NIIT does not apply because income is below the threshold; and Georgia tax at about **5.75%** on **$300,000** is about **$17,250**. Total: about **$70,250**, or near **23%**.

**Example 3: Texas seller in a lower bracket.** A **$200,000** gain includes **$40,000** of recapture. Federal long-term gain at **15%** on **$160,000** is **$24,000**. Recapture at **25%** on **$40,000** is **$10,000**. With no NIIT and no state tax, the total is **$34,000**, or **17%**.

These figures are illustrative and depend on current law and individual facts. They show a **17%** to **38%** range on comparable transaction types, driven by income, depreciation, and state. Each is the amount a qualifying 1031 could defer.

## Withholding, clawback, and planning before closing

For nonresident sellers, many states require the closing agent to withhold a percentage of sale price or gain and remit it to the state; the seller later reconciles it in the return. A documented exchange can often be exempt, but that is a state-specific question and a qualified intermediary needs to document the transaction correctly.

California also illustrates a clawback rule: it can track deferred gain from California property exchanged into an out-of-state replacement and claim its portion if the gain is ultimately recognized, even after the investor has moved. The federal analysis is unchanged, but the rule affects cash flow, logistics, and ultimate state exposure. A seller exchanging across state lines should confirm withholding and clawback rules with a CPA before the sale.

Timing can also matter. Holding more than a year protects the long-term treatment. Selling in a lower-income year may reduce the bracket or keep income below the NIIT threshold. Capital-improvement records can raise basis. An [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) may be another approach to model. For investors staying in real estate, a 1031 is the broad planning tool that can defer each applicable layer, but the structure and timing should be coordinated with a CPA and a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the capital-gains tax rates on investment property?

Long-term gains can be **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by income; recapture can reach **25%**; NIIT is **3.8%** when applicable; and state rates run from **0%** to over **13%**. A 1031 can defer applicable layers.

### What is the long-term versus short-term distinction?

More than one year generally qualifies for long-term treatment; a year or less is short-term ordinary income, up to **37%** federally. Most exchanges involve the long-term category.

### Why is depreciation recapture higher?

It recovers a portion of prior depreciation deductions. Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain can be taxed up to **25%**, and can be the largest layer for a long-held rental.

### Does the **3.8%** NIIT apply to property sales?

It can when modified AGI exceeds the applicable threshold. A large gain can create that result; an exchange can defer it when no gain is recognized at the exchange.

### How do states differ?

The range goes from no state layer in states such as Texas, Florida, Nevada, and Washington to over **13%** in California, with many states around **3–7%**. The state layer can be the largest part of the bill.

### How do I find my effective rate?

Add applicable recapture, long-term gain, NIIT, and state tax, then divide by total gain. The result may range from the mid-teens to over **35%**.

### How does a 1031 affect the bill?

It defers applicable capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax when the exchange requirements are satisfied. Basis carries into the replacement, so the tax is deferred, not simply erased.

### Why does adjusted basis matter?

Gain is sale price less adjusted basis: cost plus improvements minus depreciation. Improvements raise basis; depreciation lowers it and increases both gain and recapture.

### Are flips and dealer property different?

Yes. Property held primarily for sale is ordinary income property and is not eligible for a 1031 exchange. A qualifying rental held for investment is a different case.

### What is the highest combined rate?

A high-income seller with a heavily depreciated property in a high-tax state can exceed **35%** after combining **20%** federal gain on appreciation, up to **25%** recapture, **3.8%** NIIT, and state tax over **13%**.

### Does the location of sale matter?

Yes. Property location and sometimes residence determine the state layer. California and Texas can differ by tens of thousands of dollars on the same gain, and withholding or clawback rules may matter.

### Can timing reduce the rate?

Sometimes. A lower-income year can affect the capital-gains bracket and NIIT, and a hold over one year can qualify for long-term treatment. An exchange remains the broad deferral tool for a seller continuing in real estate.

### Does a 1031 eliminate tax?

No. It defers tax while the investor exchanges. Gain generally returns on a taxable sale; a final hold through death can have separate step-up-in-basis implications.

### Do improvements reduce tax?

Capital improvements raise adjusted basis and can reduce taxable gain. Keep records both for a sale and for basis carried into an exchange.

### Where can I estimate the tax?

Use Baker 1031’s [capital-gains and 1031 calculators](calculators.html) as a planning estimate based on sale price, basis, depreciation, state, and income, then confirm the exact figures with a CPA.

### Will my state withhold tax at closing?

Many states withhold on nonresident real-estate sales. A properly documented exchange is often exempt, but state rules and the intermediary’s paperwork control the result.

### What is a clawback rule?

Some states, including California, track deferred in-state gain moved to out-of-state property and claim their share when it is later recognized. Confirm the facts before an interstate exchange.

### Are these rates the same every year?

The **0%**/**15%**/**20%** structure, **25%** recapture maximum, and **3.8%** NIIT framework have been stable, but income thresholds and state rates change. Use current-year figures.

### How much can California versus Texas change the result?

The federal layers are the same, but California’s top state rate over **13%** and Texas’s lack of state capital-gains tax can create about **$65,000** of difference on a **$500,000** gain.

### What should I do now?

Model the gain, depreciation, income, and state treatment with your CPA before listing. If a 1031 fits, assess a suitable replacement and explore [Delaware Statutory Trusts](delaware-statutory-trusts.html) only through the offering documents and suitability process.

## Glossary

- **Long-Term Capital Gains:** Gain on property held over one year, federally taxed at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by income.
- **Short-Term Capital Gains:** Gain on property held for a year or less, taxed at ordinary-income rates up to **37%**.
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain:** Depreciation-related real-estate gain subject to a maximum **25%** rate.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on gain attributable to prior depreciation, up to **25%**.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A **3.8%** surtax for applicable higher-income taxpayers.
- **State Capital Gains Tax:** State-level gain tax, from **0%** to over **13%** in the examples described.
- **Effective Tax Rate:** Total tax divided by total gain.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Original cost plus improvements minus depreciation.
- **Dealer Property:** Property held primarily for sale, taxed as ordinary income and ineligible for 1031 treatment.
- **Capital Improvement:** A basis-increasing cost that can reduce taxable gain.
- **Modified AGI:** Adjusted gross income with certain add-backs, used for NIIT applicability.
- **Holding Period:** Ownership duration that determines long-term or short-term treatment.
- **Carryover Basis:** Basis from the relinquished property that continues in the replacement property.
