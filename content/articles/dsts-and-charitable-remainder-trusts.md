# DSTs and Charitable Remainder Trusts

I keep seeing appreciated real estate framed as a single tax problem. It is usually a capital-allocation and legacy problem first. The tax bill matters, but so does the question underneath it: do you want to preserve the asset for your family, or make an irrevocable gift to charity while retaining an income stream?

Baker 1031 Research updated this educational guide in June 2026. A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) used in a 1031 exchange and a Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT) can both arise when an owner has a highly appreciated property. They are not interchangeable. A 1031-into-DST can defer gain, keep an interest in the investor’s estate, provide passive real-estate income over a defined hold, and potentially support a step-up in basis at death. A CRT is an irrevocable trust: appreciated property is contributed to it, the tax-exempt trust can sell without immediate capital-gains tax at the trust level, income can be paid for a term or life, a partial charitable income-tax deduction may be available, and the remainder goes to charity.

First-order thinking says, “Which structure gets the gain out of sight today?” Second-order thinking asks what becomes irreversible, who ultimately owns the value, how taxable distributions work, and whether the income, estate, charitable, liquidity, sponsor, and concentration trade-offs fit the family’s actual plan. A CRT has IRS-set minimum and maximum payout rates and a minimum remainder value, and DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after a suitability review. Bring your estate-planning attorney and CPA in before you do anything.

## How CRTs work

A CRT is an irrevocable trust to which you contribute appreciated property. Once the property is in the trust, the CRT can sell it without immediate capital-gains tax because the trust is tax-exempt. That is different from selling the property yourself. The trust then pays an income stream to you or other named beneficiaries for a set number of years or for life. At the end of that period, the remaining value—the remainder—passes to one or more charities you select.

The contribution generally creates a [partial charitable income-tax deduction](glossary.html) in the year of the gift. It is based on the present value of the charity’s future remainder interest, not simply the full value of the contributed property. Distributions to you are taxable under detailed rules as they are made.

There are two familiar CRT forms. A charitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT) pays a fixed dollar amount. A charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT) pays a fixed percentage of the trust’s value, recalculated annually. Each has different characteristics. Neither changes the central fact: a CRT is irrevocable. Once the property is contributed, the decision is permanent and consequential.

## Comparing a DST and a CRT

Both tools address appreciated real estate, but they move in different directions. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) into a DST defers capital-gains tax and keeps the asset in the investor’s ownership and estate. It may produce passive real-estate income during a defined hold. If the DST interest is held until death, it may pass to heirs with a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) that can erase the deferred gain.

A CRT takes the other route. You give the asset to an irrevocable charitable trust. The trust can avoid immediate capital-gains tax at sale, pay an income stream, and support a partial deduction, but the asset ultimately goes to charity rather than to heirs.

The contrast is keeping versus giving. A DST generally suits an investor who wants to retain value for themselves and their heirs while deferring tax. A CRT generally suits an investor with a real charitable objective who is willing to give the asset away in exchange for income, a deduction, and the trust-level treatment of the sale.

Some broader plans use both. An investor with several appreciated assets might use a 1031/DST path for one asset intended for heirs and contribute other assets to a CRT for charitable and income goals. A DST might also be one component of an estate plan that includes charitable giving. That combination is highly fact-specific. It reaches income-tax, estate-tax, capital-gains, and charitable rules at the same time.

## Key takeaways

- A CRT is an irrevocable, tax-exempt trust that can sell contributed appreciated property without immediate capital-gains tax, pay income, support a partial deduction, and leave its remainder to charity.
- A DST can keep the asset in your estate for heirs while deferring gain; a CRT gives the asset to charity in exchange for income, a deduction, and no immediate trust-level gain on sale.
- The choice rests on legacy goals: preserving value for heirs or funding a charitable legacy. Some plans use both for different assets and objectives.
- CRTs are complex and irrevocable. Coordinate closely with your estate-planning attorney and CPA. This article is educational information only, not advice.

## Income and tax benefits

A DST can provide passive real-estate income: your share of rental income from the trust’s property during a defined hold. It can defer the gain on the property you exchanged. If the interest is held until death, it may pass to heirs with a step-up in basis, potentially eliminating the deferred gain. Its benefit set is deferral, current income, and a possible tax-efficient transfer to heirs.

A CRT offers a different mix. Because the tax-exempt trust sells the appreciated property, the full value can be reinvested for income without immediate capital-gains tax at the trust level. The trust pays income to you or your beneficiaries for the chosen term or life. Those distributions are taxable under specific ordering rules. The contribution generally provides a partial charitable income-tax deduction in the year of the gift, based on the present value of the charity’s remainder interest.

Both structures can pay income. They diverge on the legacy and the deduction. The DST retains the asset for heirs while deferring tax. The CRT transfers the asset to charity while adding the charitable deduction and avoiding immediate tax at the trust level. That legacy difference is the decision.

## Charitable legacy goals

For an investor with a genuine charitable goal, the CRT makes the charitable legacy part of the design. After the income term, the remainder goes to the charity or charities you designate. It can align philanthropic intent with income and the tax treatment described above.

A DST does not carry a charitable purpose by itself. It is directed toward retaining the asset’s value within the family, deferring tax, and potentially passing the interest to heirs with a step-up in basis. An owner focused on maximizing what goes to heirs would generally lean toward the DST path. An owner who wants to give to charity while retaining an income stream may explore a CRT, or use a CRT for part of their wealth.

Those are not small distinctions. A CRT’s charitable commitment is permanent because the asset leaves your estate. Careful design is necessary if a plan uses both approaches.

## Complexity, risks, and trade-offs

The CRT’s key risk is its irrevocability. Once property is contributed and the trust is established, you generally cannot reverse the contribution or reclaim the asset. The remainder will go to charity rather than heirs. For some families that is the intended result; for others it is the decisive drawback.

CRT qualification is also technical. Minimum and maximum payout rates and a minimum value for the charitable remainder must be satisfied. Income, deduction, and tax rules are intricate. There can be establishment and administration costs, and the income stream depends on the performance of the trust’s reinvested assets. A poor design or administrative mistake can be costly and may be hard or impossible to repair.

DSTs have their own risks: illiquidity, sponsor risk, concentration, fees, and the technical requirements of a valid 1031 exchange. They do not independently solve charitable objectives. Comparing or combining DSTs and CRTs compounds the work because the decision depends on assets, family circumstances, charitable intent, income needs, and the individual tax picture. Rules can change.

A CRT is irrevocable and its asset goes to charity, not heirs. This is not a self-directed choice to make from a general article. It is a professional-guidance decision with your estate-planning attorney and CPA at the table.

## Coordinating advisors

The attorney and CPA are central here. The estate-planning attorney evaluates whether a CRT fits, designs and drafts the trust, helps select its type and payout, and places it within the broader estate plan and family situation. The CPA evaluates capital-gains treatment, the charitable deduction, the income taxation of distributions, and the overall tax picture over time.

A financial professional can help with the investment component when a 1031/DST path is appropriate. The three roles are interdependent. Start the coordination before you contribute property or sign anything, particularly because a CRT generally cannot be undone once established.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT)?

A CRT is an irrevocable trust to which you contribute appreciated property. The tax-exempt trust can sell without immediate capital-gains tax, then pay income to you or other named beneficiaries for a set term or life. The remainder ultimately passes to designated charities. A contribution generally supports a partial charitable income-tax deduction based on the present value of that remainder. A CRAT pays a fixed dollar amount; a CRUT pays a percentage recalculated annually. The choice is permanent and consequential, so consult your estate-planning attorney and CPA. This is educational information only, not advice.

### How is a CRT different from a DST?

A 1031 exchange into a DST defers gain, retains the asset in your ownership and estate, can provide passive income, and may support a step-up in basis for heirs. A CRT gives the asset to an irrevocable charitable trust; the trust sells without immediate tax at the trust level, pays income and can generate a partial deduction, but the asset goes to charity rather than heirs. The core distinction is keeping versus giving. Both are complex and fact-specific, and an attorney and CPA should evaluate the fit.

### Can I avoid capital-gains tax with a CRT?

A CRT can avoid immediate capital-gains tax when the tax-exempt trust sells contributed property, allowing full sale value to be reinvested. That is not the same as a permanent elimination of all tax. Income paid to you is taxable under detailed ordering rules, and capital gain can be carried out to you over time through distributions. A DST defers gain under a 1031 exchange and may receive a step-up at death. The actual CRT treatment depends on trust type, payout, and your circumstances; your CPA must analyze it before you act.

### What income does a CRT pay?

A CRT pays income to you or named beneficiaries for a term or life. A CRAT pays a fixed annual dollar amount. A CRUT pays a fixed percentage of the trust value, recalculated annually, so its dollar payment changes with value. Qualification requires minimum and maximum payout rates and a minimum charitable remainder value. The income is taxable under detailed ordering rules. Your attorney and CPA should determine the appropriate structure and payout.

### Do I get a tax deduction for a CRT?

Generally, a contribution produces a partial charitable income-tax deduction in the year of the gift. It equals the present value of the charity’s future remainder interest, calculated with IRS factors that account for the payout rate, term or life expectancy, and prevailing interest rates. It is partial because you keep an income interest. Usual charitable-deduction limits can apply, with carryforward rules when the amount exceeds the annual limit. The rules can change, and your CPA must calculate the deduction for you.

### Can I use both a DST and a CRT?

Sometimes, but only with careful professional design. An owner with multiple appreciated assets might direct some real estate into a 1031/DST path for heirs and other assets to a CRT for charitable income and deduction objectives. A DST may be an investment component of a broader estate plan with charitable giving. Do not casually mix the two on the same asset: the 1031 and CRT rules are separate, detailed, and consequential. A CRT is irrevocable, so an attorney and CPA must design and evaluate any plan that uses both.

### Is a CRT irrevocable?

Yes. Once you contribute property and establish the CRT, you generally cannot undo the trust or reclaim the asset. The asset eventually leaves your estate for charity rather than heirs. That may be right for charitable legacy goals, but it is a major trade-off for a family-first legacy plan. A poor design or changed circumstances can be difficult to address. A DST retains the asset in the estate and does not require a charitable transfer. Do not make an irrevocable CRT decision hastily or from a general article.

### Which is better for my heirs, a DST or a CRT?

For maximizing what passes to heirs, a DST is usually the more direct path. It preserves the asset in the estate, defers gain under a 1031 exchange, and may pass with a step-up in basis. A CRT can pay income to you or beneficiaries, but its remainder goes to charity, not family. Some plans use other assets, including life insurance, to replace charitable-transfer value for heirs. The correct balance of family and charitable objectives is a job for your estate-planning attorney and CPA.

### Why is coordinating with advisors so important here?

DSTs, CRTs, and any plan that combines them sit at the intersection of income-tax, estate-tax, capital-gains, and charitable-giving rules. Your asset mix, family situation, charitable intent, income needs, and tax position govern the answer. The attorney designs and drafts; the CPA evaluates the tax results; and a financial professional can assist with a suitable DST component. Their work should be coordinated before a contribution or signature because a CRT’s errors can be costly or irreversible.

### What are the estate-planning benefits of DSTs?

DST estate planning can combine capital-gains deferral with a potential fair-market-value step-up in basis for heirs. A 1031 exchange into a DST shifts an owner from active landlording to passive real-estate ownership while remaining invested. Fractional interests can be easier to divide among several heirs than one physical property, and heirs avoid direct management responsibilities. The tax and estate details are technical and fact-specific; your attorney and CPA should confirm the result.

### Does the income from a CRT get taxed?

Yes. Although the trust avoids immediate capital-gains tax at sale, distributions to you are taxable under a tiered ordering system. They are generally treated first as ordinary income, then capital gains, then other income, and finally tax-free return of principal, to the extent the trust has each type. Capital gain can therefore be recognized gradually through distributions. The result depends on the trust’s investments and performance and requires CPA analysis.

### Can a CRT hold real estate or a DST?

The mechanics of a CRT holding real estate or a DST require qualified professional review. Typically, an owner contributes appreciated real estate, the CRT sells it at the trust level, and proceeds are reinvested to generate the income stream. Whether a CRT can hold a specific asset, how a DST would interact with 1031 rules, and how debt or other asset rules apply are complex questions. Do not assume a CRT can simply hold a DST in a particular way; ask your estate-planning attorney and CPA before contributing property.

### Who should consider a CRT?

A CRT may be worth exploring for an owner of highly appreciated property who wants income and has genuine charitable intent, accepts the eventual charitable transfer, and values the partial deduction and trust-level sale treatment. It is usually not an answer for someone whose primary goal is to maximize what passes to family or who is uncomfortable with an irrevocable commitment. The attorney and CPA must assess whether it fits the full circumstances.

### What are the risks and drawbacks of a CRT?

The fundamental drawback is irrevocability: the asset leaves the estate and goes to charity. Qualification standards, taxable distributions, present-value deduction calculations, establishment and administration costs, and exposure to the performance of reinvested assets add risk. Errors in design or administration can be costly and hard to repair. A CRT can be useful, but it is unforgiving and requires careful professional design.

## Glossary

- **Charitable Remainder Trust (CRT):** An irrevocable trust paying income to you, with the remainder to charity.
- **Irrevocable Trust:** A trust that generally cannot be undone once established.
- **Remainder Interest:** The portion of a CRT that passes to charity at the end of the term.
- **Charitable Remainder Annuity Trust (CRAT):** A CRT paying a fixed dollar amount annually.
- **Charitable Remainder Unitrust (CRUT):** A CRT paying a fixed percentage of value, recalculated annually.
- **Charitable Deduction:** The partial income-tax deduction for the remainder’s present value.
- **Tax-Exempt Trust:** A CRT that can sell appreciated property without immediate gains tax.
- **Income Stream:** The payments a CRT makes to you for a term or life.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** 1031-eligible fractional real estate kept in your estate.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.
- **Capital-Gains Deferral:** Postponing the tax on a property-sale gain via a DST/1031.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain for heirs.
- **Estate Planning:** Arranging assets for income, tax efficiency, and legacy.
- **Charitable Legacy:** A planned gift to charity built into a structure like a CRT.
- **Ordering Rules:** The tiered system characterizing taxable CRT distributions.
- **Estate-Planning Attorney:** The professional who designs and drafts these structures.
