# Building a DST Ladder for Staggered Liquidity


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I start with the capital an investor might need before a sponsor sells the property. A Delaware Statutory Trust has no early exit. It can provide real estate income, but it generally keeps capital committed until the sponsor sells the underlying property, often years from now.

First-order thinking says a DST ladder makes an illiquid investment liquid. Second-order thinking is more useful: the ladder cannot make a single DST sellable on demand. It can spread expected full-cycle exits across several trusts, which can create periodic liquidity and reinvestment windows instead of one lump-sum exit.

That is the purpose of this guide: the illiquidity problem, the bond-ladder analogy, staggered expected holds, reinvestment at each full cycle, and fitting a ladder into a diversified DST portfolio. Hold periods are sponsor-controlled estimates, not promises. This is educational information, not investment advice; Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice, so verify current rules with your advisors. The hold mechanics in [the definitive DST guide](dst-guide.html) are useful background before building a ladder.

## The Illiquidity Challenge

Illiquidity is a defining DST constraint. An investor generally remains in the trust until its sponsor sells the underlying property after a multi-year hold. There is limited or no secondary market for fractional DST interests, so an investor cannot readily sell an interest to retrieve capital sooner. A DST is therefore appropriate only for capital that can remain invested through the hold.

For many 1031 investors, that trade-off can be acceptable for tax deferral, passive income, and diversification. But putting all exchange proceeds into one DST—or several trusts expected to sell at the same time—creates an all-or-nothing profile: capital stays locked up, then returns in one large amount that must be redeployed at once. There is no intermediate access and one concentrated reinvestment decision.

Illiquidity is the cost of those DST benefits. It can be planned around, but not removed. A ladder addresses the lumpy timing of a large DST allocation; it does not alter the fact that the capital is committed until properties sell.

## What a DST Ladder Is

A DST ladder holds several DSTs with staggered expected hold or exit periods. As different trusts go full-cycle, sell property, and return capital, the investor receives a series of smaller liquidity and reinvestment windows instead of one concentrated exit.

The analogy is a bond ladder. Bonds maturing in different years return principal periodically. A DST ladder applies the timing idea to illiquid real estate: some trusts may be expected to exit earlier, some in the middle, and some later. Each sale returns capital and creates a choice to reinvest through a new 1031 exchange or take cash.

No individual DST becomes liquid. Each remains illiquid until its sponsor sells. The potential benefit exists at the portfolio level: a rolling series of access points rather than one distant date. A ladder turns one expected exit into several expected windows, not an on-demand withdrawal right.

## Staggering Expected Hold Periods

The mechanism is simple. DSTs commonly have an anticipated hold of several years before the sponsor expects to sell and return capital. Selecting offerings with differing anticipated holds aims to place their expected full-cycle exits at different points in time. A wider spread of expected holds should produce a wider spread of liquidity windows.

The caveat matters as much as the structure. Expected hold periods are estimates, not guarantees. Sponsors control sale timing, and actual exits can be earlier or later due to market conditions, property performance, financing, and sponsor judgment. A trust projected to sell in year five might sell in year four, year seven, or remain held longer in a difficult market.

A ladder is therefore a plan, not a promise. Building cushion into the schedule and avoiding reliance on any single exit date is prudent. Staggering improves the likelihood of periodic liquidity; it cannot promise precise timing.

## Key Takeaways

- DSTs are illiquid with no early exit; a ladder holds several DSTs with staggered expected exits to address the resulting timing problem.
- Different full-cycle sales can create periodic liquidity and reinvestment windows, much like a bond ladder.
- Expected holds are sponsor-controlled estimates, not guarantees, so the strategy improves staggering without promising precise timing.
- At each full-cycle, proceeds can enter a new 1031 exchange to continue deferral or be taken in cash, with deferred tax then due.

## Reinvestment at Each Full-Cycle

When a sponsor sells a DST property, the trust winds down and each investor receives a share of proceeds. That creates a real decision point: reinvest to continue deferring capital-gains tax, or take cash and pay the deferred tax then due. The ladder's periodic exits are intended to provide recurring opportunities to make that choice.

The usual reinvestment route is a new [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), moving proceeds into another DST or other like-kind real property. That can extend the ladder by replacing a matured rung with a new position while keeping capital invested in real estate and gain deferred. It also lets an investor reassess sector, sponsor, and geography at each turn.

Taking cash can be appropriate for an investor who needs liquidity or wants to leave real estate, but the deferred tax becomes due. Every mature rung is a fresh decision—not an automatic rollover—and a moment to reconsider the allocation.

## Building Your Ladder

Choose DSTs with expected holds that vary rather than cluster. An investor might pair shorter anticipated holds with medium and longer ones, aiming for an exit pattern that fits the desired timing of capital availability. Since projected holds are uncertain, the plan should include cushion and be revisited as trusts mature and proceeds are reinvested.

Laddering can work alongside diversification. The same set of positions can spread exposure across sponsors, asset classes such as multifamily, industrial, and net-lease, and geographies. The ladder spreads timing risk; diversification spreads exposure to any one property, sponsor, or sector. Both matter.

Each DST still needs to be sized appropriately, vetted, and suitable. The goal is a diversified DST portfolio that provides periodic expected windows while remaining consistent with risk tolerance—not a collection of positions chosen only for a projected exit date.

## Limits and Cautions

The central limit is plain: a DST ladder does not create true liquidity. No interest becomes sellable on demand. Capital comes back only when a sponsor sells property, on a schedule the investor does not control. Commit only capital that can stay invested through the holds and retain separate, genuinely liquid reserves for needs that cannot wait.

Timing can also disappoint. Actual exits can bunch together or stretch out, and difficult markets can delay multiple sponsors' sales at once. Reinvesting each return through a 1031 exchange also brings identification and closing deadlines, a need for suitable replacement property, and new fees and risks. More positions mean more sponsors and deals to evaluate.

The ladder can reduce, not eliminate, the lumpiness and uncertainty of DST liquidity. It should not be sold as an emergency fund or a substitute for genuine liquidity.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a DST ladder?

It is a portfolio of DSTs with staggered expected hold or exit periods. Different trusts can go full-cycle at different times, returning capital in a series of smaller windows rather than one large exit. Like a bond ladder, it spreads timing. It does not make an individual DST liquid: each interest remains illiquid until its property is sold. At each return, an investor can reinvest through a new 1031 exchange or take cash.

### Why are DSTs illiquid?

They offer no early exit and have little or no meaningful secondary market. A beneficial owner generally holds the trust until the sponsor sells its property after a multi-year hold. Unlike a publicly traded security, there is no dependable exchange for selling a fractional interest. That structural illiquidity can be an acceptable trade-off for tax deferral, passive income, and diversification, but only for capital that is not needed during the hold. A ladder can spread exits; it cannot make an individual DST sellable on demand.

### How does a DST ladder create liquidity?

It creates periodic *expected* liquidity by selecting DSTs with different anticipated hold periods. When each sponsor sells, proceeds return and a reinvestment or cash decision follows. The resulting sequence is an improvement over one concentrated exit, but it is not true on-demand liquidity. Sale dates are estimated and sponsor-controlled, so the windows can arrive early or late.

### Are DST hold periods guaranteed?

No. An anticipated hold is an estimate. Market conditions, property performance, financing availability, and sponsor judgment can shift an exit. A DST projected for year five could sell in year four, year seven, or later in a difficult market. Build cushion, do not depend on a single date, and commit only capital that can remain invested. Treat a ladder as an informed plan, never a fixed calendar.

### What happens when a DST goes full-cycle?

The sponsor sells the property, the DST winds down, and the investor receives a share of proceeds. The investor can use a new 1031 exchange to acquire another DST or other like-kind real property and continue tax deferral, which replaces a matured rung. Or the investor can take cash, gaining liquidity while paying deferred tax. Each sale is a chance to reassess sector, sponsor, geography, and whether the strategy still fits. Coordinate the mechanics and timing with a CPA and qualified intermediary.

### How do I build a DST ladder?

Select positions with varied expected holds so anticipated exits are spread across time. Combine shorter, medium, and longer expected holds, include cushion because the dates are uncertain, and reconsider the plan as positions mature. At the same time, diversify across sponsors, asset classes, and geographies. Size each position sensibly after accounting for minimums, vet each sponsor and deal, and make sure the whole allocation remains suitable.

### Does a DST ladder make my investment liquid?

No. That is the most important caution. A ladder smooths and spreads potential exit events, but no DST becomes sellable on demand and the sponsor controls when a property is sold. It improves a large allocation's liquidity profile; it does not create cash access whenever needed. Maintain liquid reserves outside the ladder.

### How is a DST ladder like a bond ladder?

Both use staggered timing: bonds maturing in different years return principal periodically, and DSTs expected to exit at different times can return capital in a series of windows. The crucial difference is certainty. Bond maturities are generally contractual and fixed; a DST hold period is a sponsor-controlled estimate. The concept is similar, but DST timing is far less precise.

### Can I diversify while building a DST ladder?

Yes. A ladder can include a shorter-expected-hold multifamily DST from one sponsor, a medium-hold industrial DST from another, and a longer-hold net-lease DST from a third. That spreads timing, property type, sponsor, and geography. The ladder reduces concentration in timing; diversification reduces exposure to one property, sponsor, or sector. A broker-dealer can help structure an allocation that pursues both, with appropriate position sizes and suitability.

### What are the risks of a DST ladder?

The strategy carries the underlying DST risks plus timing and complexity risks. Exits arrive only when sponsors sell, holds can bunch or stretch, and difficult markets can delay several sales together. Repeated 1031 exchanges bring identification and closing deadlines and a need for suitable replacement property. Each new DST brings fees, sponsor risk, concentration, and real-estate risk; more positions mean more diligence. A ladder reduces but does not eliminate lumpiness, and it is not a substitute for genuine liquidity. Vet sponsors, size prudently, and maintain separate liquid reserves.

### How many DSTs should be in a ladder?

There is no fixed number. It depends on allocation size, desired timing spread, available minimums, and the ability to vet and monitor several positions. A handful with sufficiently varied expected holds can provide useful staggering. More trusts can widen the timing spread and diversification but can create positions too small to matter or an allocation that is hard to manage. Aim for a sensible spread of anticipated exits and adequate diversification, with every position well-vetted and suitable.

### What if all my DSTs sell at the same time?

It can happen despite a ladder. Strong markets may lead several sponsors to sell at once, while weak markets can delay multiple exits together. If capital returns simultaneously, the investor faces the lump-sum redeployment challenge the strategy sought to reduce, including 1031 deadlines if deferral will continue. Extra cushion, genuinely varied expected holds, sponsor diversification, and advance reinvestment options can help, but cannot guarantee against bunching. Coordinate a large simultaneous exchange carefully with a CPA and qualified intermediary.

### Can I keep extending a DST ladder indefinitely?

In principle, an investor can replace each matured rung through a new 1031 exchange, keeping capital in real estate and gain deferred for many years. Held-until-death interests may receive a step-up in basis for heirs under current law—the source of the “swap till you drop” idea. But each extension requires available suitable replacement property, timely compliance with identification and closing deadlines, new fees and risks, and a continuing fit with goals, age, and circumstances. It is a fresh decision at every full-cycle, not an automatic continuation. Review it with CPA and estate advisors.

### Is a DST ladder right for me?

It may be worth considering for an accredited investor placing substantial capital—often 1031 proceeds—into DSTs, who wants passive income and tax deferral, values periodic windows over one distant exit, and can leave capital committed through the holds. It is less suitable for someone needing ready access to capital, unable to tolerate real-estate risk and illiquidity, or lacking enough capital for sensible diversification. Suitability depends on capital, time horizon, liquidity needs, risk tolerance, and goals. The strategy improves timing and diversification but does not change the basic character of a DST.

## Glossary

**DST Ladder**  
Holding several DSTs with staggered expected exits for periodic liquidity.

**Illiquidity**  
The inability to readily sell a DST interest before the property is sold.

**No Early Exit**  
The feature that a DST is held until the sponsor sells the property.

**Secondary Market**  
A limited, unreliable market for reselling DST interests.

**Expected Hold Period**  
The anticipated, sponsor-controlled time before a DST sells its property.

**Staggering**  
Choosing varied expected holds so DST exits fall at different times.

**Full-Cycle**  
When a DST sells its property and returns capital to investors.

**Reinvestment Window**  
The period after a full-cycle exit to redeploy proceeds or take cash.

**1031 Exchange**  
A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Qualified Intermediary**  
The party that facilitates a 1031 exchange and holds proceeds.

**Diversified DST Portfolio**  
DSTs spread across sponsors, asset classes, and geographies.

**Asset Class**  
A property type, such as multifamily, industrial, or net-lease.

**Bond Ladder**  
The fixed-income analogy of staggered maturities for periodic liquidity.

**Rung**  
An individual DST position within a ladder.

**Sponsor**  
The firm that organizes and manages a DST and controls sale timing.

**Suitability Review**  
The assessment confirming that a DST or ladder fits the investor.
