# 1031 Exchange and the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

I have been thinking about how often an exchange analysis stops at the capital-gains rate. That is understandable; it is visible and easy to discuss. For a higher-income real-estate investor, though, a property sale can bring another 3.8% layer into the calculation: the net investment income tax, or NIIT.

First-order thinking is, “The capital gain is the tax cost of selling.” Second-order thinking counts the entire recognized-gain stack, asks whether the gain changes MAGI for the year, and separates the tax deferred on sale from the tax that can continue on rental income. A 1031 exchange can defer NIIT along with qualifying gain, but that is a tax-planning result that belongs in a complete, taxpayer-specific analysis—not a reason to ignore price, liquidity, or the quality of the replacement property.

## What the 3.8% NIIT is

The [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), or NIIT, is a 3.8% tax on net investment income for individuals and some trusts and estates whose income exceeds the applicable thresholds. It was enacted under Section 1411 as part of the Affordable Care Act, took effect in 2013, and acts as a surtax: an additional 3.8% on top of regular income tax or [capital gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html). It is also sometimes called the Medicare surtax.

Net investment income can include interest, dividends, capital gains, rental and royalty income, and income from passive business activities, reduced by allocable investment expenses. For real-estate investors, gain from selling investment property is included. Rental income is generally included as well, unless the taxpayer qualifies as a real estate professional and materially participates in the rental activity.

The tax applies to the lesser of net investment income or the amount by which income exceeds the threshold. The result can be meaningful: 3.8% of a $500,000 gain is $19,000. That is why NIIT belongs in the exchange calculation for a higher-income investor who is selling appreciated property.

## Who owes it on property sales

The NIIT generally applies when modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $200,000 for a single filer, $250,000 for a married couple filing jointly, or $125,000 for a married person filing separately. These thresholds are not indexed for inflation. A taxpayer below the threshold does not owe NIIT; a taxpayer above it applies the 3.8% rate to the lesser of net investment income or the excess above the threshold.

An appreciated property sale can change the answer in the same year. The gain is both net investment income and part of MAGI, so it can push a seller over the threshold even if other income was below it. A larger gain can also expose more of the taxpayer's other investment income to NIIT.

The exact exposure depends on total income, the size of the gain, and the taxpayer's facts. A high-income seller with a large gain may face substantial NIIT; a lower-income seller with a modest gain may owe little or none. The CPA should calculate the exposure rather than relying on a general rate.

## How a 1031 defers it

A qualifying [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) defers the NIIT for the same reason it defers the qualifying capital gain: the gain is deferred rather than recognized. With no recognized investment income from the sale, there is no NIIT on that deferred gain. No separate NIIT election is needed.

This is why the exchange benefit goes beyond capital gains. Deferring the gain can defer federal capital-gains tax, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), the 3.8% NIIT, and state income tax. NIIT is not a separate exchange feature; it follows the nonrecognition of the qualifying gain.

There is a second effect. Deferring a large gain keeps it out of MAGI for that year. That can keep the taxpayer below the threshold, or less far above it, and may reduce the NIIT exposure of other investment income. The exchange can therefore defer the direct NIIT on the gain and avoid a threshold spike. It does not eliminate the need to test the exchange's qualification or to analyze the taxpayer's individual facts.

## Income-threshold considerations

The value of the NIIT deferral is income-dependent. An investor already well above the threshold may defer the full 3.8% NIIT on the gain. Someone near or below the threshold may have a smaller NIIT component. The underlying exchange rules do not change; the taxpayer-specific amount does.

The threshold effect can matter as much as the direct gain calculation. A recognized gain can push MAGI high enough to subject the gain and potentially other investment income to NIIT. Deferring the gain can avoid that annual increase, so the tax effect may be larger than simply 3.8% of the gain.

For many exchange investors, appreciated property and meaningful income make NIIT a real part of the deferred-tax calculation. The CPA should quantify it alongside all other taxes rather than treat it as a generic estimate.

## Total tax deferred

NIIT is one of four layers that can apply to an appreciated investment-property sale:

- Federal long-term capital gains tax, up to 20%.
- Depreciation recapture, up to 25% on the portion attributable to prior depreciation.
- The 3.8% NIIT for higher-income taxpayers.
- State income tax, which varies by state.

A 1031 exchange defers these layers by deferring the qualifying gain. For a high-income investor in a tax state with a $500,000 gain, federal capital gains at up to 20% would be $100,000 and the 3.8% NIIT would be $19,000. Recapture and state tax depend on the facts. Across the four layers, the total tax on a large gain can commonly exceed one-third of the gain.

The NIIT is therefore not a rounding error for some investors. It can be a five-figure piece of the deferred tax. Counting only capital gains understates the total cost of a taxable sale and the full amount that a qualifying exchange may defer.

## Key takeaways

- NIIT is a 3.8% surtax on net investment income, including investment-property gain, for taxpayers above the MAGI thresholds.
- A gain can both create investment income and push MAGI over the threshold.
- A qualifying 1031 exchange defers the NIIT on deferred gain and can avoid a MAGI spike.
- NIIT is the fourth layer of tax deferred with capital gains, depreciation recapture, and state tax.

## The NIIT on rental income

The NIIT picture does not end at sale. Rental income is generally net investment income for a higher-income investor and can bear the 3.8% tax after the applicable threshold is met. That applies to rental income from the relinquished property before sale and to income from the replacement property afterward.

The exchange defers NIIT on qualifying gain. It does not, by itself, remove NIIT from future rental income. Ongoing rents and the deferred sale gain are separate considerations in the investor's tax plan.

There is an important qualification for a taxpayer who is a real estate professional and materially participates in the rental activity. That status can potentially take rental income out of the passive net-investment-income category. It requires specific time and participation tests and also interacts with the passive-loss rules, so a CPA should determine whether the taxpayer qualifies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a 1031 exchange defer the net investment income tax?

Yes. A qualifying exchange defers the 3.8% NIIT along with the gain because no gain is recognized from the deferred sale. The NIIT is therefore part of the higher-income investor's deferred-tax calculation.

### What is the 3.8% net investment income tax?

It is the Section 1411 surtax enacted with the Affordable Care Act. It applies at 3.8% to net investment income of individuals and some trusts and estates above the thresholds. Interest, dividends, capital gains, and rental income can be included.

### Who owes the NIIT on a property sale?

Taxpayers whose MAGI exceeds the applicable threshold generally owe 3.8% of the lesser of net investment income or the excess above the threshold. Property gain is investment income and can raise MAGI past the threshold.

### How much NIIT can a property sale trigger?

It can be 3.8% of the relevant gain. A $500,000 gain produces $19,000 at that rate. The actual liability depends on income, the gain, and the lesser-of calculation.

### What are the NIIT income thresholds?

Generally $200,000 of MAGI for single filers, $250,000 for married couples filing jointly, and $125,000 for married people filing separately. The thresholds are not indexed for inflation.

### Can a property gain push me over the NIIT threshold?

Yes. The gain enters MAGI and may cross the threshold even where other income did not. Deferring it through a 1031 can avoid that spike and may reduce NIIT on other investment income.

### Is NIIT part of the total tax a 1031 defers?

Yes. It is one of the four layers: capital gains up to 20%, recapture up to 25%, NIIT at 3.8% for higher earners, and state tax. For a larger gain, the NIIT can be a five-figure amount.

### Does deferring the gain also help my other investment income?

It can. By keeping the deferred gain out of MAGI for the year, the exchange can keep other investment income below, or less far above, the NIIT threshold.

### Does the NIIT apply to my rental income too?

Generally yes for higher-income investors. Rental income is usually net investment income, so the replacement property's rental income can remain subject to NIIT even when the exchange deferred NIIT on the sale gain.

### Can real estate professionals avoid NIIT on rental income?

Potentially. A qualifying real estate professional who materially participates may remove rental income from the passive net-investment-income category. The status requires time and participation tests, and a CPA should determine eligibility.

### Is the NIIT a reason to do a 1031 exchange?

It is part of the analysis, not a standalone answer. For a higher-income investor, the additional 3.8% adds to the tax a taxable sale would trigger and can strengthen the case for exchanging when the replacement itself makes economic sense.

### Who calculates my NIIT exposure?

Your CPA. The result depends on total income, gain size, thresholds, rental-income treatment, and other taxpayer-specific facts.

### Are the NIIT thresholds adjusted for inflation?

No. The $200,000 single and $250,000 joint thresholds have remained fixed since NIIT took effect in 2013, so rising income can bring more taxpayers above them over time.

### Does the NIIT apply to a fully deferred exchange?

No. A fully deferred exchange recognizes no gain, so there is no recognized net investment income from the sale to tax. If boot causes recognized gain, NIIT may apply to that recognized portion.

### Is the NIIT separate from the capital gains tax?

Yes. It is a separate 3.8% surtax that can apply on top of capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, and state tax.

## Glossary

- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% Section 1411 surtax on net investment income above MAGI thresholds.
- **Net Investment Income:** Interest, dividends, capital gains, and rental income that can be subject to NIIT.
- **Section 1411:** The Code section imposing NIIT.
- **Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI):** The income measure compared to NIIT thresholds; a property gain is included in it.
- **NIIT Thresholds:** The $200K single and $250K joint MAGI levels above which NIIT applies.
- **Capital Gain:** Gain on a property sale, a form of net investment income.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation that may be deferred in a qualifying exchange.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, recapture, NIIT, and state tax that a 1031 can defer.
- **Rental Income:** Rental revenue generally treated as NIIT investment income for higher earners.
- **Real Estate Professional:** Status that, with material participation, can potentially exempt rental income from NIIT.
- **Material Participation:** Active involvement that can remove rental income from the passive net-investment-income category.
- **Nonrecognition:** 1031 deferral that leaves no recognized income to trigger NIIT.
- **Threshold Effect:** A gain pushing MAGI over the threshold and exposing more income, avoided by deferral.
- **Surtax:** An additional tax on top of regular income or capital-gains tax.
- **Affordable Care Act:** Legislation that enacted NIIT, effective 2013.
- **Deferral:** Postponing gain and NIIT on that gain through a 1031 exchange.
