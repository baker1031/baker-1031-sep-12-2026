# How Many DSTs Should You Own?

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about how quickly a sensible diversification idea can become a rule of thumb. “Own more DSTs” sounds prudent, but it is incomplete. Putting all exchange capital into one DST concentrates the outcome in one property, tenant base, sponsor, and market. Spreading capital can reduce that concentration, but each additional position brings another offering, another annual tax statement, another set of monitoring, and another future decision.

The first-order answer is that more holdings can mean less single-asset exposure. The second-order answer is that diversification only helps when the positions genuinely differ by sponsor, sector, and geography, and when the investor can manage the administrative load. Exchange size, DST minimums, and 1031 identification rules set practical limits long before a tidy target number does.

DST interests are securities offered to [accredited investors](who-can-invest-in-a-dst-accredited-investor-rules.html); distributions are never guaranteed; and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. The broader framework appears in [the definitive DST guide](dst-guide.html).

## Why Diversify Across DSTs

If an entire [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) goes into one DST, the investor's result depends on one property—or one closely related portfolio—one group of tenants, one sponsor's execution, and one local market. A major tenant departure, sponsor failure, or regional downturn can therefore affect all of the exchanged equity at once.

Multiple DSTs can reduce that concentration. An allocation can be spread across asset classes such as multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, and self-storage; across different sponsors; and across markets and regions. The goal is not to eliminate risk. Real estate carries inherent risk and distributions are never guaranteed. The goal is to make any one disappointment a slice of the allocation rather than the entire allocation.

Diversification works best when it has more than one dimension. Owning several properties from the same sponsor, in the same sector, in one metro can look diversified in a spreadsheet while leaving most of the same risks in place. It is the differences in management, property economics, and location that make the spread meaningful.

## Diversification vs. Complexity

Each DST is a separate investment with its own offering, sponsor, annual reporting, performance updates, distributions, and eventual full-cycle event. Own five DSTs and you will generally receive five annual tax statements, usually grantor letters. You will monitor five sets of communications and later face five separate sale or reinvestment decisions.

The complexity increases at the times when it matters most. At exchange time, several DSTs must be identified and closed inside the 45-day identification and 180-day exchange windows. At tax time, more grantor letters mean more information for a CPA. And DSTs generally reach full cycle in about five to seven years, but not on synchronized schedules. Each sale can create a separate decision about a new 1031 exchange, a 721 UPREIT if available, or taking cash and recognizing tax.

The useful question is not how to maximize the number of positions. It is how to own enough to reduce concentration without turning the investment into paperwork that exceeds its benefit. For many investors, a handful—not dozens—strikes that balance.

## Minimums and Allocation Sizing

Minimum investments constrain how finely exchange equity can be divided. [DST minimums](dst-minimum-investment-and-accreditation-requirements.html) for 1031 investors commonly range from about $25,000 to $100,000 per offering. Cash, non-1031 minimums are often higher. A $300,000 exchange facing a $100,000 minimum can hold at most three DSTs. The same exchange facing a $25,000 minimum could, in principle, support more positions, but each added position also increases complexity.

Sizing is the other side of the decision. Each DST should be large enough to be a meaningful part of the allocation. Scattering small amounts across many positions can create the appearance of diversification while adding tax and monitoring work that barely changes the result. A common approach is to invest well above the bare minimum in each position while keeping the count manageable.

The 1031 rules also constrain the plan. Many investors use the three-property rule, which allows identification of up to three replacement properties of any value. Investors identifying more than three may use the 200% rule, under which the combined value of the identified replacements cannot exceed 200% of the relinquished property's value. Those rules should be planned at the start, not treated as a last-minute paperwork exercise.

## Spreading Sponsors & Sectors

The most useful DST diversification generally works across sponsors, sectors, and geographies. Spreading sponsors means not placing all underwriting, financing, operating, and sale decisions in one manager's hands. Sponsors differ in track record, financial strength, fees, underwriting discipline, financing approach, operating capabilities, and appetite for risk. Multiple capable managers can reduce reliance on any one firm, but diversification never replaces due diligence on each one.

Sectors also respond differently through a cycle. Multifamily and self-storage may behave differently from industrial and logistics, net-lease retail, or healthcare. A net-lease property can have different tenant and lease exposure than an apartment community. A mix does not make any sector risk-free, but it can keep one property type's trouble from defining the whole portfolio.

Geography is the third layer. Metros and regions have different job markets, supply conditions, population changes, and local demand drivers. A strong local economy in one place can coexist with a supply glut or slowdown somewhere else. Spread across sponsors, sectors, and markets is more durable than simply owning several similar DSTs.

### Key Takeaways

- Multiple DSTs can reduce concentration risk, so one property, tenant, sponsor, or market does not dominate exchanged equity.
- Each added DST adds complexity: more grantor letters, more monitoring, and more exchange and full-cycle coordination.
- Minimums of about $25,000–$100,000 and the three-property and 200% identification rules constrain the practical count.
- Real diversification spreads across sponsors, sectors, and geographies. Three to five DSTs is often an illustrative shape for a mid-size exchange, not a recommendation.

## Sample Portfolios

The following are generic illustrations, not recommendations. Every investor, offering list, exchange window, and suitability review is different.

For a smaller exchange of roughly $200,000–$300,000, an investor might hold two or three DSTs in different sectors and with different sponsors. One illustrative mix could include a multifamily DST, an industrial DST, and a net-lease retail DST. That can reduce single-property concentration while keeping the number of tax statements manageable.

For a mid-size exchange of perhaps $500,000–$1,000,000, an illustrative shape may be three to five DSTs across multifamily, industrial, healthcare, self-storage, and net-lease property types, with more than one sponsor and more than one region. A larger exchange might hold a few more positions. Most investors still stop well short of dozens because the marginal reduction in concentration can become small relative to the added administration.

There is no magic number. The right count must respect the identification rules, the actual minimums in available offerings, and the investor's own preference for diversification versus simplicity.

## Matching the Count to Your Goals

A smaller exchange, a preference for simple records, or limited patience for paperwork can make two or three carefully selected DSTs reasonable. A larger exchange and a stronger desire to spread risk may justify more positions, provided the investor is comfortable tracking more grantor letters, offering updates, and full-cycle events.

Income goals and risk tolerance also matter. An investor focused on diversified income may spread across sectors with different distribution profiles, recognizing that every distribution remains a projection. An investor with a particular view on a sector or sponsor may give it more weight, but should still keep enough other exposure to avoid accidental concentration.

The decision should be deliberate. Choose a count that meaningfully reduces concentration, fits the available minimums and 1031 rules, and remains within the administrative burden you are willing to carry. A professional who understands the available offerings can turn “how many?” into a suitable, concrete plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many DSTs should I own?

There is no single right number. It depends on exchange size, available-offering minimums, the desired balance between diversification and simplicity, and the complexity you are willing to manage. The goal is enough positions that no property, tenant, sponsor, or market dominates, but not so many that grantor letters, monitoring, and coordination outweigh the benefit. Illustratively, a smaller exchange may use two or three DSTs, a mid-size exchange three to five across sectors, sponsors, and regions, and a larger exchange somewhat more. Most investors stop well short of dozens. The three-property and 200% rules also matter. This is illustrative, not a recommendation; it should be considered through a suitability review.

### Why would I own more than one DST?

More than one DST can reduce concentration risk. A single-DST exchange puts all equity behind one property, tenant base, sponsor, and local market. Several DSTs can make a problem at one property only part of the result. The spread can cover multifamily, industrial, net-lease, healthcare, and self-storage assets; multiple sponsors; and multiple markets. It does not eliminate risk or guarantee a distribution. Its purpose is risk management, not certainty.

### What's the downside of owning many DSTs?

Each DST has its own offering, sponsor, annual grantor letter, distribution stream, performance updates, and full-cycle timeline. More positions create more material for a CPA and more to monitor. At exchange time, the 45-day and 180-day windows require coordination across more purchases; at full cycle, separate sales can create separate future 1031 decisions. The benefit of each extra holding eventually may be small relative to its administrative cost. That is why many investors prefer a manageable handful rather than dozens.

### What are the minimum investments for a DST?

For 1031 investors, common DST minimums are roughly $25,000–$100,000 per offering, though sponsors and offerings differ. Cash, non-1031 minimums are often higher. The minimum controls how many positions an exchange can practically support: a $300,000 exchange with a $100,000 minimum supports at most three such positions. Confirm the documents, holding period, and suitability implications of each offering. The lowest minimum alone is not a reason to invest.

### How do the 1031 identification rules limit how many DSTs I can hold?

Replacement property generally must be identified in writing within 45 days and acquired within 180 days. The three-property rule permits up to three replacements of any value. The 200% rule allows more than three only if their combined value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property's value. A third option, the 95% rule, is rarely used. Those limits affect a plan to use many DSTs. Work with a qualified intermediary and CPA well before the deadlines; Baker 1031 does not give legal or tax advice.

### What does it mean to spread across sponsors?

Sponsors make important acquisition, financing, operating, and sale decisions. Spreading across sponsors means reducing reliance on one firm's underwriting, debt decisions, operating execution, and financial condition. Different sponsors have different records, strengths, fee structures, and risk approaches. Review each sponsor's track record, prior full-cycle outcomes, finances, and fees. Diversification across managers is useful only when it sits alongside disciplined due diligence.

### What sectors can I diversify a DST portfolio across?

Common DST sectors include multifamily apartments, industrial and logistics assets, net-lease retail, healthcare such as medical office or senior housing, self-storage, and sometimes student housing or other specialized property types. Their demand drivers and cycle behavior differ. Multifamily and self-storage may not respond in the same way as net-lease retail or industrial. The appropriate mix depends on goals, available offerings during the exchange window, and risk tolerance. No sector is risk-free, and no distribution is guaranteed.

### Is three to five DSTs a good number?

Three to five is a common illustrative range for a mid-size 1031 exchange because it can provide exposure across several sectors, sponsors, and geographies without excessive administration. It is not a rule or a recommendation. A smaller exchange may better fit two or three positions. A larger exchange might support more, although the incremental benefit of additional holdings can shrink while tax statements and monitoring accumulate. Use the range as a reference point, not a prescription.

### Do I get a separate tax statement for each DST?

Yes. Each DST is a separate investment and typically issues an annual grantor letter, sometimes called a grantor trust letter or substitute statement, rather than a K-1. It reports the investor's share of income, expenses, and depreciation. Five DSTs generally produce five grantor letters for a CPA to use in preparing the return. The format is generally more straightforward than a partnership K-1, but more DSTs still mean more statements. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; confirm each offering's reporting format with your CPA.

### Can I diversify across geographies with DSTs?

Yes. DSTs own property in specific locations, so spreading holdings across different metros and regions can reduce exposure to one local economy, job market, supply glut, or population trend. Those demand drivers do not all move together. Geographic diversification works alongside sponsor and sector diversification: a genuinely varied allocation has different managers, property types, and markets. It reduces regional concentration but does not eliminate real-estate risk.

### Does owning more DSTs guarantee better returns?

No. Owning more DSTs does not guarantee higher returns, higher income, or even a better outcome. Diversification spreads exposure so one disappointment may have less impact, but all properties can struggle in a broad real-estate downturn and distributions can vary or be suspended. Over-diversification can also dilute a strong position and add complexity without proportionate value. It is a risk-management tool, not a way to manufacture a return. Underlying property and market performance remain uncertain, and past performance does not predict future results.

### How do I size each DST position?

Start with total exchange equity and the available minimums, commonly $25,000–$100,000. Select a count that balances concentration reduction with complexity, then make each position a meaningful slice rather than a token amount above the minimum. The plan must also satisfy equal-or-greater equity and debt replacement across replacement properties and stay within identification limits. An investor may weight a sector or sponsor more heavily while retaining other exposure. The right sizing depends on the specific exchange and goals. Confirm mechanics with a qualified intermediary and CPA.

### What happens when my different DSTs reach full cycle at different times?

DSTs launched at different times and holding different properties generally reach full cycle on separate schedules. When a sponsor sells the property—often around five to seven years, though timing varies and is not guaranteed—the investor can pursue a 1031 exchange into a new replacement such as another DST, take part in a 721 UPREIT into a REIT if it is offered, or take cash and pay the deferred tax. Staggering can be convenient because reinvestment decisions arrive one at a time, but it also requires continuing attention. A new 1031 exchange restarts the applicable clock for that position.

## Glossary

**Diversified DST Portfolio:** Several DSTs spread across sponsors, sectors, and geographies.

**Concentration Risk:** Risk from tying equity to one property, tenant, or sponsor.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A 1031-eligible fractional interest in income-producing real estate.

**Sponsor:** The firm that acquires, structures, and manages a DST.

**Sector:** A property type, such as multifamily, industrial, or net-lease.

**Geographic Diversification:** Spreading DST holdings across different markets and regions.

**DST Minimum:** The smallest 1031 investment per DST, often $25k–$100k.

**Three-Property Rule:** Identify up to three replacement properties of any value.

**200% Rule:** Identify more than three, capped at 200% of relinquished value.

**Identification Period:** The 45-day window to name replacement properties in writing.

**Grantor Letter:** A DST's annual tax statement of income and depreciation.

**Allocation Sizing:** Setting how much equity goes into each DST position.

**Full Cycle:** When a DST sponsor sells the underlying property.

**Net-Lease:** A lease where the tenant covers most property expenses.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether DSTs fit the investor before recommending.
