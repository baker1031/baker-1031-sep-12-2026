# The 721 Exchange One-Way Door: Losing 1031 Eligibility

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep coming back to one question when an owner is considering a 721 exchange: if the move works exactly as planned, will they still want to own direct real estate five or ten years from now?

That question matters because a 721 exchange can offer diversification, liquidity, passive income, and estate-planning potential. It also carries a catch that deserves plain language: it is generally a one-way door. Contribute property to a REIT operating partnership, receive OP units, and the owner has left direct real estate. OP units are a partnership interest rather than like-kind real property, so they generally cannot be used in a 1031 exchange.

This is not a small technical footnote. It makes a 721 exchange a commitment to REIT ownership, not a reversible positioning move. For an investor ready to make that commitment, it can be the intended result. But it needs to be understood before property is contributed. This article explains what the one-way door means, why it exists, the choices that remain afterward, the trade-off involved, and when that trade-off may be acceptable.

## What the one-way door means

The one-way-door metaphor describes the irreversibility of a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html). An investor can move from direct property ownership into REIT ownership, but generally cannot return from OP units to direct real estate on a tax-deferred basis. After property is contributed for [OP units](op-units-explained.html), the route back through a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) is closed.

First-order thinking can make this sound like a simple swap: trade an old building for a more diversified REIT interest and revisit the choice later. Second-order thinking starts with the property type received. The transaction changes not only the asset mix and management burden; it changes the investor’s eligibility for a future 1031 exchange. The 721 is a one-directional transition into REIT ownership, not a tax-free route back out.

That is quite different from a 1031 exchange. With a 1031, the investor continues to hold real property. They may exchange into another property, and another after that, continually repositioning within direct real estate. In that sense, the 1031 can operate like a revolving door: the owner can keep moving among qualifying real-property holdings. The 721 operates as a one-way door because, after the contribution, the owner holds a partnership interest.

The practical point is commitment. A 721 is not normally a temporary holding arrangement or a move to reverse tax-free after the market changes. An investor may still hold, convert, or otherwise manage OP units, but generally cannot use those units to return to direct property without tax. The one-way nature is the defining caveat of the 721 exchange.

## Why OP units cannot be 1031'd

Section 1031 applies to exchanges of real property held for investment or productive use for other like-kind real property. OP units are not real property. They are an interest in the REIT’s operating partnership.

That distinction is decisive. Section 1031 also specifically excludes partnership interests from like-kind exchange treatment. Therefore, OP units fall outside Section 1031 both because they are not like-kind real property and because partnership interests are expressly excluded. There is no structure that turns an exchange of OP units into a 1031 exchange.

Once contributed property becomes OP units, it is no longer a 1031-eligible property interest. An investor who wants to own direct real estate again may dispose of the units in a taxable transaction—for example, by converting to shares and selling them—then use the after-tax proceeds to buy real estate. That is not a tax-free 1031 exchange, and the deferred gain is generally recognized.

The reason behind the one-way door is therefore fundamental, not a quirk. OP units are a partnership interest, not like-kind real property, and Section 1031 specifically excludes partnership interests. The unit itself is simply not property that can be exchanged under Section 1031.

## What remains available after a 721 exchange

The loss of a 1031 path does not mean there are no choices. It means the available choices are within REIT ownership.

An investor can hold OP units, continuing the deferral while receiving distributions. Depending on the structure, units can often be converted one-for-one into REIT shares after a holding period. An investor may be able to redeem units for cash in some structures. They can also hold the units until death, when a step-up in basis can generally eliminate the deferred gain for heirs.

Those choices carry different tax consequences. Conversion to REIT shares triggers the deferred gain, even if the conversion is one-for-one and occurs after the applicable holding period. A cash redemption also triggers the gain. Holding units retains the deferral and can provide distributions. Holding until death may allow the step-up in basis to erase the gain for heirs.

What the investor cannot do tax-free is exchange OP units into direct real estate under Section 1031. Nor can the investor create tax-free liquidity merely by converting units to shares; conversion triggers the deferred gain. Post-721 flexibility is meaningful, but it exists inside the REIT-ownership framework rather than as a tax-free exit back to direct property.

## The trade-off: 1031 flexibility for 721 benefits

The one-way door represents a real exchange of advantages. The owner gives up the 1031’s continued flexibility: the ability to exchange real property repeatedly, reposition among properties, remain a direct real-estate owner, defer gain while doing so, and adapt holdings as objectives change.

In return, the owner may receive the features associated with a 721 exchange: diversification through the REIT portfolio, liquidity through convertible units, passive ownership without direct management, and estate-planning potential through step-up and divisibility.

The surface-level view is that the 721 benefits are simply additive. The second-order reality is that they are received in exchange for an option that may be valuable later. The value of ongoing 1031 flexibility depends on the owner’s goals, appetite for direct ownership, desire to acquire particular properties, and need to retain control. It is a real cost for an active investor who wants to keep repositioning.

For an owner who values diversification, potential liquidity, passive ownership, and estate planning more than continued direct-property exchanges, the trade-off may be acceptable. That investor may see REIT ownership as the desired endpoint. For an owner who expects to continue selecting properties, managing them, and using 1031 exchanges, giving up that option may be too costly. The one-way door is not inherently good or bad. Its value depends on which set of advantages the investor wants to retain.

## When the one-way move can be acceptable

The clearest case is an owner who is transitioning out of active real estate for good. They may be retiring from property management, seeking a broader portfolio, and no longer intending to return to direct ownership. If they do not want to keep exchanging real property, losing that flexibility may be irrelevant. In that setting, the one-way door can be acceptable or even the intended result.

It can also fit an investor whose priorities are estate planning, divisibility, diversification through a REIT portfolio, liquidity through convertible units, and passive ownership. If those priorities outweigh the ability to keep trading direct real estate, the owner is choosing the 721 benefits deliberately. REIT ownership is the endpoint rather than a stop along the way.

The move is less suitable for investors who want to keep options open. Someone who may want to reposition into direct real estate, acquire specific properties, retain control, or preserve continued exchange flexibility should recognize that the loss of 1031 eligibility can be a dealbreaker. A 721 suits an owner ready to commit to REIT ownership, not one who wants ongoing direct-real-estate optionality.

## Key Takeaways

- A 721 exchange is generally a one-way door: after an investor holds OP units, they lose 1031 eligibility and cannot return tax-free to direct real estate.
- OP units cannot be used in a 1031 exchange because they are a partnership interest rather than like-kind real property, and Section 1031 specifically excludes partnership interests.
- After a 721, an investor can hold units, convert to shares in a taxable transaction, redeem units for cash in some structures in a taxable transaction, or hold until death for a possible step-up in basis. They cannot tax-free reverse into direct property.
- The trade-off is continued 1031 flexibility versus the 721’s potential diversification, liquidity, passive ownership, and estate-planning benefits. It may work for an owner committing to REIT ownership, but not for one seeking continued flexibility.

## Planning around the one-way nature

Planning must happen before the contribution. First, be certain that REIT ownership is the intended endpoint. Because the tax-free move is generally irreversible, a 721 should not be treated as a tentative or temporary position. The analysis should compare goals such as diversification, liquidity, passive ownership, and estate planning with the flexibility that will be lost.

Second, an owner does not necessarily need to contribute every asset. A partial transition can place some properties into a 721 exchange while keeping others in direct real estate. The contributed properties become a commitment to REIT ownership, while the retained properties preserve their own 1031 flexibility. This approach can provide some of the 721 benefits while keeping some direct-real-estate options.

Third, plan around the choices that remain after the exchange. A liquidity plan can address a gradual conversion of units. An income plan can address holding units for distributions. An estate plan can consider holding for the step-up in basis. Since the owner cannot tax-free return to direct real estate, the point is to use the REIT-ownership choices deliberately.

Thoughtful preparation—certainty about the endpoint, a possible partial transition, and a clear plan for holding, conversion, and estate strategy—helps ensure the one-way door leads where the investor wants to go. It turns a generally irreversible move into a considered decision rather than an unintended loss of flexibility.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is the 721 exchange called a “one-way door”?

It is generally a one-directional, irreversible transition. An investor can go from direct property to REIT ownership, but cannot come back tax-free into direct real estate. After contributing property for OP units, the investor loses 1031 eligibility and cannot tax-free exchange those units for real property. Unlike a 1031 exchange, which can keep an owner moving among qualifying real properties, a 721 is a commitment to REIT ownership rather than a reversible move.

### Why can't I do a 1031 exchange with OP units?

OP units are a partnership interest, not like-kind real property. Section 1031 requires real property and specifically excludes partnership interests from like-kind treatment. Once property becomes OP units, it is no longer 1031-eligible. To return to direct real estate, the investor would need a taxable disposition of the units—for example, conversion to shares followed by a sale that triggers the gain—not a tax-free 1031 exchange.

### What can I do after a 721 exchange?

You may hold OP units, retain the deferral, and receive distributions; convert units to REIT shares, often one-for-one after a holding period, while triggering deferred gain; redeem units for cash in some structures, also triggering gain; or hold units until death, when a step-up in basis can generally erase the gain for heirs. These are meaningful choices inside REIT ownership. What you cannot do is use a 1031 exchange to return tax-free to direct real estate.

### Can I ever get back into direct real estate after a 721?

Yes, but generally not tax-free. You could convert OP units to REIT shares and sell them, or redeem units for cash, recognizing the deferred gain. You could then buy direct real estate with the after-tax proceeds. There is no 1031 route back because OP units are not 1031-eligible. The need to trigger the deferred tax is why the 721 is described as a one-way door.

### What is the trade-off of the one-way door?

You surrender the 1031’s flexibility to keep exchanging real property, reposition among properties, and remain a direct real-estate owner. In exchange, you may obtain diversification, potential liquidity, passive ownership, and estate-planning benefits associated with a 721 exchange. The result is continued direct-property flexibility versus the benefits of committed REIT ownership. Whether it is worthwhile depends on what you value.

### When is the one-way move acceptable?

It can be acceptable when REIT ownership is your intended endpoint: for example, when you are retiring from active real estate, value diversification, liquidity, passive ownership, and estate planning, and do not plan to return to direct real estate. In that situation, the loss of continued 1031 flexibility may not matter. It is less acceptable for investors who want to keep repositioning direct properties and retain control.

### Do I have to put all my property into a 721 exchange?

No. A partial transition can contribute some assets to a 721 exchange while leaving others in direct real estate. The contributed assets become committed to REIT ownership, but the retained assets preserve their 1031 flexibility. That can provide some diversification and passive ownership while retaining some direct-real-estate flexibility.

### Is the one-way door a reason not to do a 721 exchange?

Not automatically. It is a reason to be certain that the exchange fits your objectives before you commit. For an investor who wants to transition to REIT ownership and does not need continued 1031 flexibility, the one-way feature may be acceptable or intended. For an investor who wants to keep exchanging direct real property, it may be a dealbreaker. It is a reason for careful consideration, not automatic avoidance.

### Can I convert OP units to shares and then 1031?

No. Conversion gives you REIT shares, which are securities rather than real property, and conversion triggers the deferred gain. REIT shares are not 1031-eligible either. Selling the shares after paying the tax and buying direct real estate is possible, but it is not a 1031 exchange. Conversion does not create a path from OP units back to a qualifying 1031 exchange.

### How should I plan around the one-way nature?

Treat REIT ownership as the intended endpoint before committing, because the tax-free move is generally irreversible. Consider staging the transition or retaining some direct real estate to keep some 1031 flexibility. Then plan to use the remaining REIT-ownership choices effectively: holding for distributions and deferral, converting gradually if liquidity is needed, and considering a hold-until-death strategy for a possible step-up in basis.

### Does the step-up at death still apply despite the one-way nature?

Yes. The inability to 1031 out does not remove the potential step-up in basis. If OP units are held until death, heirs generally receive a stepped-up basis that can erase the deferred gain. A common 721 approach is to commit to REIT ownership, hold units for life for deferral and income, and pass them to heirs with the step-up. The one-way feature and the step-up can coexist.

### Is a 1031 better than a 721 because it is not one-way?

Not necessarily. A 1031’s continued flexibility is valuable to investors who want to keep exchanging direct real estate. A 721’s diversification, potential liquidity, passive ownership, and estate-planning features may be valuable to investors ready to commit to REIT ownership. Neither is universally better; they are designed for different goals. The relevant question is whether you value continued direct-property flexibility or the potential benefits of a 721 exchange more.

## Glossary

**One-Way Door:** The 721 exchange’s generally irreversible transition into REIT ownership.

**1031 Eligibility:** The ability to do a 1031 exchange, which is lost once an investor holds OP units.

**Partnership Interest:** What OP units are; partnership interests are excluded from 1031 like-kind treatment.

**Like-Kind Real Property:** The type of property a 1031 exchange requires; OP units do not qualify.

**Irreversible:** The generally permanent, tax-free nature of the 721 move into REIT ownership.

**Revolving Door (1031):** The ability to continue exchanging qualifying real property under Section 1031.

**Conversion:** The taxable exchange of OP units for shares; it is not a 1031 exchange.

**REIT Shares:** Securities rather than real property; they are also not 1031-eligible.

**Endpoint:** REIT ownership as the generally permanent destination of a 721 exchange.

**Partial Transition:** Contributing some assets through a 721 exchange while retaining others in direct real estate.

**Flexibility Trade-Off:** Giving up continued 1031 flexibility in exchange for the potential benefits of a 721 exchange.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset at death that can remain available despite the one-way nature of a 721 exchange.

**Direct Real Estate:** Property ownership an investor generally cannot return to tax-free after a 721 exchange.

**Commitment:** The deliberate, generally permanent choice to hold REIT ownership through OP units.

**Suitability Review:** The assessment of whether the one-way commitment and related securities fit an investor’s situation.

**Built-In Gain:** Deferred gain that is generally triggered when OP units are exited in a taxable transaction.
