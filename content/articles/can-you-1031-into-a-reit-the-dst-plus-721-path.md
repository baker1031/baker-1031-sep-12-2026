# Can You 1031 Into a REIT? The DST + 721 Path

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I often see the same practical problem: an owner is ready to stop being a landlord, but the embedded gain in the property makes a straightforward sale feel prohibitively expensive. The instinct is understandable—sell through a 1031 exchange, buy a REIT, and let someone else manage the real estate.

The direct answer is no. Section 1031 requires like-kind real property, while REIT shares are securities that the tax code excludes. But the direct answer is not the end of the analysis. A well-established two-step path can ultimately put an investor into a REIT structure while preserving deferral: first complete a 1031 exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), then, if the DST’s property is later acquired by the sponsor’s REIT operating partnership, receive operating-partnership units in a tax-deferred 721, or UPREIT, exchange.

The sequence is property → DST → OP units. It can replace active ownership with passive, professionally managed real estate, and then with an interest tied to a REIT portfolio. It is also time-sensitive, document-intensive, dependent on the sponsor’s decision and timing, and generally one-way once OP units are received. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice; an investor should have a tax advisor confirm the rules and their particular facts.

For the broader context on private and non-traded REITs and their relationship to this route, see the [complete 2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs and the 721 exchange](reits-guide.html).

## Why a direct 1031 into a REIT does not work

Section 1031 permits deferral when an investor exchanges like-kind real property held for investment or business use for other like-kind real property. The replacement asset has to be real estate. It cannot be a security or another excluded asset type.

The code expressly excludes interests in entities, including stocks, bonds, partnership interests, and shares of a corporation or of a trust treated as a corporation. A REIT share is exactly that kind of interest. Buying a REIT share does not give the investor direct ownership of the underlying buildings. It gives the investor an ownership interest in a company or trust that owns real estate.

First-order thinking notices that both a building and a REIT involve real estate. Second-order thinking asks what the investor legally owns. With a REIT share, the answer is a security, not a direct interest in real property. Therefore, selling a building and purchasing REIT shares is a taxable purchase of a security, not a deferred like-kind exchange. See [why REIT shares do not qualify for a 1031 exchange](reits-and-1031-exchanges-why-reit-shares-dont-qualify.html).

The legal character of the interest controls. That hard rule is why an indirect, compliant route is necessary. A direct 1031 into a REIT share is not available even though the REIT itself owns real estate.

## Step 1: use a 1031 exchange to enter a DST

The first step is a 1031 exchange into a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html). A DST holds one or more specific real estate properties, and investors hold fractional beneficial interests in the trust.

Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a DST beneficial interest is treated as a direct, undivided interest in the underlying real property for this purpose. It can therefore qualify as like-kind replacement property. An investor can sell investment real estate and reinvest the proceeds in a DST while deferring capital-gains tax, just as they could by purchasing another qualifying building.

The DST is the linchpin in this path. It is a passive, fractional real-estate vehicle that the tax rules treat as like-kind real property, where a REIT share is a security. The sponsor manages the DST property, and the investor receives their share of rental income without managing tenants, maintenance, or financing directly.

The ordinary 1031 timing rules remain in force: identify replacement property within 45 days and close within 180 days. A DST can be convenient to identify because it is a defined, available investment. At this stage, the investor has deferred the original gain and moved into passive real estate, but has not yet bought a REIT share or received an OP unit.

## Step 2: the 721 or UPREIT roll-up into a REIT

The second step is the potential [721 exchange, also called an UPREIT transaction](from-dst-to-reit-721-rollup.html). After a period of holding the DST interest, the sponsor—often affiliated with a REIT—may arrange for the REIT operating partnership to acquire the DST’s property.

Section 721 generally provides nonrecognition when property is contributed to a partnership for a partnership interest. In this arrangement, the operating partnership acquires the DST property and issues operating-partnership, or [OP, units](op-units-explained.html). The DST investor’s interest converts into OP units without recognizing the previously deferred gain at that step.

An UPREIT is an umbrella-partnership REIT structure. The operating partnership is the entity through which the REIT holds its real estate. OP units are partnership interests in that operating partnership, representing an economic interest tied to the REIT’s portfolio. They generally provide distributions comparable to REIT dividends.

The change is material. The investor moves from a single-property DST interest into a diversified REIT structure. OP units can usually be converted into REIT shares later, often on a one-for-one or formula basis and at the investor’s election. That later conversion is generally taxable. The 721 step itself is the bridge that gets the investor from the 1031-eligible DST into the REIT’s structure without triggering gain.

## How the tax deferral carries through the two steps

The tax treatment has to be followed through the whole chain. In the original 1031 exchange, the property-sale gain is deferred under Section 1031 and the investor’s basis carries into the DST interest. When the DST property is later contributed to the REIT operating partnership in the 721 transaction, Section 721 generally treats that contribution as non-taxable. The deferred gain and carryover basis continue into the OP units.

No deferred tax is generally triggered along the path from property to DST to OP units. The taxable point comes later. Converting OP units into REIT shares is generally a taxable event, because the investor is moving from a partnership interest into a security. A sale after conversion—or another taxable disposition of the units—also realizes the gain.

This distinction deserves attention. The surface-level appeal is that a 721 “gets an investor into a REIT tax-free.” The second-order reality is that it preserves deferral while the investor holds OP units; it does not permanently erase tax merely because the investor entered a REIT structure. The investor must decide if and when to convert, considering liquidity needs and the tax consequence.

Many investors hold OP units for their distributions and portfolio exposure rather than convert immediately. If the units are held until death, heirs may receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) that can eliminate the deferred gain. The deferral therefore runs through property → DST → OP units and pauses there, with conversion or a taxable disposition as the generally taxable event and a potential step-up available if units are held until death.

> The deferral can run from a property to DST interests and then OP units. Converting the units into REIT shares and selling is the point at which the deferred tax generally becomes due, so the timing of conversion is its own capital-allocation decision.

## Is the DST + 721 path a fit for your objectives?

The potential benefits are straightforward. The path can allow an investor to leave active property management, defer a large capital-gains tax, and ultimately hold an economic interest in a diversified, professionally managed REIT portfolio. OP units offer potential liquidity because they can be converted to REIT shares that can be sold, subject to the tax consequence of conversion. Holding until death may create estate-planning potential through a step-up in basis.

For an owner tired of tenants, repairs, and property-level decision-making, those features can be compelling. The move changes a single-property concentration into a broader portfolio and replaces direct management with sponsor and REIT management.

But the trade-offs are substantial. Once DST interests become OP units, they are partnership interests rather than 1031-eligible real property. The investor generally cannot 1031 out of OP units and back into another property. Future flexibility narrows to holding the units, converting them to shares in a taxable transaction, or holding until death for a possible step-up.

The investor also gives up control over particular properties. They hold an interest in a portfolio rather than a building they selected, and depend on the sponsor and the REIT’s management. Not every DST is structured for a future 721 roll-up. Even a DST that contemplates one cannot guarantee when the sponsor’s REIT will acquire the property. The eventual share conversion is taxable, and normal REIT risks—including market, interest-rate, management, and distribution risk—remain. Distributions are not guaranteed.

The right comparison is not “tax-free versus taxable.” It is passivity, deferral, diversification, potential liquidity, and possible step-up on one side; one-way OP units, a future taxable conversion, lost property-level control, and sponsor-timing dependence on the other. It can fit an investor who values long-term REIT ownership more than ongoing 1031 flexibility. It may not fit an investor who wants to keep selecting direct properties or avoid the prospect of a taxable conversion.

## How the process works in practice

The practical sequence usually unfolds over years.

1. The investor sells investment property and engages a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) to hold the proceeds.
2. The investor identifies a replacement DST within 45 days and closes into it within 180 days, satisfying the applicable 1031 timelines.
3. The investor chooses a DST that offers, or is sponsored by a REIT that contemplates, a future 721/UPREIT roll-up. The offering documents and sponsor disclosures should address whether and how a roll-up is anticipated. A DST should not be assumed to lead to a REIT merely because it is a DST.
4. The DST holds the property for a period. When and if the sponsor’s REIT acquires it, the 721 roll-up converts the investor’s DST interest into OP units. This timing is set by the sponsor and is not fully controlled by the investor.
5. The investor holds OP units, receives distributions, and separately decides whether and when to convert to REIT shares. Conversion is generally taxable and should be coordinated with tax planning.

The first stage runs on fixed 1031 deadlines. The later 721 stage runs on sponsor timing and may take a few years. The path can therefore take several years from the initial sale to OP-unit ownership, and longer if the investor holds units before conversion. It is well established, but it is not automatic, and it requires careful review of the documents at every stage.

An investor should work closely with a CPA and attorney. The original 1031, the 721 mechanics, basis and deferral tracking, and any later conversion all have technical and time-sensitive consequences. The investor should verify the current rules and their circumstances before acting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you 1031 directly into a REIT?

No. REIT shares are securities, not like-kind real property. Section 1031 requires an exchange of investment or business real property for other like-kind real property, and excludes interests in entities such as stocks, bonds, partnership interests, and corporate or trust shares. A REIT shareholder owns an interest in an entity that owns real estate, not direct ownership of the real estate. Selling property and buying REIT shares is therefore taxable, not a deferred exchange. The compliant indirect route is to 1031 into a DST and later have the DST property rolled into a REIT operating partnership in a 721 exchange for OP units.

### What is the DST + 721 path to a REIT?

It is a two-step route designed to preserve deferral while reaching a REIT structure. First, the investor completes a 1031 exchange into a DST. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats the DST interest as a direct real-property interest for 1031 purposes. Second, after a holding period, the sponsor—often affiliated with a REIT—may have the REIT operating partnership acquire the DST property through a 721/UPREIT transaction. Section 721 generally makes the exchange for OP units non-taxable. The investor receives OP units tied to the REIT portfolio, then may later convert them to REIT shares in a generally taxable transaction.

### Why is a REIT share not like-kind property?

The issue is legal character. A REIT share is an ownership interest in a corporation or trust treated as a corporation, so it is a security. It does not provide direct, undivided ownership of the REIT’s underlying real estate. Section 1031 excludes entity interests. A DST interest, by contrast, is treated under Revenue Ruling 2004-86 as a direct, undivided interest in underlying real property. Both structures can involve real estate, but a security and a direct real-property interest are treated differently. Confirm the specific treatment with a tax advisor.

### What is a 721 exchange?

A 721 exchange, often called an UPREIT transaction in this setting, is a generally tax-free contribution of property to a partnership for partnership interests under Section 721. A REIT commonly operates through an umbrella operating partnership. When the operating partnership acquires property—or, in this path, the DST property—it issues OP units. The units represent an economic interest in the REIT’s portfolio and generally provide distributions comparable to REIT dividends. They can usually be converted into REIT shares over time, but that conversion is generally taxable. The 721 step preserves the deferral established in the original 1031.

### Does the 721 roll-up trigger deferred tax?

Generally, no. Under Section 721, the contribution of the DST property to the REIT operating partnership for OP units is generally non-taxable. Deferred gain and carryover basis continue into the OP units. The later taxable moment is generally conversion to REIT shares, a sale, or another taxable disposition of the units. An investor may choose to hold OP units for distributions and diversification; holding until death can allow a potential step-up in basis for heirs. A tax advisor should confirm timing and treatment.

### When does tax deferral end in the DST + 721 path?

The deferral starts with the 1031 exchange into the DST and continues when the DST property moves into the operating partnership under Section 721. No deferred tax is generally recognized through property → DST → OP units. Deferral generally ends when OP units are converted into REIT shares or redeemed for cash — the conversion itself is normally the taxable event, not a later sale of the shares. Conversion from a partnership interest to a security generally triggers deferred gain. Investors can hold units for income and diversification, and holding until death can provide heirs a possible step-up in basis to fair market value that may eliminate the deferred capital-gains tax. Plan that timing with an advisor.

### What are operating-partnership, or OP, units?

OP units are partnership interests received in a 721/UPREIT exchange. The REIT’s umbrella operating partnership holds its real estate, and the units represent an economic interest in that partnership and, indirectly, the REIT’s diversified portfolio. They generally provide distributions comparable to REIT dividends. The Section 721 contribution that produces them is generally tax-deferred, so deferred gain carries into the units. Units can usually be converted to REIT shares, often one-for-one or on a formula basis and at the investor’s election, but that conversion is generally taxable. They are the tax-deferred bridge between contributed property or a DST interest and REIT-share ownership.

### Can I 1031 out of OP units later?

Generally no. OP units are partnership interests, not like-kind real property, and partnership interests are excluded from Section 1031 treatment. An investor may still 1031 between DSTs or other qualifying real property before the 721 event, but once a 721 roll-up creates OP units, the future choices generally become hold for distributions and continued deferral, convert to shares and sell in a taxable transaction, or hold until death for a possible step-up. This one-way limitation is a central trade-off and is why the path is generally for investors ready to commit to REIT ownership over the long term. Confirm the specifics with a tax advisor.

### What are the benefits of the DST + 721 path?

It can provide an exit from active management, including tenant, maintenance, and financing responsibilities; deferral of capital-gains tax through two generally non-taxable steps; an economic interest in a diversified REIT portfolio rather than a single-property DST; and potential liquidity because OP units can be converted to shares that can be sold, subject to tax at conversion. An investor may also see estate-planning value if units are held until death and heirs receive a step-up in basis. Those benefits can be compelling for an owner facing a large tax bill but wanting a passive position. They still must be weighed against the trade-offs.

### What are the drawbacks of the DST + 721 path?

The path is generally one-way after the investor receives OP units: units are no longer 1031-eligible real property. Conversion of units to REIT shares and a later sale is taxable and ends deferral. The investor gives up control over individual properties, relies on sponsor and REIT management, and cannot fully control the timing of a roll-up. Not every DST contemplates a 721 path, so a suitable structure must actually be available. Market, interest-rate, management, and distribution risks apply to REIT exposure, and distributions are not guaranteed. The drawbacks center on lost flexibility, taxable conversion, lost property-level control, and sponsor dependence.

### Is the DST + 721 path right for me?

It can fit an investor who wants to exit active management, defer significant capital gain, and ultimately own a diversified, professionally managed REIT interest with potential liquidity and possible estate-planning value. An owner tired of landlording and comfortable with a long-term REIT structure may find it compelling. It is less likely to fit an investor who wants specific-property control, wants to preserve ongoing 1031 flexibility, or does not accept an eventual taxable conversion. A DST that contemplates the roll-up must be available, and its timing rests with the sponsor. The decision should be made with a CPA, attorney, and broker-dealer after a suitability review.

### Does every DST offer a 721 path to a REIT?

No. Some DSTs are standalone investments. A sponsor may hold the property for a period and then sell it, returning proceeds to investors, who may take cash and pay the deferred tax or complete another 1031 exchange. Other DSTs are sponsored by firms affiliated with a REIT and are structured to contemplate a future 721/UPREIT roll-up, in which the REIT acquires the property and investors receive OP units. If the REIT path matters to an investor, the particular DST must be selected deliberately. Offering documents and sponsor disclosures should explain whether and how a 721 is anticipated. Even then, timing is not guaranteed and depends on the sponsor’s decision. A broker-dealer can help identify DSTs structured for this path.

### How long does the DST + 721 path take?

It is generally a multi-year process. The first 1031 step has fixed rules: 45 days from the sale to identify replacement property and 180 days to close into the DST. The investor then holds the DST for a period that varies by offering. A 721 roll-up occurs if and when the sponsor’s REIT decides to acquire the DST property, which can take a few years and is not fully controlled by the investor. After receiving OP units, the investor may hold them for years or convert when appropriate, subject to the tax consequence. Review the anticipated timeline in the offering documents and plan with a CPA.

### Will my heirs owe deferred tax in the DST + 721 path?

Not necessarily. While the investor holds the DST interest and then OP units without a taxable disposition, capital-gains tax remains deferred. If OP units are held until death, heirs may receive a step-up in basis to fair market value, which can eliminate the deferred capital-gains tax. This extends the familiar “defer until death, then step up” feature of 1031 planning through the 721 structure. Estate tax, how the interest is held, and the rules in effect at that time all matter; step-up rules can change. An investor should plan with tax and estate professionals because the details are consequential.

## Glossary

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred exchange of like-kind investment real estate.

**Like-Kind Real Property:** Real estate that qualifies for a 1031 exchange; a REIT share does not qualify.

**REIT Share:** A security representing ownership of a REIT rather than direct real property.

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding fractional, 1031-eligible real-estate interests.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling that treats a qualifying DST interest as real property for 1031 purposes.

**721 Exchange:** A generally tax-free contribution of property to a partnership for partnership units.

**UPREIT:** An umbrella-partnership REIT structure that can use a 721 exchange.

**Operating Partnership:** The partnership through which an UPREIT holds its real estate.

**OP Units:** Partnership units received in a 721 exchange that can generally be converted into shares.

**Tax Deferral:** Postponement of the capital-gains tax on a property-sale gain.

**Carryover Basis:** The original basis that continues into the replacement interest.

**Conversion to Shares:** The generally taxable exchange of OP units for REIT shares.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The potential basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain.

**Qualified Intermediary:** The party that holds 1031 proceeds during the exchange.

**Sponsor:** The firm organizing the DST and any contemplated REIT roll-up.

**Diversification:** The breadth of a REIT portfolio compared with a single-property DST.
