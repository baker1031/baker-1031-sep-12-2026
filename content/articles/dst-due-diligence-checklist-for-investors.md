# DST Due Diligence Checklist for Investors

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing the projected distribution receive more attention than the documents explaining what can interrupt it. A due-diligence process reverses that order: understand the risks and the structure first, then decide what a projected payout is worth.

A Delaware Statutory Trust can help complete a 1031 exchange into passive, professionally managed real estate. It is not automatically a good investment, and sponsors differ materially in track record, fees, and transparency. DST interests are illiquid securities held for years. The time to examine the sponsor, property and market, debt, PPM risks, suitability, and fees is before an investment—not after. Interests are offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice, and distributions are projections rather than guarantees.

Use this checklist alongside [our in-depth DST pillar guide](dst-guide.html) for fuller context behind each item.

## Vet the sponsor

The sponsor is [the company that acquires the property, structures the trust, and manages the asset](how-to-choose-a-dst-sponsor-company.html) through sale. In a passive investment, its decisions shape nearly every part of the beneficial owner’s experience. Start with the record: how many offerings has it brought to market, how many years and market cycles has it operated, and what assets under management does it oversee? A sponsor that has operated through a downturn can be more informative than one with only a short, favorable-market history.

Look beyond launch records to full-cycle history: DSTs taken from acquisition through sale. That is the record of how investors actually fared. Ask how completed offerings performed relative to original projections, whether distributions were maintained, and how sale prices compared with acquisition costs. Review financial strength, regulatory or litigation history, co-investment that aligns interests, and the fee structure. A well-capitalized sponsor may be better able to support a property through a soft patch, fund reserves, and avoid forced decisions.

The sponsor controls the property and trust. That makes track record, full-cycle results, assets under management, balance-sheet strength, and aligned incentives foundational. [examining track record, full-cycle history, assets under management, and financial strength](dst-sponsor-due-diligence-what-to-investigate.html) is part of the review. A [long, transparent, full-cycle record signals discipline](how-dst-sponsors-are-vetted-and-ranked.html); a thin record limited to favorable markets is a caution flag. Even a good property can disappoint under a weak sponsor.

## Analyze the property and market

After the sponsor, examine the asset and the market around it. Is the location growing or stable, with healthy employment, population trends, and demand for the property type? Or does it face structural decline? Net-lease retail, multifamily, industrial, medical office, and self-storage have different demand drivers and risk profiles. The chosen exposure should fit both the investor’s view and the broader portfolio.

Tenants and leases create the income that supports distributions. For a single-tenant net-lease property, examine tenant credit, remaining lease term, and what may happen at expiration. For multi-tenant assets, read the rent roll, occupancy, lease rollover schedule, and tenant concentration. Compare current occupancy with the market, evaluate rent trends, and identify deferred maintenance or capital needs. The goal is not simply to see current income, but to assess whether it can endure across the multi-year hold.

First-order thinking sees a projected yield. Second-order thinking reads the rent roll, lease terms, tenant concentration, capital needs, and the market on which those tenants depend. Strong location, durable tenancy, and healthy demand support projections; weak fundamentals undermine them.

> Distributions are only as durable as the rent behind them. Read the rent roll, lease terms, and tenant market before giving the projected yield much weight.

## Review the debt structure

Most DSTs use leverage, making the debt structure a separate checklist item. The loan is at the trust level and is non-recourse to investors: beneficial owners do not personally qualify for or guarantee it. The debt still affects both return and risk.

Start with loan-to-value, or LTV. Conservative LTV generally has less risk than high leverage, which amplifies upside and downside and leaves a smaller cushion if income softens. An all-cash, debt-free DST removes financing risk, but offers no debt replacement to an exchanger who needs it.

Review loan term and maturity against the expected hold. Debt coming due in a difficult market can force a refinancing or sale at a poor time. A fixed rate locks the borrowing cost for the term; a floating rate exposes the trust to rising interest costs that can squeeze distributions. Understand interest-only periods, amortization, refinancing assumptions, rate sensitivity, and the property-performance assumptions behind them. Conservative leverage, fixed-rate debt, and a maturity aligned with the planned hold reduce financing risk; high LTV, floating rates, and near-term maturities can raise it.

## Read the PPM risk factors

The [Private Placement Memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html), or PPM, is the central DST offering document. Its risk-factors section should not be treated as boilerplate. It describes what can go wrong: distributions are projections rather than guarantees; there is no public secondary market; the interest is illiquid; investors do not control management decisions; the property may decline; tenants may default; debt may be hard to refinance; and principal can be lost.

The PPM also describes sponsor compensation, fees, conflicts of interest, governing documents, and return assumptions. It addresses the “seven deadly sins,” the IRS restrictions that limit what a DST trustee may do. After the offering closes, the trustee generally cannot accept new capital, renegotiate existing debt or borrow new funds, reinvest sale proceeds, make more than limited capital expenditures beyond normal repairs and items required by law or existing leases, reserve cash beyond reasonable needs, or enter new leases or renegotiate existing leases except within restrictions. These limits support a passive, 1031-eligible structure, but also prevent the trust from managing its way out of trouble as a direct owner might.

Give deal-specific risks the closest attention: single-tenant concentration, a near-term lease expiration, floating-rate debt, an aggressive business plan, or a market-specific exposure. Ask questions where the PPM is unclear. The relevant judgment is whether the projected return compensates the investor for the risks disclosed.

## Key Takeaways

- Vet the sponsor first: review track record, full-cycle history, assets under management, financial strength, and alignment.
- Analyze property and market fundamentals: location, property type, tenant credit, lease terms, occupancy, and demand determine the durability of projected income.
- Review debt: LTV, term and maturity, fixed versus floating rate, interest-only and amortization terms, and refinancing risk shape outcomes.
- Read PPM risks and confirm suitability and fees. Distributions are projections, DSTs are illiquid, and the load plus ongoing fees reduce net return.

## Confirm suitability and fees

The final review turns back to the investor. Is this DST suitable, and is its cost understood? DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to [accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html)s. Accreditation generally requires income over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly, or net worth above $1 million excluding the home.

The illiquid, multi-year hold must fit liquidity needs and risk tolerance. A broker-dealer suitability review considers financial situation, goals, time horizon, and the place of the DST among other holdings. It also considers whether an entire exchange is being put in one trust or diversified across several. A sound offering that does not fit the investor’s liquidity needs or concentration limits is not the right offering.

Fees reduce net return. Upfront load can include selling commissions, the dealer-manager fee, and organizational and offering costs, so not all invested capital goes into real estate. Ongoing asset-management and property-management fees apply during the hold, and a sponsor may receive a disposition fee at sale. The PPM generally summarizes the upfront amount as total load. A higher-fee DST is not automatically worse when the sponsor and asset are strong, but fees should be identified precisely and compared like for like against the projected return and offering quality.

> Due diligence asks not only whether a DST is good, but whether it is good for this investor at a cost they understand, given liquidity needs and risk tolerance.

## Putting the checklist to work

The value of the checklist is consistent use across offerings. Score each DST on sponsor, property and market, debt, PPM risk factors, and suitability and fees. Require satisfactory answers in each category before proceeding. One weakness may not be disqualifying, but it should be identified and weighed rather than passed over.

This discipline helps avoid common errors: chasing the highest projected distribution without checking income durability, ignoring leverage and maturity risk, treating the PPM as boilerplate, concentrating an entire exchange in one trust, and underestimating fees. Diversifying by sponsor, property type, and geography can reduce the effect of one weak assumption, tenant, or market. It also requires more diligence, more sponsors to vet, and potentially more fees.

Diversification cannot remove every risk. Real estate, tenants, financing, and market conditions can cause an offering that appears sound to underperform. Due diligence reduces risk; it does not eliminate it. The purpose is a repeatable comparison based on substance rather than marketing pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is DST due diligence?

DST due diligence is the structured evaluation of an offering before investment. It examines five areas: sponsor track record, full-cycle history, assets under management, and financial strength; property location, tenants, leases, occupancy, and demand; debt LTV, maturity, and rate; PPM risks; and investor suitability, load, and fees. It is a repeatable framework for understanding an illiquid security and confirming its fit before exchange proceeds are committed.

### Why does the DST sponsor matter so much?

The sponsor and trustee make virtually all material decisions: acquiring, managing, financing, and selling the property. Beneficial owners do not control those decisions. A strong sponsor may have a long across-cycle record, transparent full-cycle results against projections, substantial assets under management, financial strength, and the ability to support a property through a soft period. A short, all-bull-market record or weak balance sheet is a caution flag. Sponsor quality, fees, and transparency vary widely.

### What is a full-cycle DST and why does it matter?

A full-cycle DST has been taken from acquisition through sale. It is the evidence of how investors actually fared, rather than how an offering looked at launch. Review whether distributions were maintained, how sale price compared with acquisition cost, and what total return investors realized against original projections. Past full-cycle outcomes do not guarantee future results, but a record across market conditions helps assess sponsor discipline.

### How do I evaluate the property in a DST?

Examine whether the location has stable or growing employment, population, and demand for its property type. Then assess the property type’s own demand drivers. For a single-tenant net-lease property, inspect tenant credit, remaining lease term, and expiration risk. For multi-tenant properties, inspect rent roll, occupancy, lease rollover, and concentration. Compare rents and occupancy with the market and identify deferred maintenance or capital needs. These factors determine whether projected income can persist.

### What should I look for in a DST's debt structure?

Examine LTV, maturity relative to the expected hold, fixed versus floating rate, interest-only periods, amortization, and refinancing assumptions. Conservative leverage offers more cushion than high leverage; debt maturing in a difficult market can force a poorly timed refinance or sale. Fixed-rate debt locks borrowing cost, while a floating rate can squeeze distributions as rates rise. Trust debt is non-recourse to investors, but still shapes their risk. All-cash DSTs remove financing risk but provide no debt replacement.

### What is a PPM and why should I read the risk factors?

The PPM, or Private Placement Memorandum, is the DST’s central offering document. It describes property, sponsor, structure, fees, projections, and risks. Its risk factors disclose illiquidity, no public secondary market, lack of investor control, unguaranteed distributions, property decline, tenant default, refinancing trouble, possible loss of principal, conflicts, fees, operating restrictions, and projection assumptions. Reading it turns a headline yield into a fuller understanding of what can go wrong.

### What are the 'seven deadly sins' restrictions in a DST?

They are IRS restrictions derived from Revenue Ruling 2004-86 that preserve a DST’s 1031-eligible, passive structure. In broad terms, after closing the trustee cannot accept new capital, renegotiate debt or obtain new borrowing, reinvest sale proceeds, make more than limited capital expenditures, reserve more cash than reasonable needs, or freely enter or renegotiate leases. The restrictions limit active intervention if trouble occurs, which is why they belong in PPM review.

### How do I confirm a DST is suitable for me?

First confirm accredited-investor eligibility: generally income over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly, or net worth over $1 million excluding the home. Then determine whether an illiquid, multi-year hold fits liquidity needs and risk tolerance. Broker-dealer suitability review considers financial condition, goals, horizon, other holdings, and whether too much exchange capital is concentrated in one trust. A strong deal may still be unsuitable for a particular investor.

### What fees does a DST charge?

The upfront load may include selling commissions, a dealer-manager fee, and organizational and offering costs. Ongoing costs commonly include asset-management and property-management fees, and there may be a disposition fee when property is sold. The PPM discloses these amounts, often as total load. Fees reduce net return, so compare the full fee picture across offerings rather than assuming a higher fee is always worse or harmless.

### How many DSTs should I invest in?

There is no fixed number. Spreading an exchange across several DSTs by sponsor, property type, and geography can manage single-property, tenant, sponsor, debt, and market risk. Minimum investments are often around $25,000 to $100,000, which can make diversification practical even for a moderate exchange. More DSTs mean more diligence, sponsors, administration, and possibly fees. The right count depends on exchange size, risk tolerance, comfort with concentration, and 45-day identification rules.

### Can a DST lose money?

Yes. Distributions can fall or stop if property income falls, property value can decline, tenants can default or vacate, occupancy can drop, and debt can become difficult to refinance. Leverage amplifies losses as well as gains. Because DSTs are illiquid, an investor generally cannot exit early; capital is committed until the sponsor sells. Due diligence reduces but cannot eliminate the risk of loss, including principal. Size investments carefully and diversify.

### Is a higher projected distribution always better?

No. A higher projected distribution can result from higher leverage, weaker tenancy, shorter lease term, a riskier market, or aggressive assumptions. The relevant question is whether tenants, leases, occupancy, and market conditions can sustain income through the multi-year hold, and whether the projected return compensates for PPM risks. A lower projected yield from a strong sponsor, creditworthy tenant, conservative debt, and healthy market may be preferable. Every distribution remains a projection.

### Do all DST sponsors offer the same quality?

No. They differ in years and cycles of operation, completed-offering results, assets under management, balance-sheet strength, fees, and transparency. Some have decades of experience and a long full-cycle record; others are newer, lightly capitalized, or operate only in favorable markets. Because the sponsor makes the investment decisions, each sponsor should be evaluated on its own merits rather than treated as interchangeable.

### What's the difference between an all-cash and a leveraged DST?

An all-cash DST owns property without a mortgage. It has no maturity, rate sensitivity, refinancing need, or leverage to amplify losses, but it provides no debt replacement for an exchanger whose relinquished property had debt. A leveraged DST uses a non-recourse trust-level loan. It can help replace relinquished debt in a 1031 exchange, where investors generally need to replace both equity and debt to defer gain fully, but leverage adds maturity and refinancing risk and magnifies outcomes. The suitable structure depends on debt-replacement needs and risk tolerance.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate, with 1031-eligible beneficial interests.

**Due Diligence:** The process of evaluating an offering's risks before investing.

**Sponsor:** The company that acquires, structures, and manages a DST.

**Full-Cycle DST:** A DST taken from acquisition through to sale.

**Track Record:** A sponsor's history of offerings across market cycles.

**Assets Under Management (AUM):** The total value of assets a sponsor manages.

**PPM:** Private Placement Memorandum — the DST's central offering document.

**Risk Factors:** The PPM section disclosing what could go wrong.

**Loan-to-Value (LTV):** The ratio of loan amount to property value (leverage).

**Non-Recourse Loan:** Trust-level debt for which investors aren't personally liable.

**Fixed vs. Floating Rate:** Whether a loan's interest rate is locked or variable.

**Seven Deadly Sins:** IRS restrictions limiting what a DST trustee may do.

**Load:** The upfront fees and commissions on a DST offering.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a DST fits the investor's situation.

**Diversification:** Spreading an exchange across multiple DSTs to manage risk.
