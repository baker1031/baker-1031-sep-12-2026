# How to Evaluate a REIT: FFO, AFFO & NAV

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing REIT screens led by yield and a familiar P/E ratio. The screen is fast. It is also built on an earnings number that can obscure more than it reveals.

Standard net income understates how much a REIT really earns because real estate depreciation is a large non-cash charge even as properties often appreciate. The proper toolkit is funds from operations (FFO), adjusted FFO (AFFO), and net asset value (NAV). This guide explains why REIT earnings metrics differ, what each measure does, and how to use them together.

For broad background on a [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html), start with the complete REIT guide before applying these measures to a specific issuer.

First-order thinking is to select the biggest yield or the lowest single multiple. Second-order thinking asks whether cash covers the distribution, whether the price is fair relative to recurring earnings and assets, how debt must be refinanced, and which assumptions carry the valuation. This is educational information, not investment advice. Verify current figures and methodologies in each REIT’s filings.

## Why earnings metrics differ for REITs

GAAP net income understates a REIT’s true earnings mainly because of depreciation. Accounting rules require a REIT to depreciate buildings over time, recording a large non-cash expense that reduces annual reported net income. Yet real estate often holds or increases its value rather than declining as a wasting asset such as equipment might. The depreciation charge can therefore overstate economic decline.

For a property-heavy business, this is consequential. A REIT can show modest or even negative net income while its properties produce substantial cash. Standard P/E and ordinary net income can consequently mislead. The industry uses measures that add back real estate depreciation to better describe operating cash generation.

That is the foundation: GAAP net income is distorted by non-cash property depreciation, so REIT analysis needs FFO and AFFO rather than ordinary net income or P/E.

## Funds from operations (FFO)

Funds from operations, or FFO, is the foundational REIT earnings measure and the Nareit (National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts) standard. Its core calculation is:

> **FFO = GAAP net income + real estate depreciation and amortization − gains on property sales**

The Nareit definition has additional adjustments, but the purpose is direct. It adds back the non-cash depreciation and amortization that distort net income, then removes one-time gains on property sales that are not recurring operating income. The result better represents recurring earnings from operating properties.

FFO is the measure analysts and the industry use for operating performance. P/FFO—share price divided by FFO per share—is the REIT analogue to P/E and helps compare valuations. Our guide on [how to read a REIT's FFO](how-to-read-a-reits-ffo.html) shows where to find the measure in filings.

## Adjusted FFO (AFFO)

AFFO refines FFO toward the cash available for distribution. FFO corrects depreciation but does not account for recurring capital needed to keep property competitive, or for accounting smoothing of rent. AFFO deducts recurring maintenance capital expenditures and straight-line rent adjustments:

> **AFFO = FFO − recurring/maintenance capex − straight-line rent adjustments**

The result is closer to the cash left after maintaining the properties and is often more useful for judging sustainable distribution capacity. See [AFFO vs. FFO: which metric matters more](affo-vs-ffo-which-metric-matters-more.html) for a fuller comparison.

There is an important qualification: AFFO has no one standard formula. REITs can differ in their definition of recurring capex, rent adjustments, and other items. AFFO is more cash-focused, but it requires careful like-for-like comparison and reading of the reconciliation.

## Net asset value (NAV)

NAV is the balance-sheet view of value: the estimated current market value of a REIT’s property and other assets, less liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. NAV per share is the estimated underlying net-asset value attributable to each share.

For a traded REIT, comparing the share price to NAV per share shows a premium or discount to the property value. A price below NAV is a discount and may indicate a lower price relative to assets; a price above NAV is a premium. It is a signal to investigate, not a result by itself. The connection is discussed further in [REIT NAV premiums and discounts explained](reit-nav-premiums-and-discounts-explained.html).

For a non-traded REIT, periodic NAV usually supplies the share value and redemption price. NAV is therefore an asset-based complement to earnings-based FFO and AFFO, though it carries appraisal and assumption uncertainty.

## Putting the metrics together

No single measure is enough. Use FFO, through P/FFO, to evaluate recurring earnings and compare valuation. Use AFFO and the distribution-per-share-to-AFFO-per-share payout ratio to judge whether distributions are supported by sustainable cash; a payout above AFFO is a warning. Use NAV to see whether the market price represents a premium or discount to the underlying real estate.

Then extend the work. Review leverage and the maturity schedule, property type and quality, occupancy and lease terms, management quality, and growth prospects. FFO is the earnings view, AFFO is the distributable-cash view, and NAV is the asset view. Debt and qualitative analysis complete the assessment.

## Key Takeaways

- GAAP net income understates REIT earnings because large non-cash property depreciation depresses it even though properties often appreciate.
- FFO—net income plus real estate depreciation and amortization less gains on property sales—is the Nareit-standard earnings metric; P/FFO is the REIT P/E equivalent.
- AFFO deducts recurring maintenance capex and straight-line rent adjustments, giving a closer view of distributable cash and distribution sustainability.
- NAV is the per-share value of property less liabilities. Use FFO, AFFO, and NAV with debt and qualitative factors; no one metric is sufficient.

## Payout ratios and debt levels

Two checks sharpen the analysis. First, test the payout ratio against AFFO: distribution per share divided by AFFO per share. A ratio comfortably below **100%** of AFFO suggests real cash supports the distribution with a cushion. A ratio at or above AFFO warns that the distribution may not be sustainable and could be cut.

Second, study debt. Examine the leverage ratio, whether debt is measured against assets or a cash-flow measure such as EBITDA; the maturity schedule; and interest cost. High leverage amplifies return and risk. Near-term maturities create refinancing risk, especially in difficult or high-rate credit markets. A debt profile belongs alongside earnings and asset measures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why doesn't net income work for evaluating a REIT?

GAAP depreciation creates a large non-cash expense that can reduce reported net income even when real estate holds or gains value. A REIT can report modest or negative net income while its properties generate substantial cash. Standard P/E can therefore mislead; FFO and AFFO add back the property-depreciation distortion to better assess earning power.

### What is FFO?

FFO is funds from operations, the Nareit-standard REIT earnings measure. Starting with GAAP net income, it adds back real estate depreciation and amortization, removes property-sale gains, and includes some Nareit-specified adjustments. It better describes recurring operating-property earnings. P/FFO, price divided by FFO per share, is the REIT P/E equivalent.

### How is FFO calculated?

Start with GAAP net income. Add back real estate depreciation and amortization and subtract gains—or add back losses—on property sales. The core formula is net income plus depreciation and amortization minus gains on property sales. Per-share FFO divides the result by shares outstanding. Read an issuer’s reconciliation because minor variations exist, even though Nareit provides the common framework.

### What is AFFO and how does it differ from FFO?

AFFO is adjusted FFO. It starts with FFO, subtracts recurring maintenance capex and straight-line-rent adjustments, and may include other issuer-specific adjustments. It comes closer to cash available for shareholders because it recognizes recurring upkeep and rent smoothing that FFO ignores. The benefit is a better distribution-capacity measure; the cost is that definitions vary across REITs.

### What is NAV for a REIT?

NAV is net asset value: estimated market value of real estate and other assets, less debt and other liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. It shows an estimated per-share value of net assets. Traded shares below NAV trade at a discount; shares above NAV trade at a premium. Non-traded REITs use periodic NAV for share value and redemption pricing. NAV is useful, but appraisal and assumption uncertainty remains.

### What is the P/FFO ratio?

P/FFO is share price divided by FFO per share, the REIT version of P/E. A lower P/FFO may indicate a lower price relative to FFO; a higher P/FFO may reflect a richer valuation because of growth or property quality. Compare similar property types and quality, and use the ratio with AFFO-based distribution analysis and NAV. P/FFO alone does not capture cash coverage, leverage, or asset value.

### What does it mean if a REIT trades at a discount to NAV?

The share price is below the estimated per-share value of properties less liabilities. It may suggest a lower price relative to assets, while a premium may reflect payments for growth, quality, or management. But a persistent discount can signal poor management, weak assets, high leverage, or sector headwinds, and NAV itself relies on appraisals and assumptions. Investigate the reason, not just the discount.

### How do I assess whether a REIT's distribution is sustainable?

Focus on distribution per share divided by AFFO per share. Because AFFO deducts maintenance capex and rent smoothing, a payout comfortably below **100%** of AFFO generally gives a cash cushion. A payout at or above AFFO suggests risk of an unsustainable distribution or a cut. Also examine distribution trend, whether any payout comes from return of capital or borrowing, and growth prospects. FFO payout can help, but AFFO is the more conservative cash measure.

### Why do debt levels matter for a REIT?

REITs use leverage, which magnifies gains and losses. Review debt relative to assets or EBITDA, the maturity schedule, and interest cost. Variable-rate or maturing debt can become more expensive in a high-rate market, and near-term maturities create refinancing risk if credit tightens. A highly levered REIT with looming maturities is riskier than one with moderate, well-laddered debt at a similar FFO level.

### Are REITs sensitive to interest rates?

Yes. Higher rates may increase cost on variable and maturing debt, reducing FFO and AFFO; they can pressure property value and NAV through cap rates; and they make yields on bonds and cash more competitive. Falling rates can be supportive. Evaluate the share of variable-rate debt, maturity schedule, and sector sensitivity. Mostly fixed-rate, well-laddered debt is generally more resilient than heavy near-term variable-rate exposure.

### Can I rely on a single REIT metric?

No. FFO measures recurring earnings but not distribution coverage or asset value. AFFO better approximates distributable cash but is less standardized. NAV shows estimated asset premium or discount, but it is appraisal-based. Yield alone says little about coverage or risk. Treat P/FFO, AFFO payout, NAV, leverage, and business judgment as a toolkit; a high yield or low P/FFO can hide material weakness.

### Do REITs report FFO and AFFO themselves?

Yes. REITs generally report FFO and often AFFO in filings and earnings releases, together with reconciliations to GAAP net income. FFO follows a common Nareit framework, though the reconciliation still matters. AFFO may also be named FAD (funds available for distribution) or CAD (cash available for distribution), and capex and rent adjustments differ. NAV may be reported by a REIT—especially a non-traded issuer with periodic NAV—or estimated by analysts. Use reported figures as a starting point and verify the methodology.

### Is a higher FFO always better?

Not necessarily. Assess the price paid through P/FFO, the rate of FFO-per-share growth, AFFO distribution coverage, NAV premium or discount, debt, and asset quality. High FFO purchased at a high P/FFO can be less appealing than moderate FFO at a lower valuation. Rising FFO per share can create value; shrinking FFO per share, perhaps through dilution or declining properties, does not. The aim is sustainable, growing FFO per share at a reasonable price with manageable debt, not simply the largest FFO number.

### What qualitative factors should I consider beyond the metrics?

Review property type and quality across industrial, residential, retail, office, healthcare, or data centers; location; occupancy, lease length, tenant credit, and rent escalators; management experience, alignment, and track record; pipeline and ability to grow FFO per share; debt cost, structure, and capital access; and the sector and economic setting. Metrics support judgment, rather than replace it. Strong metrics cannot fully offset weak properties, weak management, or poor sector prospects.

## Glossary

- **FFO:** Funds from operations — the Nareit-standard REIT earnings metric.
- **AFFO:** Adjusted FFO — FFO less recurring capex and rent adjustments.
- **NAV:** Net asset value — per-share value of properties less liabilities.
- **GAAP Net Income:** Standard accounting earnings, distorted by depreciation.
- **Depreciation:** A large non-cash charge that depresses REIT net income.
- **Nareit:** The trade association defining the FFO standard.
- **P/FFO:** Price-to-FFO ratio — the REIT equivalent of a P/E ratio.
- **Maintenance Capex:** Recurring spending to keep properties competitive.
- **Straight-Line Rent:** Accounting smoothing of lease income, adjusted in AFFO.
- **Distributable Cash:** Cash truly available for distribution, approximated by AFFO.
- **Payout Ratio:** Distribution relative to FFO or AFFO per share.
- **Premium/Discount to NAV:** Share price above or below NAV per share.
- **Leverage Ratio:** Debt relative to assets or cash flow.
- **Debt Maturity Schedule:** When a REIT’s debt comes due and must be refinanced.
- **Gains on Property Sales:** One-time sale gains removed when computing FFO.
- **Occupancy Rate:** The share of leasable space that is leased.
