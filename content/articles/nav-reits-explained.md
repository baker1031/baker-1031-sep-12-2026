# NAV REITs Explained

I have been thinking about how easily the word “NAV” can make an illiquid investment sound more liquid than it is. A price that appears every month—or even every day—feels reassuring. It is not the same thing as being able to sell at will.

The modern NAV REIT is a different structure from the legacy non-traded REIT. It is perpetual-life, continuously offered, and priced frequently at net asset value. It generally has lower upfront loads and recurring opportunities to request repurchases. None of that changes the central fact: it remains a non-traded, illiquid security for an accredited or otherwise suitable investor after a broker-dealer suitability review. Current offering terms should be verified with an advisor, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

First-order thinking focuses on the attractive changes—more frequent pricing, lower upfront loads, and regular redemption windows. Second-order thinking asks what the NAV actually measures, how much liquidity is available when other investors also want out, what the fee schedule takes over a long hold, and whether the underlying real estate is worth owning. That is the more useful frame.

## What a NAV REIT is

A NAV REIT is a non-traded [Real Estate Investment Trust](reits-guide.html) whose shares are priced at net asset value, an estimate of the per-share value of its real estate and other assets, calculated on a frequent, regular schedule. Like other non-traded REITs, it is registered with the SEC but not listed on a stock exchange. It owns or finances income-producing real estate and follows REIT rules, including the requirement to distribute most of its taxable income.

The distinguishing combination is frequent NAV-based pricing, a perpetual or open-ended life, a continuous offering, and an ongoing share-repurchase program. Shares are typically offered at current NAV plus any applicable sales charge, which ties the purchase price to a recent estimate of underlying value rather than a fixed offering price padded by high commissions.

This was designed as an evolution of older non-traded REITs. The older model often had a fixed offering price, a finite life, and a distant liquidity event. The newer model looks more like a continuously priced open-ended fund. But it is still non-traded, illiquid, and subject to limits.

## Daily/Monthly NAV pricing

The defining feature is pricing frequency. Legacy non-traded REITs sometimes valued shares only once a year, or even less often. A NAV REIT usually computes NAV monthly and, in some cases, daily.

The calculation is straightforward in concept: value the real estate using appraisals and valuation methodologies, add other assets, subtract liabilities, and divide by shares outstanding. The resulting per-share NAV is normally the figure at which new investors buy, subject to any sales charge, and at which the REIT repurchases shares.

Frequent pricing gives an investor a regularly updated estimate rather than a stale number. It also ties the purchase and redemption price to recent conditions. Yet NAV is still an appraisal-based estimate, not a continuously traded market price. It can appear smoother than an exchange-listed REIT’s price because it is measured differently. Lower reported volatility is not an absence of underlying real-estate risk.

> The useful shift is cadence: a monthly or daily NAV is more current than a stale annual figure, but it is still not a live auction price.

## Perpetual-Life structure

A perpetual-life NAV REIT has no fixed end date and no predetermined liquidation event. Legacy non-traded REITs commonly raised capital during a defined offering period, held assets for a set number of years, and then sought an exit through an exchange listing, a portfolio sale, or a merger. The timing was often outside an investor’s control.

The NAV REIT is open-ended instead. It can continuously raise and deploy capital, hold property indefinitely, and manage the portfolio without a forced wind-down. An investor can hold for income and seek liquidity through the recurring repurchase program rather than wait for a terminal event.

The trade-off is important. There is no built-in finish line that returns all capital. Liquidity depends on the repurchase program, which is limited. Perpetual life means open-ended, not easy exit.

## Liquidity features and limits

Because it is not exchange-listed, a NAV REIT’s main liquidity mechanism is its ongoing share-repurchase program. That is a meaningful improvement over waiting years for a single legacy liquidity event. Investors generally have recurring, often monthly, windows to ask the REIT to repurchase shares at NAV.

The limits are equally material. Repurchases are commonly capped at around **5%** of NAV per quarter, sometimes described as roughly **2%** per month. If requests exceed the limit, requests can be prorated. The board may reduce, suspend, or gate the program, particularly in stressed markets when many holders seek an exit at the same time. There can also be early-repurchase discounts and holding-period conditions.

So the liquidity is real, but limited and not guaranteed. An investor who might need ready access to capital should not treat a NAV REIT as an on-demand source of cash. The specific repurchase provisions in the offering documents matter.

## Key takeaways

- A NAV REIT is a non-traded, frequently NAV-priced, continuously offered, perpetual-life REIT.
- NAV is normally calculated monthly and sometimes daily, setting the purchase and redemption price from a regularly updated estimate.
- Ongoing repurchase liquidity is commonly capped around 5% of NAV per quarter and can be gated or suspended.
- NAV REITs differ from legacy non-traded REITs, which often had high upfront loads of roughly 10% to 12%, a fixed life, and infrequent valuations.

## Fees and lower loads

Reducing the heavy fee burden of legacy non-traded REITs was a central aim of the NAV structure. Older programs commonly carried upfront sales loads around 10% to 12%, made up of selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, and offering costs. A meaningful part of an investor’s money could therefore miss the real estate from the start.

NAV REITs generally have lower upfront loads. Many use multiple share classes that spread costs over time rather than concentrate them in one large initial charge, allowing more capital to be deployed earlier.

That is an improvement, not a free pass. A NAV REIT can still charge management fees, performance or incentive fees tied to returns, operating expenses, and ongoing distribution or servicing fees. Different share classes can carry different structures. Over a long hold, the ongoing charges can be material. Review the complete fee schedule—upfront, ongoing, and performance-based—and its effect on net returns rather than stopping at the initial load.

## NAV REIT vs. legacy non-traded REIT

The contrast is most useful when it is explicit.

| Feature | NAV REIT | Legacy non-traded REIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pricing | Frequent NAV, often monthly and sometimes daily | Fixed offering price and infrequent valuation |
| Life | Perpetual, open-ended, no forced liquidation | Fixed life ending in a liquidity event |
| Upfront load | Generally lower or spread out | Commonly around 10% to 12% |
| Liquidity | Regular repurchase requests, capped and gateable | Limited redemptions, with distant terminal exit as the main route |

The NAV structure is more transparent and more investor-friendly in design. It has not become a publicly traded REIT. Both structures are non-traded, illiquid securities. NAV is an estimate, repurchases are limited, and the investor still bears real-estate and other risks. A NAV REIT is a longer-term product for a suitable investor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a NAV REIT?

It is a non-traded, SEC-registered REIT priced at a frequently computed net asset value, often monthly and sometimes daily. It owns or finances income-producing property, is continuously offered, has perpetual life, and provides recurring but limited repurchase opportunities. These features modernize the legacy non-traded model; they do not make the security liquid.

### How is a NAV REIT priced?

NAV comes from appraised real estate and other assets, minus liabilities, divided by shares outstanding. It is normally calculated monthly and sometimes daily, then used for purchases, subject to any sales charge, and repurchases. The figure is more current than an annual valuation, but remains an appraisal-based estimate rather than a continuously market-traded price.

### What does perpetual-life mean for a NAV REIT?

It means no fixed end date or predetermined liquidation event. The REIT can continuously raise and deploy capital and hold assets indefinitely. Investors rely on the capped share-repurchase program rather than an eventual listing, sale, or merger. There is flexibility, but no automatic finish line for their capital.

### How does liquidity work in a NAV REIT?

Liquidity generally comes from recurring, often monthly, requests that the REIT repurchase shares at NAV. The program is commonly capped around 5% of NAV per quarter, or roughly 2% per month. Requests can be prorated, and the board can reduce, suspend, or gate the program. Holding-period conditions and early-repurchase discounts can apply. Confirm the actual terms before investing.

### What is the difference between a NAV REIT and a legacy non-traded REIT?

NAV REITs generally use frequent NAV pricing, perpetual life, lower upfront loads, and ongoing limited repurchases. Legacy programs commonly had a fixed offering price, infrequent valuations, a fixed life leading to a listing, sale, or merger, and upfront loads around 10% to 12%. Both remain non-traded, illiquid products for suitable investors.

### Are NAV REITs liquid?

No, not in the way public REITs are liquid. There is no exchange on which shares can be sold at any time at a market price. Regular repurchase windows are meaningful but capped, subject to proration, and capable of being suspended or gated. Treat the investment as longer-term capital rather than capital available on demand.

### What are the fees on a NAV REIT?

NAV REITs generally reduce or spread out the legacy 10% to 12% upfront load, often through several share classes. They still have management, possible performance or incentive, operating, and sometimes servicing or distribution fees. Examine the complete schedule for the specific share class, including its long-hold effect on net returns.

### Is a NAV REIT the same as a publicly traded REIT?

No. A public REIT trades throughout the day on an exchange at supply-and-demand market prices and has daily liquidity. A NAV REIT is non-traded, uses periodic appraisal-based NAV pricing, and provides liquidity only through a capped, gateable repurchase program. NAV REITs are typically offered through broker-dealers to accredited or otherwise suitable investors after a suitability review; publicly traded REITs can be purchased through a brokerage account.

### How often is a NAV REIT’s value updated?

Most commonly monthly, and in some cases daily. The REIT values property and other assets, subtracts liabilities, and divides by shares to publish a per-share NAV. That is a regularly refreshed estimate, but it can lag rapid market change because it is not a live traded price.

### Can a NAV REIT suspend redemptions?

Yes. The board can reduce, suspend, or gate a share-repurchase program. If requests exceed the commonly cited 5% quarterly cap, the REIT can prorate them. In stressed markets, when demand for redemptions can be greatest, that discretion may be particularly consequential. Read the offering documents and plan for an extended hold.

### Why were NAV REITs created?

They were designed to address well-known issues with legacy programs: high 10% to 12% loads, stale annual valuations, fixed lives, and uncertain distant liquidity events. Lower or spread-out costs, frequent NAV pricing, perpetual life, and recurring repurchase opportunities are improvements. They do not transform a non-traded security into a liquid one.

### Who can invest in a NAV REIT?

They are typically offered through a broker-dealer to accredited or otherwise suitable investors. A suitability review considers finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. Minimum investment amounts and different share-class eligibility can apply. The review reflects the product’s illiquidity and complexity.

### How are NAV REIT distributions taxed?

They are taxed like other REIT dividends. Most ordinary REIT dividends are ordinary income rather than qualified dividends because the REIT paid no corporate income tax. Qualified REIT dividends can be eligible for the Section 199A **20%** deduction; the 199A deduction was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. Some distributions may be return of capital, which reduces basis, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the character on Form 1099-DIV. A tax advisor should determine the investor’s treatment.

### Are NAV REITs a good investment?

There is no universal answer. Frequent NAV, lower upfront loads, perpetual life, and limited ongoing liquidity can fit some longer-term investors. Capped and gateable repurchases, management and performance fees, appraisal-based valuation, and the possibility that distributions or NAV fall may make a particular offering unsuitable for others. Evaluate the specific underlying real estate, fees, and repurchase terms; confirm suitability; and do not use past performance as a forecast.

## Glossary

- **NAV REIT:** A non-traded, frequently NAV-priced, perpetual-life REIT.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** Per-share assets minus liabilities, divided by shares.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** An SEC-registered REIT that is not listed on an exchange.
- **Perpetual-Life:** An open-ended structure with no fixed end or liquidation.
- **Continuous Offering:** Ongoing sale of new shares at current NAV.
- **Share-Repurchase Program:** The NAV REIT’s ongoing, capped redemption mechanism.
- **Repurchase Cap:** The redemption limit, often around 5% of NAV per quarter.
- **Gating:** A board’s reduction or suspension of redemptions.
- **Proration:** Partial fulfillment when redemption requests exceed the cap.
- **Legacy Non-Traded REIT:** The older high-load, fixed-life, infrequently priced model.
- **Upfront Load:** An initial sales charge; legacy programs commonly ran roughly 10% to 12%.
- **Liquidity Event:** A legacy program’s terminal listing, sale, or merger.
- **Share Class:** A version of NAV REIT shares with its own fee structure.
- **Performance Fee:** An incentive fee a NAV REIT may charge on returns.
- **Suitability Review:** An assessment of whether the NAV REIT fits the investor.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
