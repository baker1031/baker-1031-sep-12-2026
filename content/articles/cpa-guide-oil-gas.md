# The CPA's 2026 Guide to Oil & Gas Investing

I keep seeing oil-and-gas tax questions framed around a single deduction. The better work begins when the CPA lays the whole picture on the table: the deduction, the income that can absorb it, AMT, at-risk limits, entity form, self-employment tax, and the investment that sits underneath all of it.

First-order thinking sees first-year IDC expensing, percentage depletion, or an active loss that can offset W-2 income. Second-order thinking asks what conditions make each result available, what AMT and at-risk rules give back, and whether the client can bear the underlying risk. Oil and gas is the most tax-driven alternative, and the CPA's analysis often has the greatest effect on the outcome. This guide covers the tax mechanics and reporting, recurring traps, and a client checklist; it pairs with the client-facing [mineral and royalty guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html) and the oil & gas cluster.

## Key Takeaways for Advisors

- Intangible drilling costs are electively expensed under §263(c)/Reg. 1.612-4; excess IDCs are an AMT preference (§57), with special rules for independent producers — model AMT.
- Depletion (§§611–613A): take the greater of cost or percentage depletion; percentage depletion (generally 15% for independent producers/royalty owners) can exceed basis, subject to 65%-of-taxable-income and 100%-of-net-property-income limits.
- A working interest is excepted from the passive-activity rules (§469(c)(3)) if not held in a liability-limiting form, so its losses are active and can offset W-2 income; at-risk rules (§465) cap the loss.
- Operating working-interest income is generally subject to self-employment tax; royalty income is not. Mineral and royalty interests can qualify as like-kind real property for §1031.

## How oil & gas investing works (advisor's refresher)

Investors participate chiefly through a working interest, the operating interest that bears costs and earns deductions, or a royalty interest, a cost-free share of revenue. The tax profile differs sharply between them; see [working vs royalty](working-interest-vs-royalty-interest.html). A direct working interest in a drilling program produces a large first-year deduction through IDCs, ongoing depletion, and, because of a passive-activity exception, active losses. The advisor's task is to model the IDC and depletion deductions, AMT and at-risk effects, self-employment-tax exposure, and reporting while keeping expectations grounded in the substantial underlying risk.

## Intangible drilling costs and the election

Under §263(c) and Reg. §1.612-4, a working-interest owner may elect to expense intangible drilling costs currently rather than capitalize them. These are labor, fuel, chemicals, site preparation, and other non-salvageable drilling costs, often 70–85% of a well's cost. Tangible equipment is depreciated, generally with 7-year MACRS. The election creates the headline first-year deduction, but its value depends on income available to absorb it and on the AMT analysis below. See the client memo on [IDCs](intangible-drilling-costs.html).

## The AMT interaction

IDCs have an alternative-minimum-tax issue that needs to be modeled. Excess IDCs — broadly, IDCs exceeding 65% of net oil-and-gas income — are an AMT preference item under §57. There is meaningful relief for independent producers rather than integrated oil companies, but the benefit can still be limited: the preference cannot reduce AMTI by more than a set percentage. A large IDC deduction can therefore trigger or increase AMT for a high-income client and partly claw back the apparent write-off. Run regular-tax and AMT calculations side by side before presenting a specific benefit.

## Depletion: percentage vs. cost

Depletion (§§611–613A) shelters production income for the life of the well, and the taxpayer takes the greater of cost or percentage depletion each year. Cost depletion recovers basis over reserves. Percentage depletion — generally 15% of gross income for independent producers and royalty owners under the §613A small-producer exemption — is limited to 100% of net income from the property and 65% of the taxpayer's taxable income, with excess carrying over. It can continue after basis is fully recovered, producing deductions exceeding the investment. Both working-interest and royalty owners can claim depletion. Details are in the [depletion memo](oil-gas-depletion-allowance.html).

## The working-interest passive exception

This provision draws high earners. Under §469(c)(3), a working interest is not a passive activity when the taxpayer holds it in a form that does not limit liability, such as a general-partner or direct working interest rather than a limited interest. Its losses are active and can offset W-2 wages and other active income, unlike most investment losses trapped by passive rules. Once losses have been allowed, subsequent net income from the working interest may be recharacterized in certain cases. A passive royalty interest does not qualify. The memo on [offsetting W-2 income](oil-gas-losses-w2-income.html) covers the mechanics.

## At-risk rules and self-employment tax

Two further limits shape the answer. The at-risk rules (§465) cap deductible losses at the amount the client has at risk, generally invested cash plus certain recourse debt. A working interest funded partly with non-recourse financing may therefore have limited losses. Income from an operating working interest is generally trade-or-business income subject to SE tax, an often-overlooked cost; royalty income is not subject to SE tax. Both affect the net benefit of the active-loss strategy.

## Mineral 1031 eligibility and reporting

Mineral and royalty interests are generally treated as interests in real property, so they can qualify as like-kind for a §1031 exchange. A producing royalty interest can be exchanged for other real property, and vice versa; that is the focus of the [mineral-rights guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html). Operating working-interest activity is typically reported on Schedule C, with SE tax, or through a partnership K-1, while royalty income goes on Schedule E. Depletion is computed and claimed against each. Get the entity form right at the outset because it drives both passive characterization and SE-tax exposure.

## Client due-diligence checklist

- Confirm the interest type — working vs royalty — and the holding form (liability-limiting?), which drives passive treatment and SE tax.
- Model the IDC election and run regular-tax vs AMT side by side.
- Compute depletion (greater of cost/percentage), applying the 65%/100% limits.
- Apply at-risk limits to the deductible loss.
- Account for SE tax on operating working-interest income.
- Set reporting — Schedule C/K-1 for working interests, Schedule E for royalties.
- Vet the sponsor and geology; underwrite the deal independent of the deductions, given fraud and dry-hole risk.

## What clients should know

The deductions are genuine and powerful, but conditional. AMT can erode the IDC benefit, at-risk rules cap losses, and a working interest brings SE tax and real liability. The tax benefit does not lessen investment risk: oil and gas is speculative, can lose its entire value, and has a real fraud history. Underwrite the program on its merits. Frame the strategy for high-income, risk-tolerant, accredited clients who want to shelter ordinary income and can lose the capital, not as a tax play separate from economics.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can oil & gas working-interest losses offset W-2 income?

Yes. Under §469(c)(3), a working interest held in a form that does not limit liability is not a passive activity, so its losses are active and can offset W-2 and other active income, driven mainly by the first-year IDC deduction. Royalty interests do not qualify.

### How are intangible drilling costs treated for tax?

A working-interest owner may elect under §263(c) to expense IDCs currently rather than capitalize them, often 70–85% of a well's cost in year one. Tangible equipment is depreciated, generally with 7-year MACRS.

### Do IDCs affect the AMT?

Yes. Excess IDCs, broadly over 65% of net oil-and-gas income, are an AMT preference item under §57, with some relief for independent producers. A large IDC deduction can trigger or increase AMT, so model both calculations.

### How does percentage depletion work?

Independent producers and royalty owners can deduct generally 15% of gross income under the §613A small-producer exemption, limited to 100% of net property income and 65% of taxable income, with excess carrying over. It can continue after basis is recovered.

### Is oil & gas income subject to self-employment tax?

Operating working-interest income is generally subject to SE tax as trade-or-business income; royalty income is not. This often-overlooked cost affects the net benefit of a working interest.

### Can mineral or royalty interests be 1031 exchanged?

Generally yes. Mineral and royalty interests are treated as interests in real property, so they can qualify as like-kind for a §1031 exchange with other real property, subject to the usual rules.

## Glossary

### §263(c) / IDC Election

The election to expense intangible drilling costs currently rather than capitalize them.

### §613A Percentage Depletion

The small-producer/royalty-owner depletion of generally 15% of gross income, which can exceed basis, subject to limits.

### §469(c)(3)

The working-interest exception making such losses active and able to offset W-2 income.

### At-Risk Rules (§465)

Rules limiting deductible losses to the amount the taxpayer has at risk.

### AMT Preference

Excess IDCs added back in the alternative minimum tax calculation under §57.
