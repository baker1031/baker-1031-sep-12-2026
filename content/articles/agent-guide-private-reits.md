# The Agent & Broker's 2026 Guide to Private REITs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Category:** For Agents & Brokers · **Research:** Baker 1031 Research · **Updated:** June 2026 · **Reading time:** 14 min read

I keep seeing agents let a real-estate conversation end when the client says, “I do not want another building.” That is often the beginning of a different useful conversation. The agent who recognizes the need, respects the securities boundary, and makes the right introduction stays in the client’s life.

A REIT can provide real-estate exposure without a deed, tenants, repairs, or a mortgage. It is not an agent’s product to sell. It is a category to explain plainly and a reason to refer the client to the right licensed professional. First-order thinking sees no commission and ends the discussion. Second-order thinking sees a compliant introduction as relationship capital.

### Key Takeaways for Agents & Brokers

- Private and non-traded REITs can give a cash investor passive, diversified real-estate income without buying or managing property.
- REITs are securities. Educate and refer; do not sell, recommend a particular REIT, offer investment advice, or accept securities compensation.
- The relevant client has cash to invest, not a property to exchange—a segment many agents overlook.
- Knowing the alternative protects the trusted relationship when there is no transaction.
- Partnerships with licensed REIT and advisory firms can send future real-estate business back to the agent.
- Direct purchase, 1031, DST, and REIT knowledge lets an agent answer more than just the questions that produce an immediate commission.

## What a private REIT is, the agent's version

A REIT is a company that owns income-producing real estate and pays out at least 90% of taxable income to investors. A public REIT trades daily on an exchange. A non-traded REIT is registered but not listed, has periodic net-asset-value pricing, and limited redemptions. A private REIT is a private placement for verified accredited investors and is the least liquid type. See the [REIT guide](reits-guide.html) and [CPA guide](cpa-guide-private-reits.html).

For an agent, the useful model is simpler: the client owns a slice of professionally managed real estate rather than a single building. The client trades control and liquidity for diversification and professional management. The agent need not know a particular yield or redemption queue; the skill is recognizing a client who wants exposure but not ownership.

## The securities line: educate and refer

Every kind of REIT is a security. Non-traded and private REITs are sold by licensed representatives; private REITs are offered to verified accredited investors under a private placement memorandum. A real-estate license does not authorize an agent to sell a REIT, recommend a particular offering, give investment advice, or receive securities compensation.

The compliant role is to educate and refer. Explain the category, its passive-income purpose, and its illiquidity. Then introduce the client to a licensed firm and the client’s own financial advisor. Do not turn the introduction into a transaction-based securities fee; any compensation arrangement must comply with securities and real-estate law. Keep it clean and let the licensed firm handle the securities work.

## Which clients are a fit

Watch for cash to deploy but no desire to own. A priced-out rental buyer, a former landlord who does not want another 1031 property, an accredited busy professional with no interest in tenants, and an income-seeking retiree are recurring examples. The common signal is interest in real estate as an investment but resistance to owning a building.

Private REITs require accredited status. The licensed firm verifies income, net-worth, or licensing qualifications; the agent does not qualify the client. Public or non-traded alternatives may fit clients who do not qualify for a private offering.

## How to talk to your client about it

Lead with the goal, name the category, draw the boundary, and offer the introduction: “It sounds like you want real-estate income and growth without owning a building. A REIT is a slice of a professionally managed real-estate portfolio. I am not licensed to sell or advise on it, but I can introduce you to a firm that handles it and include your financial advisor.”

Keep direct ownership open: if the client later wants a building, that remains the agent’s work. Avoid a particular REIT, a yield quote, or the claim that it is a good investment.

## How your clients actually use them

Clients use private and non-traded REITs for passive diversified real-estate income using cash outside a 1031 exchange. They may want an allocation beyond one property, retirement distributions, or exposure to apartment, industrial, and medical-office portfolios they could not assemble alone.

The trade is real: liquidity and control are exchanged for diversification and management. Redemptions can be limited, queued, or paused; distributions are sponsor targets rather than guarantees; fees can be higher than a public index. The licensed firm performs suitability review, but the agent should never present the option as easy money.

Consider a long-time rental client who sells, is done with tenants, does not want another 1031 exchange, and holds cash for real-estate income. A transaction-only agent leaves. An advisor-minded agent explains that a REIT is illiquid and a security, introduces a licensed firm and the client’s CPA, and charges nothing. The relationship may later produce a family purchase referral. That is the practical value of a clean introduction.

## REIT vs. DST vs. direct purchase for the client

| For the client who… | Fitting path | Agent role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Has cash, wants passive income, no building | Private or non-traded REIT | Educate and refer to a licensed firm |
| Is selling property and wants to defer gain | 1031 into a DST | Educate and refer; DST is also a security |
| Wants to own/manage a building | Direct purchase | Handle the transaction |
| Sold a business or stock and has non-real-estate gain | Opportunity Zone fund | Refer to licensed firm and CPA |

The security rows are introductions; direct purchase is the listing. The [agent DST guide](agent-guide-dst.html) follows the same compliant approach, and [DST vs. REIT](dst-vs-reit.html) explains the distinction.

## Handling the common questions a client raises

“Is it safe?” deserves a careful answer: a REIT can diversify the work and concentration of one building, but it remains real estate, is illiquid, and can lose principal. The agent is not rating it. The agent is setting expectation and referring to the firm that evaluates suitability. Background is available in [Are Private REITs Safe?](are-private-reits-safe.html).

“How do I get money out?” requires the same candor: private and non-traded REIT redemptions can be limited, queued, or paused, so the investment should be capital the client can leave invested for years. “What will it pay?” is not a yield quote; distributions vary by sponsor and strategy and are not guaranteed.

## Mistakes agents make with REITs

Do not drift into specific advice, praise a particular REIT, quote yield, or call it a good investment. Do not ignore a cash-rich client merely because there is no immediate transaction. Do not oversell the upside or treat illiquidity and a distribution reduction as an afterthought. And do not assume a securities referral fee works like a real-estate commission. Compliance and counsel should approve any arrangement.

## How it grows your business

REIT knowledge lets an agent serve the cash investor as well as the seller, retain a trusted-advisor role, form compliant referral relationships, and offer a fuller map: direct purchase, 1031, DST, and REIT. Content about real-estate investing without buying a building can also surface needs in the agent’s sphere.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a real estate agent sell a REIT?

No. A real-estate license does not permit sale, recommendation, advice, or securities compensation. Refer the client to a licensed firm and advisor.

### How is a REIT different from helping a client buy property?

A REIT is passive diversified income from cash without a building to manage. Direct purchase is ownership and an agent transaction.

### Which clients are a fit for a private REIT?

Cash investors seeking income without ownership: priced-out buyers, former landlords, busy accredited professionals, and retirees. The licensed firm verifies accreditation.

### How does this help my business if I can't sell it?

It serves clients with no transaction, builds trust and firm partnerships, and can lead to later listings and referrals.

### What should I say to a client interested in a REIT?

Describe the managed-portfolio category, state that you are not licensed to sell or advise, refer the client and advisor to a licensed firm, and keep direct ownership open.

### Can I earn a referral fee for sending a client to a REIT firm?

Transaction-based securities compensation requires securities-law compliance. A real-estate license alone does not authorize it; confirm any arrangement with compliance and counsel.

### What's the difference between a private REIT and a DST for my client?

A private REIT is a cash investment for passive income and is not 1031 replacement property. A DST is a fractional real-estate interest that can qualify as 1031 replacement property. Both are securities an agent refers rather than sells.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A real-estate investment trust that owns income-producing property and distributes most income; a security.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** SEC-registered but unlisted, with periodic NAV and limited or gated redemptions.
- **Private REIT:** A Regulation D REIT for verified accredited investors and the least liquid REIT type.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting SEC income, net-worth, or licensing thresholds.
- **Educate and Refer:** Explain the category and introduce a licensed firm and advisor.
- **Referral:** An introduction to a licensed professional; related compensation must follow securities and real-estate law.
- **Suitability:** The licensed firm’s—not the agent’s—assessment of client finances and goals.
- **Liquidity:** Ease of conversion to cash; private and non-traded REIT redemptions are limited and conditional.
