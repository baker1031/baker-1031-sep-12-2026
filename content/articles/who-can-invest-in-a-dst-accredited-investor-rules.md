# Who Can Invest in a DST? Accredited Investor Rules


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I think of accreditation as the item investors should settle before the exchange clock starts. It can look like a formality when a sale is still hypothetical. It is not. A DST may be passive, fractional institutional real estate that can serve as replacement property and satisfy debt replacement, but it is also a security offered through a broker-dealer after a suitability review.

First-order thinking says the income or net-worth threshold is the whole answer. Second-order thinking asks how title will be held, which Rule 506 offering is involved, what verification it requires, and whether the particular illiquid investment fits the investor at all. That is the practical gateway described in [our comprehensive Delaware Statutory Trust primer](dst-guide.html), not a detail to race through on day 43. The [Accredited investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html) rules set that gateway.

This guide explains who can invest in a DST: the individual income and net-worth tests, entity routes, verification, why the rule exists, and the early work that avoids an avoidable delay. The rules can change and can apply differently to different facts. Confirm the current rules and your own status with an advisor. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

## Key Takeaways

- Individuals generally qualify through income above $200,000 single or $300,000 joint in each of the prior two years, with the same expected in the current year, or through net worth above $1 million excluding a primary residence.

- LLCs, partnerships, trusts, and corporations can qualify through more than $5 million of assets, the look-through test, institutional status, or trust-specific rules.

- Rule 506(b) commonly permits self-certification. Rule 506(c), used by many DSTs, requires documented reasonable-steps verification.

- Accreditation is necessary, not sufficient. DSTs are less-disclosed, illiquid, complex Regulation D private-placement securities, and a broker-dealer suitability review still follows.

## Accredited Investor Income & Net-Worth Tests

For an individual, the analysis usually begins with two financial tests. Under the income test, income must have exceeded $200,000 for a single person or $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent in **each** of the last two years, with a reasonable expectation of the same level this year. One unusually good year is generally not enough. The point is consistency across both prior years and the current-year expectation.

The other route is net worth. An individual, alone or together with a spouse, qualifies with net worth above $1 million, excluding the value of the primary residence. The home-equity exclusion matters. Do not count the home’s equity toward the $1 million, and generally do not count its mortgage as a liability either. An increase in mortgage or home-equity debt shortly before investing can be an exception, so the calculation needs care.

Put plainly, either route can work: income over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly in each prior two years, with the same expected this year; or net worth over $1 million excluding the primary residence. You do not need both. These thresholds establish eligibility to consider a DST, but the details are nuanced; confirm them with an advisor.

## Accredited Entities

Many 1031 investors hold their relinquished property in an LLC, partnership, trust, or corporation and want the replacement interest held the same way. Those entities can be accredited in their own right. A common route is the asset test: a corporation, partnership, LLC, or certain trust generally qualifies with more than $5 million in assets, provided it was not formed specifically to buy the offered securities. Banks, insurance companies, registered investment companies, and certain other institutions qualify automatically.

The look-through test is equally important for smaller family structures. An entity can be accredited if every equity owner is accredited, even if the entity does not have $5 million in assets. That is often how a single-member LLC or small family entity qualifies.

Trusts have additional rules. A trust with more than $5 million in assets, not formed specifically to acquire the offering and directed by a sophisticated person, can qualify. Certain revocable trusts can qualify based on the accredited status of their grantors. Because the entity used for the DST may need to mirror the entity that held the relinquished property, coordinate the entity’s structure and accreditation with an attorney and advisor before the exchange is underway.

## How Accreditation Is Verified

The evidence required depends on the Regulation D exemption. Under Rule 506(b), an investor can generally self-certify on a subscription questionnaire. The issuer may reasonably rely on that representation absent a reason to doubt it, so financial documents are not ordinarily required.

Rule 506(c) takes a stricter approach. It permits public advertising, but the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify that an investor is accredited. For the income test, that may mean recent tax returns or W-2s. For the net-worth test, it can mean bank, brokerage, and other account statements together with liability information. Many investors instead use a written letter from a CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser, or registered broker-dealer confirming that the professional reviewed the finances and that the investor qualifies. Some sponsors use third-party verification platforms.

Many DSTs use 506(c). Expect documentation or a professional letter rather than a simple attestation, and arrange it before a time-sensitive [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). The lighter 506(b) process and the documented 506(c) process are not interchangeable.

## Why DSTs Require Accreditation

A DST beneficial interest is treated as direct real property for 1031 purposes under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. It is nevertheless sold as a security. DST offerings rely on Regulation D exemptions from full SEC registration, chiefly Rules 506(b) and 506(c). Those exemptions are conditioned on selling to accredited investors, except for limited 506(b) cases involving a small number of sophisticated non-accredited investors.

The rule is also an investor-protection measure. Private placements do not provide the full, SEC-reviewed disclosures of a registered public offering. They can offer less standardized information and fewer regulatory protections. Income, net worth, and certain professional credentials are the SEC’s proxy for an investor’s ability to evaluate and absorb the risks of a private offering.

That distinction matters because DSTs are long-term and illiquid. They carry real-estate, sponsor, structural, fee, and loss risk. Accreditation is not an arbitrary hurdle or a promise that the investment is appropriate; it is the legal condition that matches a less-disclosed security with investors presumed able to evaluate and bear its risks. This is educational background, not legal advice.

## Licensed Individuals & Other Paths

Accreditation is not only about income and net worth. Individuals holding a Series 7 general securities representative license, Series 65 investment adviser representative license, or Series 82 private securities offerings representative license in good standing qualify regardless of income or net worth. The SEC recognizes professional knowledge as another route.

There are other paths as well. A knowledgeable employee of a private fund can qualify with respect to that fund. Certain family offices and family clients meeting the applicable size and sophistication standards can qualify. Spousal-equivalent rules permit unmarried couples in a relationship generally equivalent to a spouse to pool income and assets for joint thresholds.

The same entity routes remain available: more than $5 million of assets, an all-accredited owner group, institutional status, and trust-specific rules. If the basic individual tests are not a clear fit, it is worth asking whether a license, entity structure, family-office status, or other path applies. Verify the route with an advisor rather than assuming it does.

## Confirming Your Eligibility

The sensible time to confirm eligibility is before a sale turns the question urgent. Identify the test or path first: income, net worth, a qualifying license, or an entity route. Then decide whether the investment will be held individually, jointly, through an LLC, or through a trust. That choice can determine which test applies and what documents are needed.

For a likely 506(c) offering, gather recent tax returns or W-2s for the income test; asset and liability statements for the net-worth test; or arrange a letter from a CPA, attorney, RIA, or broker-dealer. This keeps verification from becoming a bottleneck near the 45-day identification deadline or the 180-day closing deadline.

Accreditation is only the first gate. A broker-dealer must also conduct a suitability review for the specific DST, considering financial circumstances, goals, liquidity needs, time horizon, and risk tolerance. Work with the advisor and broker-dealer before identifying a DST so that an available offering can be evaluated without forcing the decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does it mean to be an accredited investor?

An accredited investor is someone the SEC permits to invest in certain private securities offerings, including DSTs, that are exempt from full registration and disclosure. An individual generally qualifies through income above $200,000 single or $300,000 joint in each of the last two years, with the same expected this year; net worth above $1 million excluding a primary residence; or an active Series 7, 65, or 82 license. Entities can qualify, for example, with more than $5 million in assets or all accredited equity owners. The standard is meant to identify investors presumed to have the financial resources and sophistication to assess less-regulated offerings and bear losses. Confirm personal status with an advisor.

### What is the income test for accreditation?

The income test requires income over $200,000 if single or over $300,000 jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent in each of the last two years, with a reasonable expectation of the same this year. One high-income year generally does not qualify. A person relying on joint income should generally use the joint figure consistently rather than mix individual and joint figures across years. The income test alone is enough; the net-worth test is not also required. For a 506(c) DST, recent tax returns, W-2s, or a professional letter can verify the result. Confirm the specific application with an advisor.

### What is the net-worth test for accreditation?

The net-worth route requires more than $1 million individually or jointly with a spouse, excluding the primary residence. Do not include home equity, and generally do not include the home mortgage as a liability. A recent increase in that mortgage or a home-equity loan shortly before investing may count as a liability. The calculation looks to other assets—investments, cash, and real estate other than the home—less liabilities. The net-worth test alone is sufficient. A 506(c) offering can require asset and liability statements or a professional letter. Confirm the details, particularly after recent borrowing against the home.

### Can I invest in a DST through an LLC or trust?

Yes. Many investors use an LLC, trust, or other entity, often to match the entity that held the relinquished property. The entity itself must qualify. More than $5 million of assets can qualify an eligible corporation, partnership, LLC, or certain trust if it was not formed specifically to buy the offering. Under the look-through test, every accredited equity owner can qualify an entity even without $5 million of assets. A trust over $5 million directed by a sophisticated person can qualify, and certain revocable trusts can rely on accredited grantors. Confirm the entity’s structure, qualification, and verification requirements with an attorney and advisor.

### How do I verify my accredited status?

Rule 506(b) generally permits self-certification on a subscription questionnaire, with no financial documents required absent reason to doubt the representation. Rule 506(c), which permits public advertising and is common for DSTs, requires reasonable steps to verify. That can involve recent tax returns or W-2s for income, bank and brokerage statements plus liability information for net worth, or a letter from a CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser, or broker-dealer. Third-party verification platforms are also used. Prepare the materials before the 1031 deadline period and confirm the specific offering’s requirements.

### Why do DSTs require investors to be accredited?

DST interests are Regulation D private-placement securities, even though the beneficial interest is treated as direct real property for 1031 purposes under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. Rules 506(b) and 506(c) exempt the offering from full SEC registration and are conditioned primarily on accredited investors. The policy reason is that private placements have less standardized disclosure and fewer protections than registered offerings. The accredited standard targets investors presumed, by income, net worth, or license, to evaluate and bear losses in illiquid, long-term, complex securities. It is both a securities-law condition and an investor-protection measure. This is educational information, not legal advice.

### Do professional licenses make me accredited?

Yes. A Series 7, Series 65, or Series 82 license in good standing can make an individual accredited regardless of income or net worth. The relevant licenses are the general securities representative, investment adviser representative, and private securities offerings representative credentials. The SEC may change the qualifying list, so confirm that a license remains in good standing and that the rule applies at the time of investment.

### Can my spouse and I combine income or assets to qualify?

Yes. Joint income above $300,000 in each of the prior two years, with the same expected this year, can satisfy the income test. Spouses can also combine net worth toward the $1 million threshold, excluding the primary residence. Spousal equivalents—cohabitants in a relationship generally equivalent to a spouse—may pool income and assets under the rules as well. Use the joint income test consistently rather than mixing joint and individual results across years, and expect a 506(c) verification to document both parties where relevant.

### Is accreditation enough to invest in a DST?

No. Accreditation makes an investor eligible to be offered a DST; it does not establish that a particular offering fits. A broker-dealer suitability review assesses financial situation, investment goals, liquidity needs, time horizon, and risk tolerance. A DST is illiquid, long-term, and exposed to real-estate and structural risk. Think of accreditation as the gate and suitability as the separate check on the investment itself. Both matter.

### What documentation do I need to prove I'm accredited?

It depends on the test and the offering. For income, recent tax returns or W-2s can show the required level in the prior two years. For net worth, bank, brokerage, and other asset statements, liability information, and sometimes a credit report can support a calculation above $1 million excluding the primary residence. A letter from a CPA, attorney, registered investment adviser, or broker-dealer who reviewed the finances can avoid sharing detailed records directly. An entity may need documents proving its own qualification. Gather the relevant documents or letter early and confirm the offering’s exact requirements.

### Can a non-accredited investor ever invest in a DST?

In practice, almost never. A Rule 506(c) DST is accredited-investor-only. A Rule 506(b) offering can technically include up to 35 sophisticated non-accredited investors, but sponsors usually limit DST offerings to accredited investors because including non-accredited investors brings added disclosure obligations and complexity. A person who does not qualify should examine all possible paths—income, net worth, license, or entity route—with an advisor, then consider other real-estate participation or 1031 replacement options if none applies.

### Does my primary residence count toward the net-worth test?

No. The primary residence is excluded. Its equity does not count toward the $1 million, and its mortgage generally is not treated as a liability. A recent debt increase, such as a new mortgage or home-equity loan shortly before investing, may be included as a liability to prevent borrowing against the home to create qualifying assets. The test therefore focuses on other assets less other liabilities. Confirm the calculation with an advisor, especially after recent home borrowing.

### How early should I confirm my accreditation before a 1031 exchange?

As early as possible, ideally before identifying a specific DST. The exchange offers 45 days after the relinquished-property sale to identify replacement property and 180 days to close. A 506(c) investment cannot be accepted until the required verification is complete. Identify the qualification route, decide the holding entity, and prepare documents or arrange the CPA, attorney, or RIA letter before the clock starts. Coordinate with the advisor and broker-dealer well in advance.

### Do the accreditation rules ever change?

Yes. The definition has evolved and can change again. The core thresholds—over $200,000 single income, over $300,000 joint income, and over $1 million net worth excluding a primary residence—have been in place for years, while the SEC has added licenses, knowledgeable employees of private funds, and certain family offices and entities. Policymakers have also discussed inflation adjustments and other changes. Treat these criteria as a current snapshot, verify the current definition at the time of investment, and do not rely on older information. This is educational information, not legal advice.

## Glossary

**Accredited Investor** — An investor meeting income, net-worth, licensing, or entity thresholds.

**Income Test** — Over $200k single / $300k joint in each of the last two years.

**Net-Worth Test** — Net worth over $1 million excluding your primary residence.

**Primary Residence Exclusion** — Your home is left out of the net-worth calculation.

**Accredited Entity** — An LLC, trust, or company qualifying as accredited in its own right.

**Asset Test** — An entity with more than $5 million in assets qualifies.

**Look-Through Test** — An entity qualifies if all its equity owners are accredited.

**Spousal Equivalent** — A cohabitant treated like a spouse for pooling income or assets.

**Series 7 / 65 / 82** — Professional licenses that can qualify an individual as accredited.

**Knowledgeable Employee** — A private-fund employee who can qualify for that fund.

**Self-Certification** — Attesting to accredited status, allowed under Rule 506(b).

**Verification** — Documented confirmation of accredited status, required under 506(c).

**Regulation D** — SEC rules exempting certain private offerings from registration.

**Suitability Review** — Assessing whether a DST fits an investor after accreditation.

**Rev. Rul. 2004-86** — The IRS ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible real property.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)** — A trust owning real estate, sold as a Reg D private placement.
