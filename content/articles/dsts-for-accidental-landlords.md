# DSTs for Accidental Landlords: An Exit Without a Tax Hit

I have been thinking about how many rental properties were not bought with a landlord’s plan. An inherited home, a former residence that would not sell, or a property retained after a move, marriage, or change in plans can become a rental by circumstance. Then the owner faces the work—tenants, repairs, rent collection, vacancies—without having chosen the job.

First-order thinking says: sell the burden and move on. Second-order thinking recognizes the tax bill. Appreciation and depreciation recapture can make a straightforward sale feel expensive enough that an owner keeps a property they no longer want. A 1031 exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can defer that tax while replacing active ownership with a passive fractional interest. It is not a universal exit; it is a trade of control and liquidity for professional management and a different form of real-estate exposure.

## The Accidental Landlord

An accidental landlord became a landlord by circumstance rather than deliberate investment choice. Typical paths include inheriting a property from a parent or relative, renting out a former home because it would not sell or because the market was down when the owner moved, retaining a property after a relationship change, or simply ending up with a rental through another turn of events.

The property may feel less like an asset than an obligation. Screening tenants, handling repairs, chasing rent, managing vacancies, and navigating difficult situations all require time, interest, and expertise. Selling can feel blocked because an appreciated long-held or inherited property may create a capital-gains tax bill. The owner is caught between unwanted management and unwanted tax.

## Exiting Without a Tax Hit

An outright sale can trigger tax on appreciation and on [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) previously claimed for the rental. For a property that has appreciated meaningfully, that combined bill can materially reduce the capital left after the sale.

A 1031 exchange permits reinvestment of sale proceeds into like-kind replacement real property while deferring gain. A DST interest can qualify as like-kind replacement property under IRS Revenue Ruling **2004-86**. Instead of selling, paying tax, and walking away with less equity, an owner may exchange into DST interests and keep the equity invested in passive real estate.

The distinction matters: a 1031 defers tax; it does not automatically forgive it. The deferred gain carries into the new interest. If a DST interest or subsequent like-kind property is held until death, heirs may receive a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) that can erase the deferred gain. If the owner later takes cash rather than exchanging again, the deferred tax generally becomes due. A CPA and estate advisor should apply those rules to the investor’s facts.

> The tax bill often keeps an accidental landlord stuck. A 1031 into DSTs can create an exit without the immediate tax hit.

## Passive Income via DSTs

A DST holds income-producing real estate. An investor owns a fractional beneficial interest while a professional sponsor handles acquisition, leasing, tenant relations, maintenance, repairs, financing, reporting, and the eventual sale. The investor receives a share of rental income without screening tenants, coordinating repairs, chasing rent, or filling vacancies.

That can replace an active obligation with passive ownership. DSTs commonly have a defined hold of about **5-7 years**. Net property income can be distributed to investors during that period, but it is a projection, not a guarantee. Occupancy, rents, expenses, property performance, sponsor decisions, and fees can all affect distributions.

The passivity is real, and so is the loss of direct control. DST rules sometimes described as the “seven deadly sins” sharply limit new actions by the trust and investors, which is part of the structure’s 1031 treatment. The investor trades day-to-day responsibility for reliance on the sponsor.

## Simplifying Your Holdings

An accidental landlord may own one odd property or several scattered properties that do not reflect an intentional plan. DSTs can consolidate that equity into a cleaner set of passive holdings. They can also diversify a single concentrated property across multiple DSTs, markets, and sectors.

The fractional form may also aid estate planning. A DST interest can be easier to divide among heirs than a physical property that must be split, sold, or co-managed by beneficiaries. Consolidating scattered rentals can make a portfolio easier to understand, value, and pass on. The value is not only less landlord work; it can be moving an unplanned position into a deliberate one.

## Income, Suitability, and Trade-Offs

A DST has a defined life. At the end of the hold—commonly five to seven years—the sponsor sells the property and the investor receives a share of proceeds. The investor may take cash and pay deferred tax then due, complete another 1031 exchange into new DSTs or other like-kind real property, or, if the offering provides it, potentially move into a REIT through a **721**/UPREIT exchange with operating-partnership units.

The price of passivity is meaningful. DSTs are illiquid, with little or no secondary market during the hold. They carry upfront load and offering costs plus ongoing management fees that reduce net returns. They are Regulation D securities available through a broker-dealer only to accredited investors after suitability review. That review considers financial condition, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. An owner gives up direct control and ready access to capital in exchange for passive ownership.

> A DST exit can solve the management burden and the tax barrier at once, but it asks the investor to accept multi-year illiquidity. That is exactly why suitability review matters.

## First Steps to Transition

Start before putting the property into a transaction that cannot be undone. Speak with a financial advisor or broker-dealer experienced with DSTs and 1031 exchanges, as well as a CPA. Confirm that the property, gain, goals, accredited status, time horizon, and liquidity needs fit the strategy.

The critical mechanical step is engaging a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) (QI) **before** the sale. A valid 1031 exchange requires that the owner not take actual or constructive receipt of the proceeds. The QI holds the proceeds and uses them for replacement property. A QI added after the sale cannot fix an exchange in which the seller received the funds.

With the QI in place, plan the timeline, sell the property, identify replacement DSTs within **45 days**, and close within **180 days**. DSTs can close quickly because they are pre-packaged offerings with property and financing already in place. Still, the identification must be timely and properly documented. Confirm [accredited-investor](accredited-investor-requirements.html) status early; a non-accredited investor generally cannot use a DST.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an accidental landlord?

An accidental landlord became a landlord by circumstance: inheritance, a former home that would not sell, a market downturn when moving, a relationship change, or another life event. The owner may not want tenant screening, repairs, rent collection, or vacancies, but may hesitate to sell because appreciation would create capital-gains tax. A 1031 exchange into DSTs can address that bind when the structure is suitable.

### How can a 1031 exchange help an accidental landlord?

It can defer capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture by selling the rental and reinvesting in like-kind replacement real property such as a qualifying DST interest. The QI must be engaged before sale; replacement DSTs must be identified in 45 days and acquired in 180 days. This preserves the equity in passive real estate rather than paying the tax immediately. A CPA should confirm treatment.

### Can I 1031 exchange an inherited rental property into a DST?

In many cases, an inherited rental held for investment can be exchanged into a DST. An inheritance commonly receives a step-up in basis to fair market value at death, which can reduce gain on a near-term sale. Post-inheritance appreciation and depreciation can still create tax, and a 1031 may defer it. Holding period, step-up, depreciation, and personal facts require CPA confirmation.

### Do DSTs really require no management on my part?

Yes, the sponsor handles acquisition, leasing, tenant relations, maintenance, financing, and sale while the investor owns a fractional interest. That means no landlord work for the investor. The related trade-off is that the investor also gives up direct control and liquidity; the sponsor makes the property decisions.

### Will I avoid the tax entirely, or just defer it?

A 1031 generally defers tax, including capital gain and depreciation recapture; it does not erase it immediately. Successive qualifying exchanges can continue the deferral. If qualifying property is held until death, a step-up in basis may eliminate the deferred gain—often called “swap till you drop.” If the owner cashes out instead of exchanging again, deferred tax is generally due then. Confirm the plan with a CPA and estate advisor.

### How do DSTs provide income to an accidental landlord?

The sponsor collects rent, pays property expenses, and distributes net income according to fractional ownership. Income commonly continues during a defined hold of around five to seven years without landlord work. It is projected, not guaranteed: occupancy, rents, expenses, fees, and property performance can reduce distributions.

### How do DSTs simplify my holdings?

They can turn a scattered set of inherited or incidental rentals into a smaller set of passive holdings, or spread one concentrated property across several markets and sectors. Fractional interests can also be easier for heirs to divide, value, and manage than a single physical property. The appropriate structure should be discussed with an advisor and CPA.

### Are DSTs liquid if I change my mind?

No. A DST is generally illiquid for the multi-year hold, commonly five to seven years, and there is little or no secondary market. An investor should not use capital needed in the near or medium term and may want to maintain other liquid assets. At the end of the hold, proceeds can be taken as cash, exchanged again, or potentially moved into a REIT if available.

### Do I need to be accredited to exchange into a DST?

Generally, yes. DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors. Qualification generally depends on income or net-worth thresholds, with the primary residence excluded from net worth, and the broker-dealer conducts a suitability review of finances, goals, liquidity, and risk tolerance. An inherited or appreciated property can contribute to net worth, but status should be verified early. Non-accredited investors generally need a different replacement strategy.

### What are the first steps to exit my rental into DSTs?

Talk with an advisor or DST broker-dealer and CPA about fit and accredited status. Engage the QI before selling. Plan the exchange timeline, sell with the QI in place, identify replacement DSTs in 45 days, and close in 180 days. Selling before the QI is engaged can disqualify the exchange.

### Why do I have to engage a qualified intermediary before selling?

The QI prevents the seller from taking actual or constructive receipt of the proceeds. It holds sale proceeds and uses them to acquire replacement DSTs. Receiving the money—even briefly—can disqualify the exchange and trigger the tax. The QI must be in place before closing, not added afterward.

### Is a DST exit right for every accidental landlord?

No. It may fit an accredited owner who wants to stop managing an unwanted rental, accepts loss of control and liquidity, has a suitable time horizon, and seeks passive real-estate income. It may not fit someone who needs liquidity, wants direct control, is not accredited, or has a short time horizon. Keeping the property or selling and paying tax can be better in some cases. Suitability review exists to make this assessment.

### What happens when the DST sells the property?

At the end of the defined hold, commonly five to seven years, the sponsor sells the properties, winds down the DST, and returns a share of proceeds. The investor can take cash and pay deferred capital-gains tax, begin another 1031 exchange into a DST or other like-kind property, or possibly use a 721/UPREIT route to REIT operating-partnership units. Plan that decision in advance with the advisor and CPA.

### What are the risks of exchanging into DSTs?

Risks include illiquidity, little or no secondary market, upfront load and ongoing fees, possible loss of principal, property performance that can reduce distributions, sponsor execution risk, concentration in specific assets, and non-recourse leverage at the property level. A 1031 error can also disqualify the exchange. Diversifying across DSTs, reviewing offerings, sizing investments appropriately, and confirming suitability can help manage risk, but distributions and returns are never guaranteed and past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Glossary

- **DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible fractional real estate.
- **Accidental Landlord:** Someone who became a landlord by circumstance, not by choice.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.
- **Like-Kind Real Property:** The real estate a 1031 requires; a qualifying DST interest qualifies.
- **Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling treating a qualifying DST interest as real property for 1031.
- **Capital-Gains Tax:** The tax on a property’s appreciation that a 1031 defers.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, also deferred by a 1031.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset at death that can erase deferred gain.
- **Passive Income:** Income from DSTs without landlord management work.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that acquires, manages, and sells the DST property.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds 1031 proceeds so the exchanger never receives them.
- **45-Day Identification:** The window to identify replacement property after selling.
- **180-Day Closing:** The deadline to close on replacement property in a 1031.
- **Defined Hold:** A DST’s multi-year period, often about five to seven years, before sale.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.
- **Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a DST fits the investor before investing.
