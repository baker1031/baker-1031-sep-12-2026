# The 45-Day Trap: Avoiding a Failed 1031 Exchange

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange** · **Baker 1031 Research** · **Updated June 2026** · **12 min read**

I keep seeing investors focus on the 180-day closing date because it looks like the larger number. It is important. But the more decisive clock is usually Day 45. Once that identification window closes, an investor without enough viable replacement property identified in writing has already failed, however much time remains to close.

The first-order view is that a 1031 exchange gives an owner 180 days. The second-order view is that the first 45 calendar days sit inside those 180 days, not in front of them. The critical work—finding, vetting, financing, and properly identifying credible replacements—must mostly be done before the relinquished property ever closes.

This is a procedural issue, but it is also a risk-management issue. A missed deadline can turn what was intended as a deferral into a fully taxable sale. The discussion below explains the two clocks, the identification rules, the form of a valid identification, and why a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can serve as a practical backup.

## Key Takeaways

- You have 45 calendar days from sale of the relinquished property to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days to close. Both clocks begin on the same day.
- Many failed exchanges fail at Day 45, not Day 180, because the investor does not have viable and properly identified choices.
- The 3-Property Rule, 200% Rule, and 95% Rule govern the identification list. One of them must fit.
- A valid identification is written, signed, unambiguous, and delivered to the qualified intermediary (QI) by midnight on Day 45. Verbal or informal lists do not qualify.
- A DST can be identified as a backup because it can close quickly, without an investor-level financing contingency, helping prevent unused exchange proceeds from becoming taxable.
- Weekends and holidays do not extend the deadlines. IRS relief tied to a federally declared disaster is the exception.

## The two deadlines, and why they run together

A 1031 exchange has two hard deadlines. Both start when the relinquished property transfers at closing, which is Day 0. The 45-day identification period and the 180-day exchange period run at the same time.

| Milestone | Deadline | What must happen |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day 0 | Closing of relinquished property | Sale proceeds go to a qualified intermediary, not to the investor. |
| Day 45 | Identification deadline | Replacement property is identified in writing and delivered to the QI. |
| Day 180 | Exchange deadline | Acquisition of replacement property is complete. |

The 45-day period is part of the 180-day period. By Day 45, a quarter of the overall runway has already been used.

There is another timing issue for a late-year sale. The actual closing deadline is the earlier of 180 calendar days or the due date of the tax return for the year of sale, including extensions. A seller closing in November may need to file an extension to preserve the full 180 days. Neither calendar period moves for a weekend or federal holiday. Missing either deadline collapses the exchange into a fully taxable sale.

## Why exchanges fail at day 45

Forty-five days is not enough time to begin a serious replacement search from scratch. Investors who wait until after the relinquished sale may run out of time, lose a bidding contest, or find title or financing trouble in the one property they liked.

That is usually a planning failure, not a shortage-of-property failure. A seller needs a list of options already vetted closely enough to identify with confidence. If a single primary target fails on Day 40 and there is no identified backup, the exchange is over. The deferral disappears, and capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, and net investment income surtax can all become due.

Treat Day 45 as the true finish line. Preparation before closing is what keeps the exchange alive.

## What a blown exchange actually costs

When an exchange fails, the sale becomes fully taxable. Several taxes can stack together. Long-term capital gains are taxed at 15% or 20% federally for most investors. For higher earners, the net investment income surtax can add 3.8%. Depreciation recapture can be taxed at a federal rate of up to 25%. States can tax the gain as well.

The combined bill can be roughly one-quarter to one-third of the gain. On a $1,000,000 gain, the source illustration is $250,000 to $330,000 of tax—capital that a completed exchange could have kept invested and compounding. The example is general and illustrative, not a projection of any particular transaction, but it explains why a few weeks of preparation can be high-value work.

## The three identification rules

The regulations provide three ways to identify replacement property. You need to satisfy only one. The proper choice depends on how many properties you want to name and their value relative to what you sold.

| Rule | How many you can identify | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3-Property Rule | Up to three properties, any value | It is the simplest and most common; value is irrelevant. |
| 200% Rule | Any number of properties | Combined value cannot exceed 200% of the property sold. |
| 95% Rule | Any number, any value | At least 95% of the total identified value must actually be acquired. |

Most investors use the 3-Property Rule because it permits a primary target and one or two backups without a value ceiling. The 200% Rule can help an investor spread proceeds among smaller properties, provided aggregate value does not exceed twice the relinquished property's value. The 95% Rule is a rarely used escape hatch because its acquisition requirement is demanding.

For example, after a $1,000,000 sale, the 3-Property Rule permits identification of a $900,000 apartment building, a $700,000 retail strip, and a DST backup, regardless of their combined value. If the investor instead wants five smaller rentals, the 200% Rule permits them only if the combined identified value is no more than $2,000,000. Select the rule before drafting the identification, not afterward.

## How to make a valid identification

An identification is not a casual note to a broker. It must be in writing and signed by the investor. It must describe the property unambiguously: normally a street address or legal description for real estate, and the specific DST plus the intended dollar amount or percentage interest for a DST. It must be delivered before midnight on the 45th day, to a party involved in the exchange—typically the QI. Delivery to an investor's own attorney, real-estate agent, or relative does not satisfy the rule because those are disqualified persons.

An investor can revoke and replace an identification before the deadline if the revocation and replacement are also written and signed. An early list is not a cage. But a vague description is a real problem. “A four-unit building in Austin” is not enough; a full address is. Deliver a clean written identification a day or two early and retain proof of delivery.

The replacement must be like-kind real estate held for investment or business use. That category is broad: apartment property, raw land, net-lease retail, industrial property, and DST interests can be on the same list. But the investor should not add filler property without a genuine intention and ability to acquire it. The identification is a statement of real intent, and a list padded with names invites scrutiny.

## The DST backup that defuses the trap

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) is a pre-packaged, institutionally managed real-estate investment that can accept 1031 money. It can be useful as a backup identification for three reasons.

First, its minimum can be low—often around $100,000—so it can take the leftover proceeds that need placement. Second, there is no investor-level financing contingency because the sponsor has already arranged debt at the trust level. Third, it can often close in days through a subscription rather than a negotiated purchase.

Name the DST as one of the three identified properties. If the primary purchase closes, the DST may not be used. If the primary stalls on Day 40, the investor can subscribe to the already identified DST, place the remaining proceeds, and complete the exchange on time. A DST can also mop up boot: if a direct replacement costs slightly less than the relinquished property, a small DST interest can absorb the difference and reduce a partial tax bill. Our guide explains the [mechanics of a 1031 exchange into a DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html).

Consider the source example. An investor sells a building for $1,200,000 and identifies a $1,200,000 medical-office property as the primary, a smaller retail property as the second choice, and a DST for the full amount as the third. On Day 38, the medical-office seller backs out. Instead of finding a new property with seven days left, the investor subscribes to the already identified DST, places the entire $1,200,000, and closes inside the 180-day window. The deferral is preserved and no boot is created. It is an illustration, not an assurance that a DST or any replacement will be available or suitable.

## Your 45-day game plan

The exchange is usually won before closing. Engage the QI before the relinquished sale closes; if proceeds touch the investor's hands, constructive receipt can make the exchange fail at the outset. Build a target list while the sale is pending. Vet financing and title on the primary target early, because those are familiar late-stage sources of delay.

Then identify with depth: a realistic primary, a genuine second option, and a DST backup that can close quickly if needed. Deliver the written identification to the QI by Day 40, not Day 45, leaving time to fix an error. Keep the 180-day clock in view and confirm the selected replacement can realistically close in that window. Our [full 1031 timeline](1031-exchange-timeline.html) sets out this sequence day by day.

Coordination matters as much as selection. The QI, lender, CPA, and replacement closing agent should work in parallel. A slow loan or title search can push a closing past Day 180. Investors who clear both deadlines tend to treat the exchange as a project with a schedule rather than a form signed at the end.

## Reverse, improvement, and disaster-relief wrinkles

The basic 45- and 180-day periods also matter in variations of the exchange. In a reverse exchange, the replacement property is acquired first and parked with an exchange accommodation titleholder. The investor then has 45 days to identify the relinquished property and 180 days to complete its sale.

In an improvement or build-to-suit exchange, construction must be completed and the improved property received within the 180-day window for those improvements to count toward exchange value. That makes timing tighter, not looser.

IRS disaster relief is the only circumstance in the source that can actually move the deadlines. When a federally declared disaster affects a taxpayer or property, the IRS can postpone the 45-day and 180-day periods, often to the later of 120 days or a general postponement date identified in a relief notice. The relief is automatic for affected taxpayers when it applies. It is for emergencies, not a plan for a late investor.

There is also an ownership-structure issue. If partnership or LLC co-owners intend to separate at sale, a possible drop-and-swap into tenant-in-common interests must be set up well before closing. Trying to reorganize ownership during the 45-day period risks a failed identification. Decide who is exchanging and who is cashing out before the clocks begin.

## Common mistakes that blow the deadline

The repeat failures are predictable:

1. Starting the replacement search after closing.
2. Identifying only one property and leaving no fallback.
3. Using a vague description rather than an address or a specific DST and dollar amount.
4. Delivering the identification to an agent or attorney instead of the QI.
5. Taking constructive receipt of the sale proceeds, even briefly.
6. Assuming a Saturday, Sunday, or holiday Day 45 shifts to a business day.

Each of these errors can be avoided through early preparation, a genuine backup, and careful delivery. The work is unglamorous, but it is the exchange. The practical capital-management stance is to build the list before sale, name backups you can actually execute, and treat documented delivery to the QI as a hard control. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the 45-day rule in a 1031 exchange?

After sale of the relinquished property, an investor has 45 calendar days to identify replacement property in writing and deliver the identification to the QI. The clock starts at closing and does not extend for weekends or holidays.

### Do the 45-day and 180-day periods run separately?

No. Both begin on the day the relinquished property transfers and run concurrently. The 45-day identification period sits inside the 180-day exchange period; an investor does not receive 180 days in addition to the 45.

### How many properties can I identify?

The 3-Property Rule permits up to three properties at any value. The 200% Rule permits any number if combined value stays within twice the sale price. The 95% Rule permits identification beyond those limits only if at least 95% of the total value identified is acquired.

### Why do most exchanges fail at day 45 rather than day 180?

Because Day 45 requires viable, vetted options to be ready in writing. Investors who search only after closing may not have credible replacements in time, and a single target that falls through without a backup ends the exchange.

### Can a DST be used as a backup identification?

Yes. A DST can have a low minimum, no investor financing contingency, and a quick subscription close. Naming one as an identified property can allow an investor to complete an exchange when a primary purchase fails, subject to suitability, availability, and the risks of the investment.

### What makes an identification valid?

It must be written, signed, unambiguous about the property, and delivered to the QI by midnight on Day 45. An address or legal description is appropriate for real property; a specific trust and intended amount or percentage is appropriate for a DST. A verbal list or note to the investor's own agent does not count.

### Can I change my identification after I submit it?

Yes, but only before Day 45. The investor can revoke an identification in writing and replace it with another signed written identification before the deadline. After Day 45, the list is locked.

## Glossary

**Identification Period:** The 45 calendar days after sale of the relinquished property in which replacement property must be identified in writing.

**Exchange Period:** The 180 calendar days, or the tax-return due date if earlier, in which replacement property must be acquired.

**3-Property Rule:** An identification method allowing up to three replacement properties at any value.

**200% Rule:** An identification method allowing any number of properties whose combined value is no more than 200% of relinquished-property value.

**95% Rule:** An exception allowing identification beyond the other limits only if at least 95% of identified value is acquired.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds exchange proceeds and receives the written identification, keeping the investor out of constructive receipt.

**Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange that is taxable; a small DST position can absorb it.
