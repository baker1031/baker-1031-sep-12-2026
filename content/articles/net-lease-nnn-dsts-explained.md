# Net-Lease (NNN) DSTs Explained

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing investors focus on the steadiness of a long rent check and skip the question that matters most: who has to send it? A net-lease DST can make real estate feel unusually quiet. That is useful. It is not the same thing as making its risks disappear.

First-order thinking says that a long triple-net lease and a familiar tenant make income safe. Second-order thinking asks what happens if that one tenant defaults, restructures, does not renew, or leaves a specialized building. This guide covers the structure, the credit question, the concentration trade-off, and the choice between single- and multi-tenant DSTs. DST interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after a suitability review; distributions are projected, never guaranteed.

## What Is an NNN DST?

A net-lease, or triple-net (NNN), DST is a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) that owns single-tenant commercial real estate leased to a business. The tenant pays base rent plus property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. The trust investor owns a fractional beneficial interest in the property and receives a share of rent that is largely passive. Properly structured DST interests can be like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange.

The buildings are often free-standing: a pharmacy, quick-service restaurant, [dollar store](pharmacy-and-dollar-store-nnn-dsts.html), bank branch, auto-parts store, or distribution facility. A corporate tenant may occupy the entire property on a long contract. That combination makes this one of the most hands-off, bond-like DST types, but it also puts the income behind a single corporate obligation.

> In a triple-net lease, the tenant pays the three large property costs—taxes, insurance, and maintenance—leaving the DST landlord with rent and very little to manage.

## How Triple-Net Leases Work

Under a typical NNN lease, the tenant pays real-estate taxes, building insurance, and maintenance and repairs in addition to base rent. Tax increases, insurance premium increases, and repair bills therefore do not ordinarily erode the landlord's rent in the way they can under a gross lease.

These leases frequently run **10–25 years**, often with renewal options. They may use rent escalators: fixed percentage increases every few years or periodic bumps to a stated amount. Long term, scheduled rent growth, and tenant-paid expenses produce contractual income with little day-to-day landlord work. The lease is doing much of the operational work, but the tenant still has to perform for the whole term.

## Income Stability and Credit Tenants

Credit quality is central. Review the tenant's credit rating, balance sheet, profitability, and the strategic importance of the location to its business. A large investment-grade national tenant generally offers more income security than a weaker or unrated business.

With a strong credit tenant, a long escalating, expense-insulated rent stream can resemble a corporate bond backed by real estate. The expected trade-off is usually a more modest yield than a property that needs more management. “Bond-like” is a description of cash-flow shape, not a promise. Even a strong company can run into trouble, so its credit must be assessed and monitored.

## Key Takeaways

- An NNN DST owns single-tenant property where the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance, creating a largely passive rent stream.
- Net leases often last 10–25 years and use rent escalators, which supports a low-management, bond-like income profile.
- Tenant credit quality drives the reliability of income.
- The central risk is single-tenant concentration: a vacancy can reduce income to zero, so credit, remaining term, and location matter.

## Risks: Single-Tenant Exposure

Simplicity has a price. One property and one tenant means income is all-or-nothing. A payment default, bankruptcy, or non-renewal can push property income to zero. There is no second tenant inside the property to soften that result.

Re-tenanting can take time and capital, and the new rent may be different. A longer remaining lease term can reduce near-term vacancy exposure. Stronger credit reduces default risk. A location important to the tenant and usable by other businesses can reduce the cost of a vacancy.

The same DST concerns remain in the background: sponsor, market, and financing risk; leverage; fees and load; and illiquidity throughout the hold. The question is whether the tenant, lease, location, economics, and hold period compensate you for the concentration you are accepting.

## NNN DST vs. Multi-Tenant DST

A single-tenant NNN DST offers the cleanest and simplest version of the structure. It can suit an investor who prioritizes passivity and is comfortable relying on one strong tenant. A multi-tenant DST may own a [grocery-anchored shopping center](grocery-anchored-retail-dsts.html), medical office building, or retail strip. Several tenants spread income, so one vacancy reduces rather than eliminates it.

That diversification brings multiple leases, rollover dates, tenant relationships, and more operational complexity. The choice is not which structure is universally better. It is simplicity and concentration on one side, diversification and more management on the other.

## Evaluating a Net-Lease DST

Start with the tenant: credit rating, balance sheet, profitability, and whether the location is important to its operations. Then read the lease: remaining term, rent escalators, and renewal options. Finally, test the real estate. Is the building well located, flexible, and re-leasable if the tenant leaves?

Look at the structure and economics together: single- or multi-tenant exposure, projected distributions, fees, leverage, and hold period. Projected distributions are estimates, not guarantees. A strong tenant, lease, and location can reinforce one another; a weakness in several places is more consequential than any isolated line item.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a net-lease (NNN) DST?

It is a DST that owns single-tenant commercial real estate under a triple-net lease. The tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance above base rent. Under IRS Revenue Ruling **2004-86**, a properly structured beneficial interest can be like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. Typical buildings include pharmacies, restaurants, dollar stores, bank branches, and distribution facilities. It is a passive, bond-like form of real estate income, not a guarantee: interests are securities offered through a broker-dealer to accredited investors after suitability review, and distributions are projected.

### What does “triple-net” mean?

It means the tenant pays the three nets—real-estate taxes, building insurance, and maintenance and repairs—besides base rent. A gross lease leaves those costs with the landlord; a single- or double-net lease moves only one or two categories. NNN treatment is why the landlord's rent is more insulated from operating-cost increases.

### How do triple-net leases work?

The tenant pays base rent plus the three nets, usually under a 10–25-year contract with renewal options and scheduled escalators. Fixed-percentage increases, periodic bumps, or similar terms can raise rent over time. The cash flow may be predictable and low management, yet remains dependent on the tenant's ability to pay.

### Why is tenant credit quality important?

There is one rent payer. Review its rating, balance sheet, profitability, and the site's importance to its operations. An investment-grade national tenant is generally stronger than a weak or unrated one, but no tenant is beyond financial trouble. Credit should be reviewed over the lease's life.

### What makes net-lease income bond-like?

A creditworthy corporate obligor, a stated long-term lease, scheduled rent increases, and tenant-paid operating costs can resemble corporate-bond cash flow. The yield is often modest because investors value that stability. It remains real estate with a single tenant and physical-property exposure, so it is not a bond and not risk-free.

### What is the main risk of a net-lease DST?

Single-tenant concentration. If the tenant defaults, goes bankrupt, or does not renew, income can fall to zero. Re-tenanting can cost time and money. Sponsor, market, financing and leverage risk, fees and load, and illiquidity also remain relevant.

### What happens if the tenant leaves?

The sponsor must find a new tenant and negotiate a new lease. Rent may be higher or lower; distributions can be reduced or suspended while the property is vacant, and debt service on a leveraged property still has to be covered. A broadly usable, well-located building can be easier to re-tenant, but distributions are never guaranteed.

### How does a net-lease DST compare to a multi-tenant DST?

Single-tenant NNN offers clean income and minimal management but full concentration. A multi-tenant property or portfolio spreads income across several tenants, so a vacancy has less effect, but adds leases, rollovers, and management complexity. Some investors use both within a diversified allocation.

### Are net-lease DSTs good for retirement income?

They can suit income-focused or retirement-minded investors who want passive, escalating rent and less active management. A 1031 exchange may move a retiring landlord into such an interest while deferring tax, with potential stepped-up-basis considerations later. But the interests are illiquid for a multi-year hold, a vacancy can cut income to zero, and distributions are projected. Size and diversify thoughtfully rather than treating the interest as a guaranteed bond substitute.

### Can I use a net-lease DST in a 1031 exchange?

Yes, if it is properly structured under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. You work through a qualified intermediary and generally must reinvest equal or greater equity and replace debt to fully defer, identify within **45** days, and close within **180** days. Confirm requirements and your facts with your intermediary and tax advisor; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### What kinds of properties do net-lease DSTs own?

They commonly own free-standing pharmacies, quick-service and casual-dining restaurants, dollar and discount retailers, convenience stores, auto-parts stores, bank branches, medical or veterinary clinics, and industrial or distribution facilities. Strong offerings typically combine a financially sound tenant, long lease, escalators, and a location that can be re-leased. Multi-tenant versions can include shopping centers, medical offices, and retail strips.

### What are rent escalators in a net lease?

They are scheduled increases in rent. A flat rent can lose purchasing power during a 10–25-year lease. Escalators can be fixed increases every year or every few years, periodic step-ups, or less commonly index-linked. Stronger, more frequent escalators can support income growth; weak or absent ones can allow real income to erode. Read the actual lease terms.

### Are net-lease DST distributions guaranteed?

No. They are projections based on the tenant continuing to pay. Default, vacancy, rising interest rates, refinancing needs, and unexpected costs can change cash flow. A DST is non-promissory: it does not promise return of capital or a stated yield, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Is a net-lease DST truly passive?

It is among the more passive real-estate structures: a professional sponsor owns and oversees the property while fractional beneficial owners receive their share of income, and the tenant bears the three nets. That differs from self-storage, apartments, or student housing, where leasing, marketing, maintenance, and rate management remain active businesses. The trade-off is concentration risk and a typically modest yield.

## Glossary

- **Net-Lease (NNN) DST:** A DST that owns single-tenant property on a triple-net lease.
- **Triple-Net (NNN) Lease:** A lease under which the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance.
- **The Three Nets:** Property taxes, insurance, and maintenance paid by the tenant.
- **Single-Tenant Property:** A building occupied by one tenant under one lease.
- **Multi-Tenant DST:** A DST whose property is leased to several tenants.
- **Credit Tenant:** A financially strong tenant whose lease income is more reliable.
- **Credit Quality:** A tenant's financial strength and ability to pay rent.
- **Bond-Like Income:** Predictable lease income that resembles a corporate bond's cash flow.
- **Rent Escalator:** A scheduled rent increase in a lease.
- **Lease Term:** The length of a lease, often 10–25 years for a net lease.
- **Single-Tenant Concentration:** The risk that losing one tenant cuts property income to zero.
- **Re-Tenanting:** Finding a new tenant after a vacancy.
- **Beneficial Interest:** A fractional, 1031-eligible ownership interest in a DST.
- **1031 Exchange:** A like-kind exchange that can defer capital-gains tax.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.
- **Sponsor:** The firm that structures and operates the DST.
