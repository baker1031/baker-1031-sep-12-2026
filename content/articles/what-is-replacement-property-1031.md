# What Is Replacement Property in a 1031 Exchange?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often “like-kind” is heard as a restriction. In real estate, it is usually a much wider door than people expect. The sale gives an investor an opportunity to change a portfolio, but the exchange rules still require discipline around the replacement asset, value, debt, and timing.

An exchange has two halves. The relinquished property is sold; replacement property is acquired. Choosing property that qualifies and serves long-term goals determines the tax deferral, the deadline outcome, and the portfolio that remains after the transaction.

First-order thinking asks, “What can I buy fast enough?” Second-order thinking asks whether the asset is truly held for investment, whether its value and debt work for full deferral, how its risk and effort compare with the property sold, and what happens if the primary deal does not close. The broad like-kind rule creates opportunity, not a reason to stop asking hard questions.

## What replacement property means

Replacement property is the real estate acquired to complete a 1031 exchange. Sale proceeds flow through a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) into that property so gain on the relinquished property can be deferred rather than currently taxed.

The replacement must be real property, held for investment or productive use in a trade or business, like-kind to what was relinquished, and acquired within the exchange deadlines using proceeds the QI holds. Those conditions matter together.

“Like-kind” refers to the nature or character of real estate held for investment, not its grade, type, location, or condition. A duplex can be exchanged for farmland, an office building for an apartment community, or a retail strip for a fractional interest in a hospital. Each can be investment real estate even though the operating experience differs.

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act narrowed Section 1031 to real property, eliminating personal-property exchanges. That change carried no sunset date. The standard remains broad inside real estate.

The boundaries are also important. A primary residence and a personally used second home do not qualify as investment property. Property held primarily for resale, such as fix-and-flip or dealer inventory, does not qualify. Nor do ordinary securities or partnership interests. A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) beneficial interest is the notable exception because the IRS treats a qualifying DST interest as a direct interest in real estate for 1031 purposes.

## What qualifies as like-kind replacement property

The test is purpose and character, not a rigid asset category. The property must be real estate held for income, appreciation, or productive business use, rather than a quick resale.

Investment intent is based on facts and circumstances: how the owner uses the property, how long it is held, and what happens during the hold. There is no fixed holding period, although a longer hold can make investment intent easier to demonstrate.

The eligible field includes apartment buildings, single-family rentals, office, retail, industrial, self-storage, hotels, medical offices, farmland, ranchland, timberland, and raw land held for investment. Leaseholds of 30 years or more, water and mineral rights that rise to real property under state law, and fractional interests held as tenants-in-common or through DSTs can also qualify. An apartment need not be replaced by an apartment.

Exclusions still govern the edges. Homes and personal-use second homes are out. Dealer property is out. Stocks, bonds, partnership interests, and other securities are out, except for the specific DST tax treatment. A DST can be a security for securities-law purposes while being treated as undivided real-estate ownership for tax purposes. Foreign real estate is like-kind only to other foreign real estate, not U.S. property.

## Just how broad the like-kind universe is

The breadth of the rule turns an exchange into a portfolio tool. An investor leaving a tired suburban rental can acquire a share of an institutional-grade industrial portfolio. A landlord tired of tenant calls can move into a triple-net property where the tenant handles taxes, insurance, and maintenance. A family holding appreciated farmland can diversify into apartments across several states.

All three can preserve deferral because the replacement is real property held for investment. The exchange can change asset class—from retail to residential—geography—from one local market to a national footprint—and the owner’s level of involvement—from active management to passive ownership.

That is the important distinction. Like-kind does very little to narrow the field. The investor should select for income, growth, risk, and effort, rather than buy a near-duplicate of the property being left behind.

## Active vs. passive replacement options

Active replacement means direct ownership and operation: another rental, a small commercial building, or a property managed alone or with partners. It offers control, the full upside, and the chance to add value through work. It also carries ongoing management and the pressure of closing a specific transaction within 180 days while financing and inspections are moving.

Passive replacement reduces day-to-day operations. A DST is a securitized fractional interest in professionally managed institutional real estate. A triple-net lease is another largely passive approach; the single creditworthy tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance under a long lease. Each can keep an investor in real estate without requiring maintenance calls.

The choice need not be all or nothing. Exchange proceeds can be split between an active property and a DST for diversification and ease. Identification rules allow multiple replacements, so a passive option can also be held as a backup. The right mix depends on desired control, tolerance for work, and confidence that the exchange will close on time.

## DSTs as replacement property

A DST can hold institutional properties such as apartment communities, industrial parks, medical offices, or grocery-anchored retail. It sells fractional beneficial interests to [accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html). Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, the IRS treats a qualifying beneficial interest as direct ownership of real estate, allowing it to serve as replacement property.

DSTs can solve practical replacement issues. Typical minimum investments are often $25,000 to $100,000, so an exchanger can spread proceeds over several properties and markets. They can close within days, making them a possible primary choice and a useful backup if a direct purchase stalls. Pre-arranged, non-recourse financing can replace debt paid off at sale without requiring a new loan application.

The trade-offs deserve equal attention. DST investors are passive; they cannot direct property decisions, and the sponsor controls operations and the eventual sale. Fees and a load are built into the offering. Holding periods are typically multi-year and illiquid, and results depend on sponsor competence and the underlying real estate. DSTs are securities offered through a broker-dealer and require a suitability review. For an investor seeking hands-on control, they may not fit.

## The equal-or-greater-value rule

Full deferral requires replacement property of equal or greater value than the property sold and reinvestment of all net equity. “Value” is the net sale price: gross sale price minus selling costs such as commissions.

Buying down creates [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), taxable up to realized gain. Two numbers control the analysis: total value and equity. Reinvesting all equity is not enough if the new property has less total value. An investor can buy up with additional cash or financing without a penalty.

A simple “napkin test” is helpful during the search. Draw one line for value and one for equity. Both should stay level or rise from the relinquished property to the replacement. A declining value line signals value boot; equity taken out signals cash boot. It is a rough check, not a substitute for the CPA’s calculation.

## The debt-replacement rule

Debt matters alongside equity. If the relinquished-property mortgage is paid off, that debt relief is treated as cash received unless it is replaced. An investor can replace it with at least as much new debt, equivalent cash contributed from outside the exchange, or a combination.

For example, an exchanger with a $400,000 mortgage who buys replacement property with only a $250,000 loan has $150,000 of mortgage boot unless $150,000 of additional cash is added. Cash can substitute for debt. Simply borrowing less without added cash does not avoid the consequence.

This is one reason a leveraged DST can be useful. Its non-recourse debt is already in place at a stated loan-to-value ratio. A DST whose LTV matches the old loan can replace the mortgage without a loan application, personal guarantee, or new underwriting for the investor. That can matter for retirees or others with limited current income who may not easily qualify for new financing.

## Identifying your replacement within 45 days

Even a suitable asset will not complete the exchange if it is not identified properly. Replacement property must be formally identified within 45 days after the relinquished-property sale. The notice must be written, signed, and unambiguously describe each candidate—usually by address or legal description—and it must be delivered to the QI, not the agent or attorney. A verbal intention or notice to the wrong party does not count.

There are three principal identification rules. The 3-property rule permits up to three properties at any value. The 200% rule permits any number of properties with a combined value up to 200% of the value sold. The 95% rule permits any number at any value, but requires closing on at least 95% of the identified value; its acquisition requirement makes it uncommon.

After day 45, an investor cannot add a new property. Identifying a primary target and at least one backup—often a fast-closing DST—builds insurance into the process. If the primary property stalls on day 70, an identified backup can make the difference between closing within the 180-day window and losing the deferral.

## Matching replacement property to your goals

The broad qualifying universe shifts the work from finding any eligible asset to choosing one that fits. Start with income: is current cash flow the goal, or is long-term appreciation more important? Stabilized fully leased property and many DSTs can lean toward steadier income. Value-add and development can lean toward growth, with more risk and effort.

Then consider control. An investor leaving management may prefer passive ownership. An investor who wants to operate property and add value may accept the additional closing pressure of a direct acquisition. Diversification matters too: one replacement concentrates risk, while several properties or DSTs reduce the impact of a single underperformer.

Finally, weigh closing certainty. Financing, inspections, and a seller’s decision can derail a complex direct deal. A certain-to-close option, often a DST, can anchor the exchange while the investor pursues a more ambitious asset. The best replacement is one that advances the investor’s goals and can actually close within 180 days.

## Key takeaways

- Almost any U.S. investment real estate can be like-kind replacement property for another type of U.S. investment real estate.
- Full deferral generally calls for equal-or-greater value, all equity reinvested, and debt replaced.
- DSTs can qualify as replacement property and can address passive ownership, debt, and closing-certainty needs.
- Choose the property for income, growth, effort, and diversification goals—not because it resembles the property sold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is replacement property in a 1031 exchange?

It is the like-kind investment real estate acquired to complete the exchange: the asset into which sale proceeds flow through the qualified intermediary so gain on the property sold is deferred. It must be real property held for investment, like-kind to the relinquished property, and acquired within the exchange deadlines.

### What qualifies as like-kind for real estate?

Almost any U.S. real property held for investment or business use is like-kind to any other. Apartments, office, retail, industrial, land, farmland, leaseholds of 30+ years, and fractional interests such as DSTs can qualify and are like-kind to one another. Like-kind refers to investment-real-estate character, not type, grade, or location.

### Can I exchange one property for several?

Yes. A single exchange can be diversified across several replacement properties, subject to identification rules. The 3-property rule allows up to three at any value; the 200% rule allows more if their combined value remains under 200% of what was sold. Many investors trade one property for several passive holdings.

### Does a DST count as replacement property?

Yes. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a qualifying DST beneficial interest as direct real-estate ownership for 1031 purposes, so it can qualify as replacement property. DSTs offer low minimums, fast closings, and pre-arranged financing, which is why they are used as both a primary choice and a backup.

### What's the equal-or-greater-value rule?

To defer full gain, acquire replacement property of equal or greater value than the net sale price and reinvest all equity. Buying down creates taxable boot equal to the shortfall, up to gain. Buying up with additional cash or financing is permitted.

### Do I have to replace my mortgage?

For full deferral, debt paid off is treated like cash received and should be replaced with new debt or offsetting cash. If the replacement carries less debt, the gap is taxable mortgage boot unless additional cash offsets it. A leveraged DST can match old debt through pre-arranged, non-recourse financing.

### Can replacement property be in a different state?

Yes. U.S. real estate is like-kind to other U.S. real estate regardless of state, so an investor can exchange across state lines and diversify nationally. A CPA should check state clawback rules, which can tax previously deferred gain when an out-of-state replacement is later sold without another exchange.

### Can I use replacement property as a vacation home?

Only if it is genuinely held for investment rather than personal use. A personally used property generally does not qualify. Rev. Proc. 2008-16 provides a safe harbor requiring minimum fair-rental days and limited personal use; a true investment rental meeting those tests can qualify, while a personal-use second home cannot.

### Can I build or improve replacement property with exchange funds?

Yes, through an improvement or construction exchange. The QI or exchange accommodation titleholder holds the property while improvements are made and paid for within the 180-day window, and the completed value counts toward the replacement. These exchanges are more complex and require advance planning with the QI.

### How is active replacement different from passive?

Active replacement means owning and operating property directly: full control and upside, but continued management and closing pressure. Passive replacement, such as a DST or triple-net property, lets an investor defer tax while professionals handle operations. Many investors blend both, often anchoring the exchange with a passive option for certainty.

### What if my replacement property is worth less than I sold?

The difference is taxable boot up to realized gain. A partial exchange can still defer the portion reinvested and pay tax on the shortfall, but full deferral requires equal-or-greater value and all equity reinvested. Plan any intentional buy-down with a CPA.

### How do I choose the right replacement property?

Match it to current income versus appreciation, active versus passive ownership, concentration versus diversification, and confidence in closing within 180 days. Because the like-kind field is broad, the work is finding the right fit and getting it closed on time, not merely finding something that qualifies.

### When must I identify replacement property?

Within 45 days of the sale, by written notice that unambiguously describes each candidate, is signed by the exchanger, and is delivered to the QI. After day 45, no property can be added or changed, so identify a primary and at least one backup—ideally a fast-closing DST.

## Glossary

**Replacement Property:** Like-kind investment real estate acquired to complete a 1031 exchange.

**Relinquished Property:** The property sold in the exchange.

**Like-Kind:** The nature-or-character standard under which nearly all U.S. investment real estate can qualify.

**Held for Investment:** Kept for income or appreciation, not resale or personal use.

**Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** Requirement to acquire replacement value at least equal to net sale price for full deferral.

**Boot:** Cash or other non-like-kind value received, taxable up to realized gain.

**Mortgage Boot:** Taxable gain from replacing less debt than was paid off, unless cash offsets it.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A securitized fractional interest in institutional real estate that can qualify as replacement property under Rev. Rul. 2004-86.

**Triple-Net (NNN) Lease:** A lease under which the tenant pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance, creating largely passive ownership.

**Improvement Exchange:** A structure using exchange funds to build or renovate replacement property within 180 days.

**3-Property Rule:** Identification method allowing up to three replacement properties at any value.

**200% Rule:** Identification method allowing any number of properties with combined value up to 200% of the relinquished property.

**Net Sale Price:** Gross sale price less selling costs; the measure for the equal-or-greater-value target.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** Financing secured only by the property, common in DSTs, that can replace an exchanger’s prior loan.

**Suitability Review:** Assessment of whether a securities product such as a DST is appropriate for a particular investor.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds proceeds and documents the exchange so the taxpayer does not receive the funds.
