# How to Analyze a REIT's Balance Sheet

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read**

I keep seeing investors start a REIT discussion with the buildings, the tenant list, or the yield. Those matter. The balance sheet often decides whether those good things can survive a difficult market.

A REIT can own excellent properties and still be a poor investment if its financial footing is fragile. REITs use debt to fund real estate and pay out most of their income, so they cannot easily stockpile retained cash. Too much leverage, debt maturing into an unfriendly market, or development commitments that cannot be funded comfortably can force a dilutive equity raise, a distressed asset sale, or a distribution cut even when the real estate is sound.

First-order thinking sees the current leverage ratio or the current distribution. Second-order thinking asks what happens when a maturity arrives, rates are higher, credit is tight, and a pipeline still needs cash. This is educational information, not investment advice; verify current data and your circumstances with your advisors. The amount of disclosure available also varies with a REIT's structure, as the [comprehensive REIT primer](reits-guide.html) explains.

## Key Balance-Sheet Lines

Start with the assets and the financing that supports them. On the asset side, real estate is usually shown as gross real estate assets, the total cost, and net real estate after accumulated depreciation. GAAP depreciation can leave net book value well below market value, which is why an analyst also estimates net asset value, or NAV, separately. That is the approach behind [how non-traded REITs are valued](how-non-traded-reits-are-valued.html). Cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments are the most liquid assets.

On the financing side, read total debt, preferred equity, and common equity together. Separate mortgages secured by specific properties from unsecured corporate bonds and credit lines. Separate fixed-rate debt from floating-rate debt, because floating-rate debt exposes the REIT to rising interest cost. Preferred equity sits between debt and common equity: it carries a fixed dividend that ranks ahead of the common distribution.

Those lines reveal the capital structure—what the REIT owns, how it is financed, and where claims rank. They are the starting point, not the finish line.

## Leverage & Liquidity Checks

The common leverage measure is net debt-to-EBITDA: total debt less cash, divided by EBITDA, an operating-cash-flow proxy. It expresses roughly how many years of current earnings it would take to repay debt. Lower ratios generally indicate a more conservative balance sheet; higher ratios indicate more financial risk.

Read it with debt-to-gross-assets and [the fixed-charge coverage ratio and other leverage and coverage metrics](reit-leverage-and-debt-coverage-metrics.html). Fixed-charge coverage asks how comfortably operating income covers interest and preferred dividends. No single ratio is enough.

Liquidity is the other half. It is cash on hand plus unused capacity on the revolving credit facility, or revolver. That cushion lets a REIT fund operations, cover maturing debt, meet near-term commitments, and pursue opportunities without being forced into a bad equity raise or asset sale. High leverage paired with thin liquidity is a weak combination when markets tighten.

Two REITs can own the same buildings and face very different outcomes in a downturn. The difference is often leverage and liquidity written on the balance sheet.

## Debt Maturity Profile

The maturity profile shows when principal comes due. It is easy to miss because headline leverage can look moderate while a large block of debt is scheduled to mature in a short period. Since a REIT pays out most of its income, it often must refinance rather than repay the debt from retained cash.

A large maturity that arrives when rates are high or credit markets are tight can mean sharply higher borrowing costs, restrictive terms, or limited access to financing. A laddered maturity schedule spreads debt in roughly even amounts across many years, reducing the chance that the REIT must enter the market all at once. Concentrated near-term maturities—the maturity wall, often in the next year or two—do the opposite.

Check weighted-average maturity, the share due in the next one to three years, and whether a single year is overloaded. Maturity timing is a refinancing risk that leverage alone can hide.

### Key Takeaways

- Read gross and net real estate assets, cash and liquidity, total debt by secured/unsecured and fixed/floating type, and preferred equity.
- Use net debt-to-EBITDA and coverage ratios for leverage, and cash plus available revolver capacity for liquidity.
- Prefer laddered, well-spread maturities to concentrated near-term obligations.
- Weigh committed development spending and funding needs with leverage, coverage, maturities, and liquidity in one judgment.

## Development Pipeline Risk

A development pipeline can create future NOI at attractive yields. It also creates committed multi-year spending that standard leverage ratios do not fully capture. That obligation may sit off the balance sheet or only partly on it, but it still must be funded from cash flow, the revolver, new debt, asset sales, or equity.

The pressure increases when remaining spend is large relative to liquidity and cash flow, uses floating-rate construction debt, or depends on raising capital later. If markets tighten before projects are funded and leased, the REIT can be overextended: it must keep financing half-finished projects while its access to financing narrows. Cost overruns, delays, and lease-up risk—the space fills later or at lower rents—can add to the problem.

Assess the committed spend remaining, the intended source of funding, and the size of the commitment relative to liquidity. A large, underfunded pipeline meaningfully increases balance-sheet risk.

## Putting It Together

No one measure tells the whole story. A REIT with moderate leverage, a near-term maturity wall, and a large underfunded pipeline can be riskier than one with somewhat higher leverage but laddered maturities, ample liquidity, and a modest pipeline.

The useful synthesis is a stress test. If rates rose and credit tightened, could the REIT refinance near-term debt, fund development commitments, and sustain its distribution without a dilutive raise or distressed sale? Low-to-moderate leverage, strong coverage, laddered maturities, ample liquidity, and a manageable pipeline are the ingredients of resilience. Compare the result with sector peers and the REIT's own history, because different property types can support different amounts of debt.

The real test is not how the balance sheet looks today. It is whether it holds up if rates spike and credit freezes.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Book value can mislead. GAAP carries real estate at historical cost less depreciation, so book equity can be understated and a REIT can look more leveraged than it is. NAV-based and cash-flow measures such as net debt-to-EBITDA are generally more useful than simple debt-to-book-equity measures.

Do not stop at total debt. A reasonable leverage ratio can conceal a maturity wall. Do not overlook floating-rate debt, whose interest expense can rise quietly with rates; off-balance-sheet obligations and joint-venture debt, which may not appear fully on the REIT's own balance sheet; or an underfunded pipeline. Judge leverage in the right property-sector context, and consider the cost, rate, structure, and timing of debt rather than its amount alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does a REIT's balance sheet matter?

It shows the financial risk behind the real estate. A REIT can own excellent properties but still produce a poor outcome if borrowing is excessive, maturities arrive into a poor market, or development commitments cannot be funded. A strong balance sheet gives flexibility to weather downturns, refinance on workable terms, and pursue opportunities. It complements property-level analysis; it is not optional.

### What are the key lines on a REIT's balance sheet?

Read gross and net real estate, remembering that GAAP depreciation can understate value and NAV is estimated separately; cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments; total debt split by secured/unsecured and fixed/floating; and preferred and common equity. Together they describe the assets, financing, and ranking of claims.

### What leverage ratios should I use for a REIT?

Start with net debt-to-EBITDA—total debt less cash divided by EBITDA—to gauge debt against earnings power. Add debt-to-gross-assets, fixed-charge coverage, and sometimes debt-to-NAV. Lower net debt-to-EBITDA and higher coverage are generally safer, but sector norms, debt cost, and debt structure matter. A property type with stable, long leases may support more leverage than another.

### What is a REIT's liquidity and why does it matter?

Liquidity is cash plus undrawn revolver capacity. Because REITs distribute most of their income and rely on outside capital, liquidity lets them fund operations, cover maturing debt, finish development, and act without a bad equity raise or distressed sale. Compare it with near-term maturities and committed pipeline spending. Thin liquidity alongside high leverage is a warning.

### What is a debt maturity profile?

It is the schedule of principal maturing in each future year. A REIT may have moderate overall leverage yet face pressure if too much debt must be refinanced in a short window. Review weighted-average maturity, the amount due in the next one to three years, and any overloaded year. Laddering is generally healthier than a concentrated near-term maturity wall.

### What is refinancing risk for a REIT?

It is the risk that maturing debt must be refinanced on poor terms, or cannot be refinanced, because rates, credit conditions, or the REIT's finances have worsened. Higher cost can pressure FFO and distributions; in severe conditions it can lead to asset sales or dilution. Laddered maturities, longer fixed-rate debt, liquidity, and moderate leverage reduce the risk.

### What is the difference between laddered and concentrated maturities?

Laddered maturities spread manageable amounts across many years, so the REIT does not need to refinance a large balance at once. Concentrated maturities put a large share into a short period, often the next year or two, creating a maturity wall. A long weighted-average maturity and a well-spread schedule are reassuring even when headline leverage is only one part of the analysis.

### How does development pipeline risk affect a REIT?

Development can add future NOI, but it requires committed spending over years. Funding can come from cash flow, a revolver, debt, asset sales, or equity. Large commitments relative to liquidity, floating-rate construction financing, future fundraising needs, cost overruns, delays, and lease-up risk can overextend a REIT if markets tighten. A modest, well-funded pipeline is far less concerning than a large, underfunded one.

### What is net debt-to-EBITDA for a REIT?

It divides net debt—total debt less cash and equivalents—by EBITDA, or earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization, used as a proxy for operating cash flow. The result approximates years of current earnings needed to repay debt. Lower generally signals a more conservative balance sheet; higher can mean greater sensitivity to rising rates, refinancing difficulty, or lower income. Read it with coverage, liquidity, maturities, sector norms, and history.

### Where can I find a REIT's balance-sheet details?

The 10-Q quarterly and 10-K annual filings on the SEC's EDGAR database provide the balance sheet and debt notes, including assets, cash, debt, preferred and common equity, type, rate, and maturity. The quarterly operating and financial supplemental in the REIT's investor-relations section often provides the full maturity schedule, secured/unsecured and fixed/floating mix, reported leverage and coverage, available liquidity, and pipeline commitments. Earnings releases and presentations can summarize the figures.

### Does book value reflect a REIT's true asset value?

Not usually. Historical cost less accumulated depreciation can put net book value well below actual market value. That can overstate leverage when measured against book equity. Analysts often use NAV, an estimate of property market value less debt, and cash-flow measures such as net debt-to-EBITDA. NAV often applies capitalization rates to property income, so it remains an estimate rather than a precise figure.

### What are common mistakes in analyzing a REIT's balance sheet?

Common mistakes include relying on book value, ignoring maturity timing, missing floating-rate exposure, overlooking off-balance-sheet and joint-venture debt, understating an underfunded development pipeline, judging leverage without sector context, and focusing on debt amount while ignoring its cost and structure. Read the notes, maturity schedule, and pipeline, then compare with the right peers.

### How do I judge whether a REIT's balance sheet is strong?

Combine leverage, fixed-charge coverage, maturities, liquidity, and pipeline commitments rather than treating them in isolation. Stress-test whether the REIT could refinance, fund commitments, and sustain distributions if rates rise and credit tightens without dilution or distressed sales. Moderate leverage, solid coverage, laddered maturities, ample liquidity, and a manageable pipeline support resilience. Peer and historical comparisons add context.

## Glossary

**Real Estate Assets:** A REIT's properties, shown gross and net of depreciation.

**Net Asset Value (NAV):** Estimated market value of properties less debt, per share.

**Total Debt:** All of a REIT's borrowings, secured and unsecured.

**Secured Debt:** Mortgages pledged against specific properties.

**Unsecured Debt:** Corporate bonds and credit lines not pledged to assets.

**Floating-Rate Debt:** Debt whose interest cost rises and falls with rates.

**Preferred Equity:** Equity ranking ahead of common, with a fixed dividend.

**Net Debt-to-EBITDA:** Net debt divided by operating cash flow — a leverage gauge.

**Fixed-Charge Coverage:** How well income covers interest and preferred dividends.

**Liquidity:** Cash plus undrawn revolver capacity for near-term needs.

**Revolver:** A revolving credit facility a REIT can draw on as needed.

**Debt Maturity Profile:** The schedule of when a REIT's debt comes due.

**Laddered Maturities:** Debt spread evenly across years to reduce refinancing risk.

**Refinancing Risk:** The risk of refinancing maturing debt on poor terms.

**Development Pipeline:** Projects under construction requiring committed future spend.

**EBITDA:** Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.
