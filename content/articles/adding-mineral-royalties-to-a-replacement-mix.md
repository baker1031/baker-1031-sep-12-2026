# Adding Mineral Royalties to a 1031 Replacement Mix


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Mineral & Royalty Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 13 min read

I have been thinking about how quickly an attractive distribution number can turn a small diversifier into the whole decision. First-order thinking sees a 9 to 10% target and wants more of it. Second-order thinking asks what happens to portfolio income when commodity prices fall, wells decline, and an illiquid position has grown past its intended role.

A royalty interest can carry a higher headline yield than a core real-estate DST, and depletion can shelter part of that income. Used as a small sleeve in a diversified replacement mix, it may lift portfolio income without making the entire exchange a commodity wager.

Most 1031 exchangers replace one building with another or move into a Delaware Statutory Trust and stop there. Mineral and royalty interests are often ignored simply because owners have not been told that oil and gas royalties can be like-kind real property. They can. A royalty interest offers higher headline income than a stabilized real-estate DST, while percentage depletion can shelter part of the income from tax. The trade-off is more variable income than rent. For that reason, the usual fit is not a full allocation but a small, deliberate sleeve within a diversified replacement mix. This article explains how the sleeve works, how to size it, and how it fits within the 45- and 180-day deadlines.

## Key Takeaways

- Mineral and royalty interests in real property can be like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, allowing exchange proceeds to go into a royalty program without tax at sale.
- A royalty interest bears no drilling or operating costs. It can therefore offer a higher headline yield than a comparable working interest, with target distributions often in the 9 to 10% range versus 5 to 6% for stabilized real-estate DSTs.
- Percentage depletion is generally 15% of gross income, subject to limits. It can shelter part of royalty income, can exceed original cost basis over time, and investors generally take the larger of cost or percentage depletion each year.
- Commodity prices and well decline make royalty income more variable than rent. That is the reason to treat it as a sleeve rather than a core holding.
- A royalty interest is passive and bears no costs. A working interest is non-passive, bears operating and intangible drilling costs, and carries substantially more risk. Royalty is generally the cleaner replacement-mix fit.
- Mineral and royalty programs are speculative, illiquid securities sold only to verified accredited investors through a private placement memorandum. Commodity-price and geologic risk can cause loss of principal.

## Why a 1031 exchanger would look at royalties at all

An exchanger selling an appreciated building can have a difficult problem: substantial capital but too few replacement choices that improve the total portfolio. Another apartment building can replace one set of tenant, vacancy, and capital-expenditure concerns with the same concerns elsewhere. Putting all gain into one DST concentrates outcome in one sponsor and one asset. Royalties address a narrower question: whether the replacement portfolio can generate more income from an asset class that behaves differently from rent.

The first surprise is eligibility. Mineral and royalty interests in real property can qualify as like-kind real property under Section 1031. In most states, the right to receive a share of production from minerals in the ground is a real-property interest. It can sit on the same identification list as a fee-simple building or a [DST](dst-guide.html). An apartment building and a royalty interest do not appear intuitive together, but the real-property like-kind test is broad enough for royalties. State characterization and structure both matter, so confirm the treatment for your facts with your CPA and qualified intermediary.

The other draw is income, with a possible tax shelter. Royalty distributions tend to be higher than stabilized real-estate yields, and percentage depletion offsets part of annual income for tax purposes. An exchanger seeking more current cash flow, while accepting that the cash flow will move around, has a reason to evaluate the option. The [mineral and royalty 1031 guide](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html) covers the eligibility question in greater depth.

## What a royalty sleeve is, and why income drives the case

A “sleeve” is a small, bounded position within a broader replacement mix, sized so a poor year in that asset class cannot sink the portfolio. The distinction matters. A core allocation is something an investor is prepared to live and die with; a sleeve is a satellite. It is large enough to influence blended income and small enough for the rest of the portfolio to dilute its volatility.

Royalties deserve sleeve treatment because income is more variable than rent. A net-lease building pays the same contracted amount each month. A royalty check moves with two forces outside the owner’s control: commodity price and natural well decline. A large royalty allocation makes portfolio income track oil and gas prices. A small sleeve can capture higher yield and the depletion shelter while the bulk remains in real estate with contractual rent.

This is ordinary diversification logic. The aim is not to make royalties the portfolio’s engine. It is to use a contained amount to lift blended yield and add income that does not move with the real-estate cycle. Sizing supplies the discipline.

The initial economic attraction is headline yield. A royalty owner receives a share of gross production revenue but does not pay for drilling, equipment, labor, or ongoing lifting costs. The operator funds those items. The royalty owner receives a share from the top, so the yield on royalty capital is higher than a working interest in the same well and often higher than stabilized real estate.

In rough terms, royalty programs often target current distributions of 9 to 10%, compared with 5 to 6% for a stabilized real-estate DST. Those figures are illustrative, not promised. The royalty number is a target, not a contract, and it changes with prices and production; real-estate income comes from contractual rent and is steadier. The gap is the point: a modest sleeve above a real-estate core can raise blended cash yield by a meaningful fraction of a percentage point over a hold.

The higher yield is not free. It compensates for commodity-price risk, geologic risk, and the certainty that production from any well declines over time. The income and risk cases are inseparable, which is why the position should stay small. See [why mineral royalties yield more](why-mineral-royalties-yield-more.html) for a fuller explanation of the yield.

## The depletion shelter

Royalty income can be attractive because not all of it is taxed as it arrives. The depletion allowance recognizes that the mineral resource is being used up. There are two methods. **Cost depletion** recovers actual basis over the producing life of wells in proportion to annual reserve production. **Percentage depletion** is a statutory deduction, generally 15% of gross income from the property, subject to limits.

Percentage depletion is based on gross income rather than remaining basis. It can therefore continue each year and, over a long enough holding period, total depletion may exceed the original cost of the interest. Cost depletion cannot exceed basis; it ends after basis is recovered. Investors generally use the larger amount each year, often the percentage method for royalty owners. Limits apply, the rules are detailed, and actual benefit depends on the investor’s facts and return. The [oil and gas depletion allowance](oil-gas-depletion-allowance.html) explains the mechanics.

In a replacement mix, that means some royalty-sleeve distributions may have less current tax drag than an equivalent dollar of rent. This can improve after-tax income relative to headline yield. It does not alter underlying risk or guarantee a deduction; limits can cap the benefit and no depletion deduction is assured. It is, however, a reason the sleeve can contribute more after tax than its size suggests.

## The risk that argues for sizing it small

Every attraction of a royalty interest has a matching risk, and stating them plainly keeps the sleeve honest. **Commodity price** comes first. Royalty income is revenue, and revenue is volume times price. When oil or gas prices fall, the royalty check falls as well, immediately and without a lease to cushion the decline. A real-estate tenant still owes contractual rent in a downturn. A royalty owner does not have that protection.

**Well decline** is second. Oil and gas wells usually produce most in early months and then decline along a curve—often sharply at first for shale wells and then more gradually. A fixed group of wells is naturally a depleting income stream; without new drilling, income tends to decline. A program’s value depends materially on how it models decline and whether its acreage includes future drilling opportunities.

Third is **concentration**. A royalty position tied to one basin, operator, or handful of wells has risks a diversified program can spread. An operator bankruptcy, basin takeaway constraint, or regulatory change can hit a narrow position hard. Commodity price, decline, and concentration are why royalty exposure belongs as a measured sleeve rather than a core position. [Oil and gas investment risks](oil-gas-investment-risks.html) sets out the broader risk picture.

## How to size the sleeve in a diversified mix

Sizing is the discipline. The objective is a position large enough to lift blended income and add a differentiated stream, but small enough that a weak commodity year does not break overall cash flow. There is no universal percentage. The appropriate amount depends on income needs, risk tolerance, and the rest of the replacement mix. A useful question is how much income variability the investor can absorb, then working back to a sleeve size.

Consider an illustration. An investor exchanges $2,000,000 of proceeds. The $1,700,000 core goes into stabilized real-estate DSTs yielding about 5.5%. A $300,000 royalty sleeve—roughly 15% of the mix—targets about 9.5%. Blended target yield moves from 5.5% to about 6.1%, and depletion can improve the after-tax figure. If royalty income fell by a third in a weak price year, the effect on total portfolio income would be roughly two percentage points of the sleeve’s contribution, not a portfolio-wide shock. That containment supports keeping the sleeve modest rather than doubling it to chase yield.

Two guardrails help. First, size the sleeve by the income movement the investor can absorb, not by a desired headline yield. Second, diversify inside the sleeve across basins, operators, and ideally well vintages, so the sleeve itself is not a single-operator or single-play bet. A concentrated sleeve defeats its purpose.

## Fitting royalties into the 1031

A royalty sleeve follows the same mechanics as any replacement property. Proceeds from the relinquished sale go to a qualified intermediary, never to the investor, and the royalty interest is identified in writing within 45 days. It can share an identification list with a building or DST. Mixing asset types is permitted when each is like-kind real property and described unambiguously. With a securitized royalty program, identify the specific offering and the dollar amount to be acquired.

The 45- and 180-day clocks operate as in every exchange. A royalty sleeve often appears beside larger real-estate replacement property on the identification list. That is practical because securitized royalty programs, like DSTs, can usually close through a subscription rather than a negotiated acquisition. The ability to close quickly can make the sleeve useful when the main replacement is a slower building purchase. Review the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) for identification mechanics and timing.

Structure is material to eligibility. Many investable royalty programs are securitized real-property interests built to qualify for 1031 treatment, frequently through a trust or similar arrangement, and are offered only to accredited investors by private placement memorandum. The like-kind property must be the mineral or royalty real-property interest, not a partnership interest or other security that fails the real-property test. Confirm the program’s structure with the CPA and QI before identifying it.

## A diligence checklist

Program quality is largely the quality of its answers to a short list of questions. Work through these before identifying a position.

- **Sponsor track record.** Who assembled the acreage, how long have they operated, and how have earlier programs performed through a price cycle? Mineral aggregation is specialized work.
- **Basins and diversification.** Is income spread across several basins and plays, or concentrated in one? Geographic and geologic dispersion reduces single-point failure.
- **Operators.** Who drills and produces on the acreage, and how creditworthy are they? A royalty owner depends on operators to produce.
- **Decline and price modeling.** How does the program model well decline and commodity prices, and under which price assumptions does the target distribution hold? Aggressive price decks can flatter yield.
- **Future drilling.** Is income limited to producing wells, or does the acreage include undeveloped locations that can add production? This affects whether income only declines or can be replenished.
- **Fees and structure.** What does the sponsor charge, and does the legal structure qualify the interest as like-kind real property?
- **Liquidity and hold.** These are illiquid, long-hold positions without a public market. Confirm the ability to hold for the full term.

If a program cannot clearly answer its price-assumption and decline questions, that itself is an answer. Brochure yield is only as credible as the assumptions beneath it.

## Royalty versus working interest inside a replacement mix

The key oil-and-gas distinction is royalty interest versus working interest. For a replacement mix, the answer usually favors royalty. A **royalty interest** provides a share of production revenue and carries none of the cost of drilling or operating a well. It is passive income for tax purposes, with no operating control or liability. A **working interest** is the operating stake: it receives revenue but bears a proportional share of drilling costs, lifting costs, and intangible drilling costs. It is non-passive and brings operating decisions and much greater downside.

| Feature | Royalty Interest | Working Interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bears drilling/operating costs | No | Yes, proportional share plus IDCs |
| Headline yield | Higher, no cost burden | Lower net, after costs |
| Tax character of income | Passive | Non-passive |
| Control over operations | None | Operating decisions and liability |
| Primary risks | Commodity price, decline, depletion | All royalty risks plus cost overruns and operating risk |
| Fit for a replacement sleeve | Cleaner, passive, sized small | Heavier, riskier, rarely a sleeve |

General comparison for educational purposes. Both interests carry commodity-price and geologic risk and can lose principal.

For an exchanger building a sleeve, royalty is usually the better side of the comparison. It supplies the higher headline yield, depletion shelter, and passive treatment without cost-bearing exposure or operating risk. The full comparison is available in [working interest versus royalty interest](working-interest-vs-royalty-interest.html).

## How it fits the portfolio, and who it suits

The sleeve only makes sense in light of what surrounds it. A small royalty position can add income that does not move with the real-estate cycle. When rents soften in a recession, royalty income follows oil and gas prices instead. That is what a satellite position is intended to add. It will not always help—prices and rents can fall together—but the two streams do not normally move in lockstep, and a blend can be steadier than either stream alone in some environments.

The other side is direct commodity exposure, which the real-estate core does not have. It can be acceptable while the position is small and the core remains anchored. It becomes a problem when strong royalty checks tempt an investor to let the sleeve exceed its role. Rebalancing matters: a sleeve whose income share grew because prices spiked has quietly become a bet. Keep it in its intended range.

A royalty sleeve may suit an exchanger who wants more current income, has a diversified real-estate core, and can accept that a portion of income moves with commodity prices. It may suit an investor who values depletion and understands the asset is depleting, illiquid, accredited-only, and held for years. It works as a deliberate satellite, not a reach for yield.

It does not suit an investor who needs predictable contractual income for monthly living expenses; royalty checks are neither contractual nor predictable. It also does not suit someone likely to oversize after a good year, or unable to tolerate illiquidity and the possibility of principal loss in a sustained price downturn. It is unavailable to non-accredited investors, since programs are offered only to verified accredited investors through private placement memorandum. A small, variable, tax-favored income stream over a stable core may be worth discussing with advisers. If that uncertainty would cause lost sleep, this is not the right tool.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I 1031 exchange into mineral or royalty interests?

Often, yes. Mineral and royalty interests in real property can qualify as like-kind real property under Section 1031, allowing building-sale proceeds to be exchanged into a qualifying royalty interest. Treatment is fact-specific and depends on state characterization and program structure, so confirm with a CPA and qualified intermediary.

### Why are royalty yields higher than real-estate DST yields?

A royalty owner receives a share of gross production revenue but pays no drilling or operating costs. This can yield more than real estate or a working interest. Royalty programs often target 9 to 10% distributions against 5 to 6% for stabilized real-estate DSTs. The higher yield compensates for commodity-price and decline risk, and the target is not guaranteed.

### What is the depletion allowance and how does it help?

Depletion lets an oil and gas owner deduct part of income as the resource is used up. Percentage depletion is generally 15% of gross property income, subject to limits, and may exceed original cost basis over time. Cost depletion recovers actual basis. Investors generally use the larger amount each year, sheltering part of royalty income from current tax. No depletion deduction is guaranteed.

### How much of a replacement portfolio should go into royalties?

There is no fixed figure. The principle is a small sleeve, not a core holding, so a weak commodity year cannot break portfolio income. Size the position by income variability the investor can absorb, anchor the mix with diversified real estate, and diversify the sleeve across basins and operators. Discuss the appropriate size with an adviser.

### What is the difference between a royalty interest and a working interest?

A royalty interest receives production revenue, has no drilling or operating costs, is passive, and has no operating control. A working interest bears drilling and operating costs, including intangible drilling costs, is non-passive, and carries operating risk and liability. For a replacement sleeve, royalty is the cleaner and lower-risk choice.

### How does a royalty interest fit the 45 and 180-day deadlines?

It follows the same rules as other replacement property. Proceeds go to a QI; the royalty interest is named on written identification within 45 days; and the position closes within 180 days. Securitized royalty programs can often close quickly through subscription, allowing them to share an identification list with a slower building purchase.

### Are royalty programs risky?

Yes. They are speculative, illiquid securities offered only to verified accredited investors by private placement memorandum. Commodity-price risk, well decline, reserve depletion, and operator risk can cause loss of principal. A small sleeve inside a diversified mix is one way investors manage the exposure, but it does not remove the risk.

## Glossary

**Royalty Interest:** A right to a share of production revenue from minerals, free of drilling and operating costs. Passive income with no operating control.

**Working Interest:** An operating stake in an oil and gas property that shares revenue but also bears its proportional drilling and operating costs. Non-passive and higher risk.

**Replacement Property:** The like-kind real property acquired in a 1031 exchange to defer gain on the relinquished property. Mineral and royalty interests can qualify.

**Depletion Allowance:** A deduction that reflects the using up of a mineral resource, reducing taxable oil and gas income. Available as cost or percentage depletion.

**Percentage Depletion:** A statutory depletion method, generally 15% of gross income from the property, subject to limits. Can exceed original cost basis over time.

**Diversification Sleeve:** A small, bounded satellite position inside a larger portfolio, sized so its volatility cannot dominate the whole.

**Decline Curve:** The pattern by which a well's production falls over time, steep early then flattening, which makes a fixed set of wells a depleting income stream.

**Net Revenue Interest:** An owner's share of production revenue after royalties and other burdens are deducted, expressing the actual revenue entitlement from a property.
