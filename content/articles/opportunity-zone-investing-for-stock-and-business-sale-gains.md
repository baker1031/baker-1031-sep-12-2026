# Opportunity Zone Investing for Stock & Business-Sale Gains

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

<div class="article-meta"><span class="cat">Opportunity Zone Funds</span> Baker 1031 Research &nbsp;&middot;&nbsp; Updated June 2026 &nbsp;&middot;&nbsp; 16 min read</div>

I keep seeing the same issue around a planned stock or business sale: the conversation starts with the tax bill and ends before anyone has asked whether the replacement investment stands on its own. First-order thinking sees a way to defer a gain. Second-order thinking starts with the 180-day clock, the character of the gain, the long hold, illiquidity, and development risk.

<div class="article-intro"><p style="margin:0;">Investors with stock, business-sale, or cryptocurrency gains can't use a 1031 exchange — but they can use an Opportunity Zone. This guide explains why 1031 won't work for stock gains, reinvesting equity gains in a QOF, business-sale and M&A gains, crypto and other capital gains, and 180-day planning for sellers.</p></div>

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">Most tax-deferral discussions for real estate investors center on the 1031 exchange — but the 1031 has a major limitation: it only works for real estate gains. Investors with gains from selling stock, a business, cryptocurrency, or other non-real-estate assets can't use a 1031 to defer them. This is where Opportunity Zones matter: an OZ investment can defer (and potentially grow tax-free) virtually any capital gain, including stock, business-sale, and crypto gains. For a tech employee with appreciated shares, a founder selling a company, or a crypto investor with large gains, OZs can open a tax-deferral option that the 1031 does not. This guide covers why 1031 won't work for stock gains, reinvesting equity gains in a QOF, business-sale and M&amp;A gains, crypto and other capital gains, and 180-day planning for sellers. Note that OZ rules are time-sensitive and evolving — verify the current rules with your tax advisor.</p>
<h2 id="why-1031-wont-work">Why 1031 won't work for stock gains</h2>
<p>The <a href="1031-exchange-guide.html">1031 exchange</a> requires like-kind real estate. It defers tax only on the sale of real property held for investment or business and reinvested in like-kind real property. It applies exclusively to real estate: you must sell real estate and buy real estate.</p>
<p>Stock, business interests, cryptocurrency, and other non-real-estate assets are not real property, so their gains do not qualify for a 1031. You cannot 1031 stock into real estate, or stock into stock; the 2017 tax law limited 1031s to real property. An investor with a stock gain therefore has no 1031 option.</p>
<p>That leaves non-real-estate gains outside the 1031's deferral, which is the gap <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">Opportunity Zone</a>s fill. The 1031 is real-estate-only; OZs can address a different set of capital gains.</p>
<h2 id="reinvesting-equity">Reinvesting equity gains in a QOF</h2>
<p>Opportunity Zones let an investor reinvest an equity or stock gain into a QOF to defer it, an option a 1031 does not provide. If you sell appreciated stock or other securities and realize a capital gain, you can invest that gain into a QOF within 180 days to defer it and earn the OZ benefits: deferral and tax-free growth after 10 years. An equity gain can be redirected into an OZ investment.</p>
<p>This can matter for a concentrated or large stock position: a tech employee with appreciated company shares, an investor with a long-held winning stock, or anyone facing a large capital-gains bill from selling securities. Rather than paying the tax or staying concentrated to avoid it, the investor can sell, defer the gain via an OZ, and diversify into the OZ investment. That is a tax-deferral and diversification tool the 1031 cannot provide for securities.</p>
<div class="pullquote"><p>For a tech employee sitting on appreciated company stock, or anyone facing a big gain from selling securities, the Opportunity Zone is often the only tax-deferral tool available — the 1031 simply does not apply.</p></div>
<h2 id="business-sale">Business-sale and M&amp;A gains</h2>
<p>Business-sale and M&amp;A gains are another major OZ use case because a 1031 generally cannot defer them. When an owner sells a business or business interests, or in an M&amp;A transaction, the capital-gain portion can generally be invested in a QOF to defer it. A founder or owner selling a company can use an OZ for the capital-gain portion of the sale.</p>
<p>The nuance is important: a business sale may have both capital-gain and ordinary-income components, including <a href="depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html">depreciation recapture</a> or <a href="investing-section-1231-gains-in-an-opportunity-zone.html">gains on business-use property under Section 1231</a>. Only the capital-gain portion qualifies for the OZ, and a CPA determines that split. For a large business-sale capital gain, an OZ can defer, and potentially grow tax-free, a significant amount. It remains a tool the 1031 cannot provide for founders and owners exiting.</p>
<h2 id="crypto-other">Crypto and other capital gains</h2>
<p>Cryptocurrency and other capital gains can also qualify for OZ investment. If you sell appreciated cryptocurrency held as a capital asset and realize a capital gain, you can <a href="investing-crypto-and-stock-gains-in-an-opportunity-zone.html">invest that gain into a QOF</a> within 180 days to defer it. Crypto investors with large gains therefore have an OZ deferral option; the 1031 cannot defer crypto.</p>
<p>Gains from collectibles, certain other investment assets, or any capital asset can generally be invested in a QOF as long as they are capital gains. This extends the OZ to a wide range of capital gains beyond stock and business sales. It does not change the requirement to establish the gain's capital character and timing with a CPA.</p>
<div class="takeaways"><div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>The 1031 only works for real estate gains — stock, business-sale, crypto, and other non-real-estate gains can't use it.</li>
<li>OZs let investors defer (and potentially grow tax-free) virtually any capital gain, filling the gap the 1031 leaves.</li>
<li>Key use cases: stock/equity gains (concentrated positions), business-sale and M&amp;A gains (the capital-gain portion), and crypto and other capital gains.</li>
<li>180-day planning is essential for sellers — plan the QOF investment around the sale to meet the window.</li>
</ul></div>
<h2 id="180-day-planning">180-day planning for sellers</h2>
<p>Sellers of stock, businesses, or other assets should plan around the 180-day window. You have 180 days from realizing the gain to invest it into a QOF, so suitable QOFs and the investment process should be considered before the window becomes tight.</p>
<p>For business sellers, coordinate the sale, the gain's realization, any pass-through gain flexibility, and the QOF investment plan. For stock sellers, the sale date starts the clock, with <a href="investing-a-partnership-or-k-1-gain-in-a-qof.html">pass-through flexibility if the gain comes through an entity</a>. Map the 180-day period from the applicable start date and line up the QOF investment. Advance planning with an advisor and CPA reduces the risk of missing the window or rushing a fund selection.</p>
<h2 id="audience-expansion">Who benefits most</h2>
<p>OZ investing for non-real-estate gains serves an audience the 1031 cannot: tech employees and executives with appreciated company stock, including RSUs, options, and ESPP shares; founders and business owners selling companies; crypto investors with substantial digital-asset gains; and holders of concentrated positions seeking to diversify without an immediate tax hit.</p>
<p>For these investors, OZs expand the tax-deferral audience beyond real estate investors to anyone with a significant capital gain from a non-real-estate asset. They may be uniquely valuable where no 1031 option exists, but that does not erase the need to evaluate the long hold, illiquidity, development risk, and the fact that the original gain is eventually taxed.</p>
