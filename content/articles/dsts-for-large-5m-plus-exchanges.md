# DSTs for Large ($5M+) Exchanges

*Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read*

I keep seeing a familiar impulse when a large property sells: find one impressive replacement and put the full $5 million, $10 million, or more into it. It feels decisive. It also turns decades of tax-deferred wealth into one asset, one market, and one tenant base.

The first-order view is that a large exchange gives an investor more buying power. The second-order reality is that size magnifies concentration risk and makes the identification rules harder to manage. A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can be useful because fractional interests can divide a large exchange among institutional properties, sponsors, sectors, and geographies. That does not make a DST portfolio risk-free, and it does not make it suitable for every investor. ## Challenges of Large Exchanges

Large 1031 exchanges have two problems that smaller exchanges may not feel as sharply.

First is concentration. When $5 million or more goes into one replacement property, a soft local market, a major tenant departure, a sector that falls out of favor, or simple underperformance can affect the whole exchange. There is no second property, region, or operating team to offset the result. For a sophisticated investor, that is usually at odds with the diversification applied elsewhere in a portfolio.

Second is identification-rule management. The three-property rule, the 200% rule, and the 95% rule were not designed as a simple portfolio-construction tool. A large exchanger who wants several replacements must identify enough suitable options to build a diversified set and still remain within the applicable limits. The 200% cap can bind; the three-property count can bind; and an identification error can disqualify the exchange.

That is why a multi-DST approach is often considered. It addresses the investment question—how much should ride on one asset?—and the procedural question—how can several replacements be identified and acquired within the rules?

## Spreading Across Sponsors & Sectors

DSTs allow fractional beneficial interests in income-producing real estate. Instead of buying one large building, an investor may divide a $5 million or $10 million exchange among several DSTs.

The useful dimensions are separate:

- **Sponsors:** Using different sponsors reduces reliance on any one operator's execution, underwriting judgment, and management.
- **Sectors:** Multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, healthcare, and self-storage do not all respond to the same conditions in the same way.
- **Geographies:** Several regional markets mean one local economy is less likely to determine the entire outcome.

An illustrative allocation might include a Southeast multifamily DST, a Midwest industrial DST, a national net-lease retail DST, and a Western healthcare DST, each from a different vetted sponsor. The aim is not to predict a winner. It is to avoid making one property, one region, or one sector responsible for everything. Income may then come from several properties and markets; if one struggles, the others may help offset it.

Putting $10 million into one building is a bet. Spreading it across multifamily, industrial, net-lease, and healthcare DSTs from different sponsors and regions is a portfolio.

## Key Takeaways

- Large exchanges bring both single-property concentration risk and identification-rule constraints.
- Multiple DSTs can diversify a portfolio by sponsor, sector, and geography.
- The 200% rule is commonly the workhorse for a diversified large exchange, but it requires careful value and availability modeling.
- Large investors may sometimes obtain custom or off-market access; the portfolio still needs income, growth, and defensive balance and CPA coordination.

## Managing Identification Rules

The three identification paths have different uses:

| Rule | What it permits | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3-property rule | Up to three replacement properties, regardless of value | Straightforward, but may be too limiting for a broadly diversified DST allocation. |
| 200% rule | Any number of properties with combined value no more than 200% of relinquished-property value | Often the workhorse for a large, multi-DST exchange. |
| 95% rule | Any number and value | At least 95% of identified value must actually be acquired, so it is rarely used. |

For a large exchange, the 200% rule often permits a basket across sponsors and sectors. The hard part is not merely staying below 200% of the relinquished property's value. It is identifying enough DSTs, and allocating enough to each, that a diversified set can actually close if an offering fills or becomes unavailable.

That calls for modeling before the identification is delivered. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) helps facilitate the exchange, the broker-dealer helps structure and evaluate the DST choices, and the CPA confirms values and tax treatment. The QI must be engaged before the relinquished property sells. A botched identification can disqualify the exchange.

## Custom & Off-Market DSTs

Large exchangers may sometimes see options that are not part of the ordinary, broadly marketed lineup. Depending on capital size, sponsor and broker-dealer relationships, and what is available at the time, these can include larger allocations, early or priority access to a new offering, or a more tailored sector emphasis, debt level, or hold profile.

The advantage is usually access and tailoring, not a promise of fundamentally better economics. In a standard offering, stated fees and structure generally apply to all investors. A larger investor may be able to secure a meaningful allocation in an offering that fills quickly, helping build a diversified set within the identification window. None of this is guaranteed.

The diligence remains the same: examine the real estate, sponsor quality, fees, debt, and suitability. Custom or off-market access is a complement to diversified construction, not a substitute for it. The real advantage of scale is the ability to assemble a diversified institutional portfolio, not simply to buy a bigger building.

## Building the Portfolio

This is a construction exercise, not a random scattering of capital. A large DST portfolio can blend several roles:

- An **income sleeve** may emphasize net-lease retail and healthcare properties with stable, long-term leases and projected distributions.
- A **growth sleeve** may include multifamily and industrial properties in markets with demand and rent-growth potential.
- A **defensive sleeve** may favor sectors and locations expected to hold up better through a cycle.

The right mix depends on the investor's goals, risk tolerance, income needs, time horizon, and broader wealth picture. Allocation by sector and market, sponsor selection, and the portfolio's overall leverage all matter. Leveraged and all-cash DSTs have different risk and debt-replacement profiles, so construction and 200% identification planning have to happen together.

The goal is a tax-deferred, passive portfolio with income from many sources—not a claim that the portfolio cannot lose value or that distributions will occur as projected. A diversified allocation can reduce concentration risk; it cannot eliminate real-estate or market risk.

## Weighing the Trade-Offs

Diversification does not erase the cost of going passive. DST interests are illiquid. An investor generally remains invested until the sponsor sells the underlying property, typically after a five-to-seven-year hold, and there is little or no secondary market. In a multi-DST portfolio, sponsors may sell at different times, creating staggered exit decisions.

Distributions are projections, not guarantees, and depend on the underlying properties' performance. Fees apply to each offering, so the cumulative cost of a multi-DST portfolio must be understood. The sponsor makes property-level decisions, including when to sell; the investor cannot direct the assets, choose tenants, or time exits. A broad downturn can still pressure several real-estate sectors at once.

The practical choice is clear-eyed: accept staggered illiquidity, projected income, cumulative fees, and less control in exchange for tax deferral, diversification, passivity, and access to institutional real estate. That can be worthwhile for a suitable investor. It is not a free option.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What challenges do large 1031 exchanges face?

They face concentration risk and identification-rule management. A $5 million, $10 million, or larger exchange concentrated in one replacement leaves all tax-deferred capital exposed to that asset, market, and tenant base. The 200% and three-property limits can also constrain the several replacements needed for diversification. DSTs can spread capital across institutional properties within the rules, but the QI and CPA should confirm the structure.

### How do DSTs help a large exchanger diversify?

Fractional DST interests can divide the proceeds among several sponsors, sectors, and geographies. An investor might combine Southeast multifamily, Midwest industrial, national net-lease, and Western healthcare holdings. Different sponsors reduce operator concentration; different sectors and regions reduce the chance that one property type or local economy drives every result. The broker-dealer helps evaluate a suitable set, while the CPA confirms tax treatment.

### What is the 200% rule and why does it matter for large exchanges?

Within the 45-day identification period, it permits any number of replacement properties if their combined fair-market value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property's value. That can allow more than three DSTs, but it requires modeling the identified values carefully. The investor must allow for offerings that may fill or become unavailable without breaching the cap. The [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) provides wider context.

### Which identification rule should a large exchanger use?

It depends on the intended number of DSTs. The three-property rule can work when three provide sufficient diversification. The 200% rule is usually more useful for a larger basket. The 95% rule permits any number and value but requires the investor to acquire at least 95% of identified value, which is why it is rarely used. Make the decision with the QI and CPA before the relinquished property sells.

### What are custom or off-market DSTs?

They are tailored or non-standard opportunities that may be available to a large exchanger: larger allocations, earlier access, or a sector, debt, or hold profile better aligned to the investor's needs. Their availability depends on relationships and timing. They can help secure allocations in offerings that fill quickly, but the same diligence on property, sponsor, fees, debt, and suitability applies.

### How do I build a diversified DST portfolio from a large exchange?

Start with the job each holding is intended to do: income, growth, or defensiveness. Then decide how much to allocate by sector and market, which sponsors to use, what leverage is appropriate, and how each choice fits the broader wealth picture. A net-lease or healthcare sleeve may be income-oriented; multifamily or industrial may be growth-oriented. Build the plan alongside the identification plan, not afterward.

### Is concentration risk really a problem in a large exchange?

Yes. When one replacement carries $5 million, $10 million, or more, a local-market decline, anchor-tenant departure, sector change, or weak property result has no cushion. The larger the exchange, the more meaningful that exposure becomes. Multiple DSTs can reduce single-asset risk by spreading capital across sponsors, sectors, and regions, though they cannot remove market risk.

### Can a large exchange be split across many different sponsors?

Yes, and sponsor diversification is important. Sponsors differ in properties, management approach, underwriting standards, and track record. Putting the whole exchange with one sponsor relies on that operator's execution. A large exchange can often take meaningful positions with several vetted sponsors while still diversifying by sector and geography. Sponsor quality, experience, and track record should be evaluated, not assumed.

### What are the trade-offs of a large DST portfolio?

The main ones are illiquidity, projected income, cumulative fees, and loss of control. Each DST is typically held until sponsor sale, often five to seven years, so a large portfolio can create several staggered exits. The sponsor controls asset decisions, and distributions depend on the properties. Diversification reduces concentration risk but does not prevent a broad real-estate downturn from affecting several holdings.

### How does leverage work in a large DST exchange?

To fully defer gain, an exchanger generally needs to replace debt on the relinquished property or contribute additional cash. Many DSTs have pre-arranged non-recourse property financing, so a leveraged DST can provide replacement debt without the investor qualifying for or personally guaranteeing a new loan. Leverage can increase risk as well as potential return. A portfolio may blend leveraged and lower-leverage DSTs; the CPA should model the debt need and the broker-dealer can help match offerings.

### Why not just buy one large property with a big exchange?

An investor can. But one asset retains concentration and active ownership responsibilities, or reliance on hired management. A multi-DST portfolio may provide diversification and full passivity and can be assembled within the identification rules. The trade is less liquidity and control, plus fees. The choice turns on whether the investor values a single directly controlled asset more than diversification and passivity.

### Do large exchangers get better DST terms?

Sometimes they get better access or more tailoring: a larger allocation, priority access, or a custom/off-market structure. In a given standard offering, however, the core economics, fees, and structure generally remain the same for every investor. Size does not guarantee better economics. Evaluate every opportunity on its merits and suitability.

### How do I plan the exits for a large DST portfolio?

Plan for an ongoing process. Each sponsor may sell after a defined life, typically five to seven years, and a portfolio's sales may occur at different times. At each sale, the investor may take cash and pay deferred tax, begin another 1031 exchange into DSTs or other like-kind real property, or, where offered, use a 721/UPREIT path into a REIT. Each renewed exchange has its own deadlines, so CPA and broker-dealer planning should begin well before the exit.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.

**Diversified DST Portfolio:** Multiple DSTs spread across sponsors, sectors, and geographies.

**Concentration Risk:** The risk of placing too much capital in one property or asset.

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Relinquished Property:** The large property sold to start a 1031 exchange.

**Replacement Property:** Like-kind real estate, such as DSTs, acquired in the exchange.

**200% Rule:** Identify any number of properties up to 200% of relinquished-property value.

**3-Property Rule:** Identify up to three replacement properties regardless of value.

**95% Rule:** Identify any number, but acquire at least 95% of identified value.

**Sponsor:** The firm that acquires, structures, and manages a DST's property.

**Off-Market DST:** A non-standard or tailored DST offering that is not broadly marketed.

**Net-Lease Property:** A single-tenant property on a long-term lease, often income-oriented.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** DST financing not personally guaranteed by the investor.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds proceeds and facilitates a 1031 exchange.

**721 / UPREIT Exchange:** Contributing DST property to a REIT for OP units while preserving deferral.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Regulation D offerings.
