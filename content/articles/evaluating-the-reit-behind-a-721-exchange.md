# Evaluating the REIT Behind a 721 Exchange

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › Evaluating the REIT Behind a 721 Exchange

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

A 721 exchange does more than complete [the step-by-step tax mechanics of the transaction](how-a-721-exchange-works-step-by-step-process.html). It places the investor in a specific REIT, where distributions, holding value, and future liquidity depend on the assets and people behind that REIT.

First-order thinking treats the 721 as capital-gains deferral. Second-order thinking recognizes the lasting commitment: a REIT may be strong or weak, transparent or opaque, liquid or limited, and the 721 is generally a one-way move. The investment deserves the same diligence as the tax structure.

## Why evaluating the REIT matters

In a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), the investor ties financial outcome to the REIT. Distributions, OP-unit value, and liquidity depend on REIT performance, management, and financial health. A strong REIT can support value and returns. A weak REIT can impair both.

This assessment matters because the exchange is generally one-way and largely irreversible. The holder has committed to the REIT and cannot easily exit tax-free if the choice disappoints. A poor destination is therefore difficult to undo.

REIT diligence is not optional. The benefits of the [capital-gains deferral an UPREIT conversion provides](upreit-conversion-and-capital-gains-deferral.html), plus diversification and eventual liquidity, depend on the REIT performing well. The review should cover portfolio quality and diversification, management and sponsor, financial health, track record, and whether the REIT is traded or non-traded.

## Portfolio quality and diversification

Start with what the REIT owns. Portfolio quality is the caliber of its properties: whether they are well located, well maintained, institutional-quality, and income producing. Strong locations, quality tenants, and sound property condition are more favorable signs than weaker assets.

Then measure diversification. Look at the number of properties, geographies, property types, and tenants. A broad portfolio with no one property or tenant dominating can provide more stability and risk reduction than a concentrated portfolio.

These two axes work together. Quality points to the likely performance of the assets; breadth points to risk reduction. A well-located, institutional-grade, income-producing portfolio spread across properties, markets, sectors, and tenants is a stronger sign than a weak or concentrated portfolio.

## Management and sponsor

The management team determines acquisition, operation, financing, disposition, and strategy. Its competence, experience, and track record across market cycles matter directly to the investor's outcome. Experienced management with sound decisions is a positive; weak management is a risk.

The sponsor—the firm behind the REIT—also requires review. Consider reputation, integrity, financial strength, history, alignment of interests, conflicts, and any prior problems. A reputable sponsor with a strong history can provide confidence. A sponsor with financial issues, conflicts, or a poor record is a warning sign.

In short, people drive the performance. The management team and sponsor should be evaluated for competence, experience, track record, reputation, and strength before the investor commits.

## Financial health

Financial condition determines a REIT's resilience. Review **leverage**: excessive debt increases risk, especially if rates rise or income softens. Review **debt coverage**: the REIT should comfortably cover debt service with income. Review **liquidity** and balance-sheet strength: adequate cash and resources support stability.

Distribution coverage is another essential test. Ask whether distributions are supported by actual operating income or partly funded by borrowings or offering proceeds. Borrowings or offering-proceeds support is not sustainable and can be a particular concern in some non-traded REITs.

Reasonable leverage, strong coverage, adequate liquidity, a sound balance sheet, and distributions covered by operating income are signs of financial health. High leverage, weak coverage, thin liquidity, or unsupported distributions call for more scrutiny.

## Track record and performance

The track record supplies evidence, not a guarantee. Review historical returns and distributions, their consistency and level, performance through varied market conditions and downturns, management decisions and their outcomes, and any red flags in the history.

A REIT that has delivered reasonable returns and distributions, navigated market cycles, and made sound decisions can support confidence. A poor or limited history is a warning. Past performance does not guarantee future results, but it is still useful evidence of REIT quality when considered alongside the portfolio, people, and financials.

## Key Takeaways

- A 721 ties the investor's outcome to a REIT in a largely irreversible move, so thorough evaluation is essential.
- Assess portfolio quality and diversification: property caliber and breadth across assets, markets, types, and tenants are indicators of performance and risk reduction.
- Evaluate management and sponsor for competence, experience, reputation, track record, and financial strength; they drive results.
- Examine financial health—leverage, coverage, liquidity, and distribution sustainability—and track record. Consider traded-versus-non-traded structure for transparency and liquidity.

## Traded vs. non-traded considerations

The traded-versus-non-traded distinction affects transparency, liquidity, and how the investor evaluates the REIT. A publicly traded REIT has public financial reports, a market price, and analyst coverage. Those tools make review easier, and, once OP units convert, public-market liquidity is more robust.

A non-traded REIT can provide less public information. Its valuation is generally periodic NAV rather than a continuous market price, liquidity is limited to redemption programs, and fees may be higher. FINRA emphasizes careful review of non-traded REITs. Their NAV, fees, liquidity terms, and disclosures all warrant closer diligence.

If transparency and liquidity are priorities, a traded REIT may be more suitable. A non-traded REIT is not necessarily unsuitable, but it needs more careful review because its information, valuation process, liquidity, and fees differ materially.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is evaluating the REIT so important in a 721 exchange?

Returns, holding value, and liquidity depend on REIT performance, management, and financial health. The 721 is generally one-way, so a disappointing REIT cannot be easily exited tax-free. Thorough review before commitment is essential, not optional diligence.

### What should I look at in the REIT's portfolio?

Look at quality and diversification. Quality concerns property location, condition, institutional caliber, tenants, and income production. Diversification concerns spread across properties, geographies, property types, and tenants, with no single asset or tenant dominating. Both are indicators of likely performance and risk reduction.

### How do I evaluate the REIT's management and sponsor?

Review management competence, experience, and performance through market cycles. Review sponsor reputation, integrity, financial strength, history, conflicts, alignment, and any problems. The people make decisions that drive the REIT, so a weak team or troubled sponsor is a warning.

### What financial factors should I check?

Check leverage, debt coverage, liquidity, and balance-sheet strength. Also determine whether distributions are covered by operating income or funded by borrowings or offering proceeds. A financially sound REIT with sustainable distributions is a positive sign; high leverage and weak coverage raise risk.

### Does the REIT's track record matter?

Yes. Historical returns, distributions, management decisions, and performance through market conditions provide evidence of quality. A strong, consistent record supports confidence; a poor or short one is a warning. It is evidence, not a guarantee of future performance.

### How does the traded-vs-non-traded distinction affect evaluation?

Public REITs generally provide financial reports, a market price, analyst coverage, and more robust liquidity after conversion. Non-traded REITs may offer less information, periodic NAV rather than market pricing, limited redemption liquidity, and higher fees. The non-traded structure needs added diligence.

### What are red flags in evaluating a REIT?

Look for weak or poorly located assets; excessive concentration; inexperienced or troubled management; a sponsor with conflicts, financial issues, or a poor history; high leverage; weak debt coverage; distributions not supported by operating income; poor track record; and, for a non-traded REIT, high fees, limited liquidity, or opaque valuation. Investigate these signs further or consider another REIT.

### Can I evaluate a non-traded REIT thoroughly?

Yes, but it is harder because less information is public and NAV replaces a market price. Review the offering documents, including PPM, financials, portfolio, fees, risk disclosures, distribution coverage, and redemption terms. Use advisor help to interpret disclosures and structure. FINRA specifically calls for careful review.

### Should I evaluate the REIT myself or with help?

Use professional help. The work requires financial analysis, industry knowledge, and judgment across portfolio, management, financials, history, and structure. Professional guidance supports informed diligence and is especially important because the 721 is generally irreversible.

### What information sources should I use to evaluate a REIT?

For a public REIT, use annual and quarterly filings, financial statements and disclosures, market price, analyst coverage, ratings, and research. For a non-traded REIT, use the PPM or prospectus, financials, portfolio information, fees, risk disclosures, and periodic reports. Review the sponsor’s reputation and history as well; an advisor can help gather and interpret the information.

### How important is the REIT's fee structure in the evaluation?

Very important. Upfront fees, management and advisory fees, and other expenses reduce net returns. Some non-traded REITs have material upfront and continuing charges. A reasonable fee structure is positive; excessive fees are a concern. Consider fees alongside portfolio, management, and financials.

### Can a REIT look good but still be a poor choice for me?

Yes. A high-quality REIT may not match an investor's liquidity needs, sector preference, or risk tolerance. For example, a sound non-traded REIT can be a poor match for an investor who needs liquidity. Evaluate both objective quality and personal fit; a REIT that is good in general may not be right for a particular investor.

### How much time should I spend evaluating the REIT?

As much as the significance warrants. A standalone 721 has no statutory deadlines, so there is no reason to rush. Review the portfolio, people, financials, history, fees, and structure with the advisor until the commitment is understood. Careful diligence is time well spent when the outcome will be tied to the REIT.

### Should I compare multiple REITs before choosing?

Yes, when possible. Compare portfolio quality, diversification, management, financials, record, fees, liquidity, and fit across available REIT or DST-to-REIT choices. Availability may be limited by the property and transaction, but comparisons can help select the strongest suitable option rather than accepting an inferior one.
