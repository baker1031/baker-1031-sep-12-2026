# 721 Exchange vs. Cash-Out Refinance

I keep seeing property owners frame this as a question of how to unlock value. That is true, but incomplete. The first-order question is cash today. The second-order question is whether the owner wants to keep a concentrated, managed property with more debt, or trade it for a passive, diversified REIT holding that is generally a one-way commitment.

## Key Takeaways

- Refinance keeps the property and provides tax-free loan proceeds, but increases debt and management.
- A 721 exchange converts the property to passive, diversified REIT units, defers gain, and gives up control.
- Refinance favors cash and flexibility; 721 favors transition, diversification, passivity, and estate planning.

## Two ways to access value

A cash-out refinance keeps the property. The owner replaces the old loan with a larger one, pays off the old balance, and takes the difference as cash. Loan proceeds are not taxable; see the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/) and the IRS [Publication 525, Taxable and Nontaxable Income](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525). The owner keeps the property's income, appreciation, depreciation, management, and future ability to sell, refinance, do a 1031 exchange, or later pursue a 721 exchange. The trade-off is more leverage, higher debt service, refinancing cost, and continued management.

A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) takes the opposite route. The owner contributes the property to a REIT operating partnership for [OP units](op-units-explained.html), deferring gain under Section 721. The property is exchanged for a passive stake in a diversified REIT portfolio. The owner does not receive direct cash; units may be convertible later, but conversion triggers the deferred gain. The 721 can offer diversification, distributions, passivity, a liquidity path, and estate-planning benefits, but it gives up the property, direct control, and future 1031 flexibility.

## Comparing liquidity, tax, control, and diversification

The refinance supplies immediate tax-free cash because it is debt, but leaves embedded gain in the property. The 721 defers gain—and the deferral can be eliminated by a step-up at death—but does not supply tax-free cash. A refinance preserves control and keeps the owner concentrated in one property. A 721 gives up control to the REIT and changes single-property exposure into its portfolio diversification.

So the refinance is for liquidity while keeping the property. The 721 is for a transition out of the property into passive REIT ownership. A refinance adds debt and retains management. A 721 avoids a new borrowing decision but creates a generally irreversible REIT commitment.

| Dimension | Cash-out refinance | 721 exchange |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liquidity | Immediate, tax-free loan proceeds | OP units; later taxable conversion |
| Gain | Embedded gain remains | Gain deferred, potentially through step-up at death |
| Control | Retained | REIT controls portfolio |
| Diversification | One property remains concentrated | REIT portfolio diversification |
| Reversibility | Debt can be repaid while the owner keeps the property and full direct control | Generally a one-way move into REIT ownership |
| Trade-off | More debt and management | One-way passive REIT ownership |

## When a refinance fits

A refinance fits an owner who needs cash for another investment, an expense, or an opportunity but wants to retain the property. It also fits an owner who values direct management, wants to retain the property’s income and appreciation, and is comfortable servicing more debt. It preserves optionality: sell, refinance again, conduct a future 1031, or make a later 721 contribution.

## When the 721 fits

A 721 fits an owner whose aim is not just cash but a transition out of direct ownership: less management, less concentration, more passivity, tax deferral, and estate planning. It is for an owner willing to exchange control and the property itself for diversified REIT units. The owner needs to accept that conversion to cash is taxable and that REIT ownership is generally the endpoint.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a 721 exchange and a cash-out refinance?

A refinance keeps the property and borrows tax-free cash against equity. A 721 exchanges the property for passive, diversified REIT units and defers gain. One is keep-and-borrow; the other is transition-and-convert.

### Which gives me cash?

The cash-out refinance gives immediate tax-free cash. A 721 provides OP units, not cash; conversion to shares and sale can provide liquidity later but trigger the deferred gain.

### Which is more tax-efficient?

They are tax-efficient in different ways. Refinancing produces tax-free loan proceeds but leaves gain in the property. A 721 defers gain, potentially through death, but does not provide tax-free cash.

### Which keeps me in control?

The refinance. The owner retains property, management, and future transaction choices. A 721 makes the owner a passive REIT investor.

### Which diversifies me?

The 721 can turn one property into a stake in the REIT's diversified portfolio. A refinance keeps the owner concentrated in the same property, with more debt.

### When should I choose a cash-out refinance?

Choose it when cash and retention of the property are the goals, direct control matters, and added debt can be serviced.

### When should I choose a 721 exchange?

Choose it when the goal is to leave the property for passive, diversified REIT ownership, defer gain, reduce management, and accept the loss of control and one-way nature.

### Can I do both?

Potentially, in sequence: refinance first and later contribute remaining equity through a 721, or use different properties for each. Debt can create deemed-distribution complexity, so consult advisors.

### Does a refinance avoid the one-way commitment?

Yes. It keeps ownership and options open. A 721 generally ends tax-free return to direct real estate because OP units are not 1031-eligible real property.

### How does Baker 1031 help me choose?

### Is the refinance's tax-free cash really better than the 721's deferral?

No universal answer. Refinancing provides tax-free borrowed cash now, with direct access to the full loan proceeds, but it must be repaid with interest, leaves embedded gain, and increases debt. A 721 defers gain, potentially until a step-up at death, but does not provide direct cash or access to the full value tax-free. The better approach follows the objective.

### Should I refinance before a planned 721 exchange?

Not automatically. More debt can create larger-debt and liability-share or deemed-distribution complexity in a later 721 contribution. Model the transaction with the CPA before borrowing if a 721 is likely.

### Which transaction is simpler?

Refinancing is generally simpler because it is a lending transaction while ownership stays put. A 721 is a partnership-contribution and securities transaction with tax, basis, documentation, and OP-unit issues.

### What are the risks of a cash-out refinance?

More debt, greater leverage, higher debt service, refinancing costs, rate risk, and continued management, concentration, and property-market exposure.

### What are the risks of a 721 exchange?

Loss of property and control, generally irreversible loss of 1031 flexibility, REIT performance and sponsor dependence, taxable conversion, liquidity limitations, fees, and market or valuation risk.

### Can a refinance help fund another investment?

Yes. Cash proceeds can fund another investment, expense, or opportunity, but the added debt and ability to service it should be examined.

### Is a 721 better for estate planning?

It can be. OP units are divisible and may retain deferred gain until a step-up at death. A refinance retains the original property and its estate-planning characteristics, but does not create REIT-unit diversification or passivity.

## Glossary

- **Cash-Out Refinance:** Replacing a loan with a larger loan and taking the difference as cash.
- **Tax-Free Cash:** Borrowed loan proceeds, generally not taxable income.
- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing gain recognition through a 721 contribution.
- **Equity:** Property value remaining after debt.
- **Leverage:** Debt used against property equity, increasing both obligations and risk.
- **721 Exchange:** A contribution of property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units with gain deferral.
- **OP Units:** Operating-partnership units received in a 721 transaction.
- **Embedded Gain:** Appreciation in retained property taxable on a future sale.
- **Deferred Gain:** Gain not recognized at contribution, generally recognized on conversion or later taxable disposition.
- **Conversion:** Exchange of OP units for REIT shares, generally triggering deferred gain.
- **Control:** Owner authority over management, leasing, financing, and sale decisions.
- **Passivity:** Investing without direct property-management responsibility.
- **Concentration:** Reliance on one property or local market.
- **Diversification:** Exposure across a broader REIT portfolio.
- **Debt Service:** Required principal and interest payments.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Basis adjustment at death that can eliminate prior unrealized gain.
- **Suitability Review:** Broker-dealer assessment of the investment against the investor's circumstances.
- **Liquidity:** Access to cash; immediate but borrowed in refinance, later and taxable after OP-unit conversion.
- **Transition:** Moving from direct property to passive REIT ownership.
- **One-Way Move:** Generally irreversible transition into REIT ownership.
- **Deemed Distribution:** A potential tax issue when debt is reduced or reallocated in a partnership contribution.
