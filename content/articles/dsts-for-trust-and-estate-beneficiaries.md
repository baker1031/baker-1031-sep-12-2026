# DSTs for Trust & Estate Beneficiaries

I keep coming back to the same estate-planning problem: a building can be a fine investment and still be a poor thing to hand to three people with different lives, needs, and views on risk. Someone has to collect rent, approve repairs, deal with financing, and make decisions. That work does not disappear because the owner has died.

For a trust or estate, the first-order view is usually tax: “Can we defer the gain?” The second-order question is often more practical. Can the fiduciary turn one management-heavy, indivisible asset into a set of passive interests that can be held or divided in a way the beneficiaries can actually live with?

## Inherited Property in a Trust

[Real estate frequently passes into a trust or estate](1031-exchange-on-inherited-property.html). A revocable living trust, an irrevocable trust, or an estate in administration may be responsible for a rental house, an apartment building, or commercial property. The trustee or executor must manage it for the beneficiaries.

That is an active assignment. There may be tenants, repairs, financing, leasing decisions, and a property far from the fiduciary's home or expertise. A trustee may be capable of handling it and still reasonably conclude that it is not the best use of the trust's resources or attention.

The division problem can be even harder. Suppose three children are entitled to a fourplex. No one receives a clean third of a building. The fiduciary may sell, keep the children as co-owners, or distribute the building to one child and try to equalize the others with different assets. A sale can produce gain, although a [step-up in basis at death](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) may reduce or eliminate the decedent-period gain. Co-ownership often produces friction. Equalization is not always available.

The important point is simple: the estate may be holding an asset that is both difficult to operate and difficult to divide.

## Exchanging Into Fractional DSTs

A 1031 exchange into a [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) can address both problems. A trust or estate that holds qualifying investment real estate may sell its relinquished property and reinvest in like-kind replacement real estate, including DST interests, to defer any remaining capital gain. The DST sponsor manages the property, leasing, and operations. The trust or estate holds fractional beneficial interests and receives a share of the income.

The conversion matters. One building becomes interests measured by percentages or dollar amounts. Rather than asking how to split a physical property, the fiduciary can allocate defined interests under the trust or will. The exchange can also [spread proceeds across multiple DSTs](diversifying-a-1031-across-multiple-dsts.html), sectors, and markets. That can reduce concentration in a single property and make a tailored allocation easier.

Low minimums and fast closing can make the allocation workable within an exchange timeline. A leveraged DST may provide replacement debt on a non-recourse basis, so the trust does not personally guarantee a new loan. That does not make the underlying investment risk-free. The trust still bears real estate, sponsor, and market risk.

The trade is worth stating plainly. DST interests are illiquid securities, generally held until the sponsor sells the underlying property years later. Their value can fall. Distributions can be reduced or suspended. The private placement memorandum (PPM) controls the terms and risks of a particular offering and must be read carefully.

> One exchange can change two facts at once: the trustee no longer operates a building, and the beneficiaries can receive fractional interests that divide cleanly.

## Dividing Among Beneficiaries

Once the asset has been exchanged into DST interests, the fiduciary can allocate those interests in the proportions the governing document calls for. Three children can receive one-third each. A different percentage split can be followed just as directly. The trust need not sell merely to create divisible value or force beneficiaries into managing one physical asset together.

There are different ways to hold the interests. The trust or estate may distribute DST interests directly, leaving each beneficiary with an individual holding and income stream. A direct holder must meet accreditation and suitability requirements. Alternatively, the trust can continue to hold the interests and distribute income under its terms. Where the fiduciary has used several DSTs, particular interests can be assigned to particular beneficiaries.

That flexibility does not decide what is best for a family. It does give the trustee a more precise tool than a single, indivisible building.

## Step-Up in Basis Considerations

The [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can change the reason for an exchange. Under Section 1014, inherited property generally receives a basis reset to fair market value as of the date of death. If it is sold shortly after death for roughly that value, there may be little or no taxable gain from appreciation that occurred during the decedent's life.

For a living owner with a low basis and large gain, deferral often drives the 1031 decision. For a recently inherited property, that gain may already have been reduced or erased by the step-up. In that case, a DST exchange may be less about tax deferral and more about passive management, clean division, and diversification.

The analysis changes again if the property has appreciated since death. That post-death appreciation can create new gain, which can make deferral relevant again. The estate's CPA should model the actual basis, depreciation, and gain; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### Key Takeaways

- A trust or estate may be responsible for management-heavy, indivisible real estate while serving multiple beneficiaries.
- A 1031 exchange into DSTs can turn the property into passive income and divisible fractional interests.
- A Section 1014 step-up at death may reduce or erase gain, making passivity and division—not deferral—the central reason to consider the exchange.
- The trustee or executor coordinates the decision with the attorney, CPA, qualified intermediary, and broker-dealer. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

## Trustee Coordination

The trustee or executor is the fiduciary decision-maker. Fiduciary duty requires acting prudently, loyally, and in the beneficiaries' best interests. That means confirming that the governing document permits the transaction, evaluating whether passive fractional interests are suitable, documenting the reasoning, and communicating with beneficiaries where appropriate.

The work has several owners. The estate-planning or trust attorney confirms authority and how the interests may be held or distributed. The CPA models the step-up, remaining or new gain, debt, and the relevant tax positions. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) facilitates the 1031 exchange. The DST sponsor and broker-dealer handle subscription and [suitability](dst-suitability-and-finra-reg-bi.html). The trustee orchestrates the decisions and timing.

This is not a casual transaction. It must comply with the trust terms and the 1031 rules, and it must be defensible as a decision for the beneficiaries.

## Income and Diversification for Beneficiaries

One inherited building concentrates the beneficiaries' real estate exposure in one asset, one market, and one tenant base. If the property struggles, the income may struggle with it. A fiduciary can instead allocate the exchange value among multiple DSTs in sectors such as multifamily, industrial, net-lease retail, and healthcare, and in different geographic markets.

That can reduce concentration and allow different allocations inside the overall plan. A beneficiary needing income may receive a larger allocation to income-oriented DSTs while the trust maintains a broader balance. It does not guarantee a steadier result or protect against losses.

DST interests remain illiquid until each sponsor sells its property, typically after a five-to-seven-year hold. Distributions are projections, not guarantees, and fees apply. The first-order appeal is a potentially simpler income stream. The second-order reality is that the fiduciary exchanges control and liquidity for a diversified, professionally managed structure. That trade must fit the beneficiaries, not just the tax calendar.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a trust or estate do a 1031 exchange?

Generally, yes, when it holds qualifying investment real estate. It sells the relinquished property and reinvests in like-kind replacement real estate, such as DST interests, to defer any remaining gain. The same-taxpayer rule, like-kind requirement, 45-day identification deadline, and 180-day closing deadline apply. The trustee or executor acts for the beneficiaries and should confirm authority and tax consequences with the estate attorney and CPA.

### How do DSTs help divide property among multiple beneficiaries?

They change an indivisible building into fractional beneficial interests measured by percentages or dollar amounts. The fiduciary can distribute clean shares—such as one-third to each of three children—under the trust or will. Interests may be distributed directly, subject to accreditation and suitability, or held by the trust while it distributes income.

### What is a step-up in basis and how does it affect the decision?

Under Section 1014, inherited property generally receives a fair-market-value basis at death. A near-term sale may therefore have little or no gain from the decedent's lifetime appreciation. That can shift a DST decision from tax deferral toward passive management and divisibility. Appreciation after death can create new gain and restore deferral as a consideration.

### Does the trust still benefit from a 1031 if the step-up already erased the gain?

It can. Even with little gain to defer, a DST exchange can replace an active building with passive management, create divisible interests for multiple beneficiaries, and diversify exposure. If new gain has built up since death, deferral can be an additional benefit.

### Who decides whether a trust should move into DSTs?

The trustee, or executor for an estate, decides in a fiduciary capacity. The decision should be prudent, permitted by the governing document, suitable for the beneficiaries, documented, and supported by the estate attorney, CPA, qualified intermediary, sponsor, and broker-dealer.

### Can beneficiaries each receive their own DST interests directly?

Often, if the trust terms and transaction structure allow it. Direct recipients generally must be accredited and pass suitability review. If that does not fit, the trust may hold the interests and distribute income instead. The attorney and CPA should guide the structure.

### What are the deadlines for a trust's 1031 exchange?

Replacement property must be identified within 45 days after the relinquished-property sale and acquired within 180 days. The clocks do not pause for trust or estate administration. The 3-property rule, 200% rule, and 95% rule also govern identification. DSTs can often close in days, and low minimums can help the trustee size interests within those rules, but planning must start early.

### Are DST distributions to a trust or its beneficiaries guaranteed?

No. They depend on occupancy, rents, expenses, and market conditions and can be reduced. Diversification across several DSTs can reduce concentration but cannot guarantee income. Interests are generally illiquid until the sponsor sells the property after a five-to-seven-year hold. Past projections do not guarantee future results.

### Does the trust need to be an accredited investor?

Generally, the trust—or a beneficiary receiving an interest directly—must meet applicable accredited-investor requirements and pass suitability review. The precise qualification depends on the trust's structure. The fiduciary and broker-dealer should establish this early, before an exchange is underway.

### Can a trust with a mortgaged property still exchange into DSTs?

Yes. To fully defer gain, the taxpayer generally replaces relinquished-property debt or contributes additional cash. A leveraged DST may provide replacement debt through financing already in place, without a personal guarantee from the trust's investors. The CPA, sponsor, and broker-dealer should model the debt requirement after accounting for the step-up and any remaining gain.

### What are the risks of moving a trust's property into DSTs?

There is execution risk: deadlines, same-taxpayer consistency, and authority under the governing document matter. There is investment risk: illiquidity, fees, sponsor and concentration risk, loss of value, and reduced distributions. There is fiduciary risk: the trustee must be able to support the decision as prudent. Diversification, documentation, and suitability review can help manage risk; they do not eliminate it.

### Is moving into DSTs better than just selling the trust's property?

It depends on beneficiary goals. A sale may better serve a beneficiary who needs cash now or is not accredited. A DST can preserve value in passive, income-producing real estate, create divisible interests, defer any remaining gain, and diversify. A blend can also be appropriate, with some shares going to DST interests and others taken in cash. The trustee should compare the outcomes with the CPA and attorney.

### How does diversification help a trust's beneficiaries?

It spreads exposure across properties, sectors, and markets instead of leaving the beneficiaries dependent on one building, tenant base, or local market. It can also allow more income-oriented allocations for a beneficiary who needs cash flow. It reduces concentration risk; it does not eliminate investment risk, illiquidity, fees, or the possibility of reduced distributions.

### What is the trustee's fiduciary duty in this decision?

It is the duty to act in the beneficiaries' best interests with prudence, loyalty, and impartiality. The trustee should confirm authority, weigh the benefits and risks, obtain appropriate professional advice, document the analysis, and avoid treating a complex exchange as a routine choice.

## Glossary

- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.
- **Trustee:** The fiduciary who manages a trust for the beneficiaries.
- **Executor:** The fiduciary who administers a decedent's estate.
- **Beneficiary:** A person entitled to benefit from a trust or estate.
- **Fiduciary Duty:** The legal duty to act in the beneficiaries' best interests.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The Section 1014 reset of basis to fair market value at death.
- **Section 1014:** The code section providing the inherited-property basis step-up.
- **1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.
- **Fractional Beneficial Interest:** An undivided, divisible share of a DST's real estate.
- **Relinquished Property:** The trust's property sold to begin the exchange.
- **Replacement Property:** The like-kind real estate, including a DST, acquired in the exchange.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that facilitates a 1031 exchange and holds proceeds.
- **Diversification:** Spreading real estate exposure across DSTs, sectors, and markets.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** DST financing not personally guaranteed by the investors.
- **Accredited Investor:** An investor or trust meeting thresholds for Regulation D offerings.
- **Suitability Review:** The broker-dealer's assessment of whether a DST fits the investor.
