# OP Unit Income vs. Rental Income: A Comparison

*721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep seeing owners compare a 721 exchange by asking one clean-looking question: “Will the check be bigger or smaller?” It is a reasonable place to start. It is not enough to finish there.

A 721 exchange replaces income from a directly owned rental property with distribution income from OP units. The useful comparison is broader: yield, income stability, tax efficiency, income growth, and the effort and cost required to earn the income. Those are the pieces that show how the exchange may affect an income-focused owner.

First-order thinking looks only at the stated yield. Second-order thinking asks what that yield is net of, how much work and concentration produced it, how dependable it may be, and what control is being exchanged for passivity. Price and value are not the only distinction. Income and the burden of producing it are distinct, too.

## Comparing the income sources

Rental income starts with a specific property: rent paid by tenants, less taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, and any debt service. It is tied to that property’s performance, and the owner can influence it through management.

OP unit income comes from distributions on a REIT’s diversified portfolio. It reflects the holder’s share of income across many properties, all managed by the REIT.

That is the central trade. Rental income is concentrated, controllable, single-property, and active. OP unit income is diversified, portfolio-based, and passive. The distinction drives the rest of the comparison.

## Yield comparison

Yield is income relative to value. A rental property’s net yield is net rental income after expenses divided by the property’s value. A REIT distribution yield is distributions divided by the value of the units. Comparing them is one way to estimate whether income relative to value might rise or fall in a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html).

The answer depends on the particulars. Property type, location, management, and leverage affect a property’s net yield. The REIT’s own portfolio and distribution policy affect its distribution yield. A well-managed, high-yielding property can have a higher net yield than the REIT’s distribution yield, which could mean lower income relative to value after an exchange. A lower-yielding property, or one that consumes substantial management attention, may point the other way.

Compare the property’s after-expense net yield with the REIT’s after-fee distribution yield. The REIT’s fees reduce its yield; rental expenses reduce the property’s yield, but the owner still performs the work or pays someone else to do it. The comparison is situation-specific.

> Whether income rises or falls in a 721 exchange depends on a comparison of the property’s after-expense net yield and the REIT’s after-fee distribution yield.

## Income stability

One rental property can produce a good result for years, then face a major tenant departure, vacancy, large repair, or local downturn. Those events can sharply reduce or interrupt income because the exposure is concentrated in one asset.

OP unit distributions come from a diversified REIT portfolio. A problem at one property normally affects only a portion of the portfolio’s income, and REITs often aim to make regular distributions. That tends to make distributions more stable and consistent than income from a single rental property. It does not make them guaranteed.

For an income-dependent owner, including a retiree, that distinction can carry real weight. Diversification can make the distribution stream more reliable, while a single property’s fortunes can be more volatile.

## Tax efficiency of each

Both income sources can benefit from real estate depreciation, but the mechanism is different.

Rental income can be sheltered by depreciation deductions on the property, reducing current tax and improving after-tax yield. Depreciation is generally recaptured at sale.

OP unit distributions are reported under partnership rules through a K-1. Partnership depreciation can pass through and shelter part of the income. Some distributions may also be return of capital, reducing basis rather than being immediately taxed.

Neither side has a blanket tax advantage. Both carry depreciation-based tax efficiency; one comes through the owner’s property and the other through the partnership. A CPA should assess the facts and the owner’s tax position.

## Income growth potential

Rental income can grow when an owner raises rents within market and lease constraints, improves the property, or changes how it is operated. That offers a measure of control.

OP unit distributions can grow when the REIT improves portfolio income by raising rents, improving properties, acquiring additional assets, or increasing distributions. The holder participates passively; the result depends on the REIT’s performance and decisions, not the holder’s. Neither income source has guaranteed growth, and both remain tied to real estate performance.

## Key Takeaways

- **Yield:** Compare the property’s net yield after expenses with the REIT’s distribution yield after fees. The result is situation-specific.
- **Stability:** OP unit distributions are generally more stable because they are diversified, while single-property rental income is concentrated.
- **Tax efficiency:** Both benefit from real estate depreciation, through the owner’s deductions or the partnership’s pass-through treatment.
- **Growth and effort:** Both can grow. Rental growth is more directly controlled by the owner; OP unit income requires no owner effort.

## Effort and cost

Yield calculations can understate the cost of producing rental income. A rental property requires management, tenant work, maintenance, and the general responsibilities of being a landlord. An owner either supplies that time or pays a manager. Both are real costs.

OP unit distributions require no owner management. The REIT manages the portfolio, and its fees are embedded in the distributions. The trade is straightforward: owner effort on one side, professional-management fees on the other.

For an owner who wants to step back, the saving in time can matter even if the stated yields are close. That judgment is personal, especially for retirees or owners who no longer want landlord work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will my income go up or down in a 721 exchange?

It depends on the comparison between the property’s net yield—net rental income divided by value—and the REIT’s distribution yield—distributions divided by unit value. A high-yielding property may produce a higher yield than the REIT, while a lower-yielding or management-heavy property may produce less. Compare after-fee REIT yield with after-expense rental yield with an advisor. The REIT’s yield is net of fees; the property’s yield is net of expenses, but still carries owner work.

### Is OP unit income more stable than rental income?

Generally, yes. OP unit distributions are sourced from a diversified REIT portfolio, so a problem at one property affects only part of the income. A single rental can be heavily affected by one tenant leaving, a vacancy, or a local downturn. REITs often seek steady distributions, which can be helpful to income-dependent owners, including retirees. That relative stability is not a promise of income.

### Which income is more tax-efficient?

Both can benefit from real estate depreciation. Rental income is sheltered by the owner’s depreciation deductions. OP unit distributions may be sheltered by partnership depreciation that passes through, and some may be return of capital that reduces basis rather than being immediately taxed. Neither has a universal tax advantage; a CPA should evaluate the owner’s facts.

### Can OP unit distributions grow over time?

They can. Growth can follow higher REIT portfolio income from rents, property improvements, acquisitions, and increased distributions. It depends on the REIT’s performance and decisions. Rental income can also grow through rent increases and owner management. Neither is guaranteed; rental growth is more directly within the owner’s control.

### Do OP unit distributions require any work?

No owner management is required. The REIT handles the portfolio, while the owner receives distributions. This differs from rental income, which requires management, tenant and maintenance work, or a manager’s fee. REIT fees are built into the distributions and are the cost of professional management.

### Is the effort savings worth a possibly lower yield?

For many owners, including retirees or busy owners who want to stop managing property, it may be. Time and manager fees are real costs. An owner may reasonably value passive distributions even if their property’s net yield is comparable or somewhat higher. The trade depends on the owner’s own valuation of time and control.

### How do the REIT’s fees affect the income comparison?

REIT fees reduce the distribution yield, but they pay for the management the owner would otherwise perform or pay for. Compare the after-fee distribution yield with the after-expense net rental yield. The fees are a real factor, and so is the work that rental income requires.

### Which income source is better for retirees?

OP unit distributions often fit retirees who want passive, diversified income without landlord work. They may be more stable than a single rental’s income. A retiree with a high-yielding property they are happy to manage, or with a manager they trust, may still prefer rental income. The right fit is personal.

### Should I base my decision only on the income comparison?

No. Income is one part of a 721 decision. Owners should also weigh diversification, the one-way nature of giving up the property and 1031 flexibility, unit liquidity through conversion, estate planning and the step-up, and the loss of direct control. Consider the income comparison alongside the full set of trade-offs.

### Does my property’s leverage affect the income comparison?

Yes. Mortgage debt service affects net rental income and a leveraged yield on equity. Leverage can amplify a property’s yield on equity and its risk. The REIT’s own leverage is built into its distribution yield. A fair comparison accounts for leverage and risk on both sides, ideally with an advisor and CPA.

### How do vacancies factor into the income comparison?

Vacancy is a key reason single-property income can be less stable. A vacancy at one rental can sharply reduce or eliminate income until it is re-leased. A diversified REIT portfolio cushions the effect of any one vacancy. Historical and potential vacancy affect a property’s effective net yield and stability.

### Will I miss the control over my rental income?

It depends on temperament and goals. An owner who enjoys raising rents, improving property, and managing expenses may miss that control. An owner who prefers passive income may welcome the absence of landlord work. For some retirees, loss of control is a relief; for hands-on owners, it can be a meaningful cost.

### How do I estimate my property’s net yield for the comparison?

Start with net operating income: gross rent less operating expenses such as taxes, insurance, maintenance, and management. For a property-level yield, this is typically before the owner’s debt service; for an equity yield, it is after debt service. Divide by property value or by equity, and use a consistent basis when comparing with the REIT’s distribution yield. An advisor and CPA can help build an apples-to-apples comparison.

## Glossary

**OP Unit Income:** Passive distribution income from OP units.

**Rental Income:** Income from a directly owned property, net of expenses.

**Net Yield:** Income after costs relative to asset value.

**Distribution Yield:** REIT distributions relative to unit value.

**Income Stability:** Reliability of income, which generally favors diversified distributions.

**Diversification:** The portfolio basis that can support distribution stability.

**Depreciation Shelter:** Depreciation that reduces tax on rental income.

**Depreciation Pass-Through:** Partnership depreciation that can shelter distribution income.

**Return of Capital:** Tax-efficient distributions that reduce basis.

**Income Growth:** The potential for income to increase over time.

**Effort:** The work required for rental income and absent from OP unit distributions.

**REIT Fees:** The cost, reflected in distributions, of REIT management.

**Passivity:** The no-effort nature of distribution income.

**Control:** Influence over rental income that is absent from OP unit distributions.

**After-Fee Yield:** Distribution yield net of REIT fees.

**Single-Property Risk:** The volatility of rental income from one property.

## OP unit income vs. rental income at a glance

Income from REIT operating-partnership (OP) units and income from a directly owned rental can be compared this way:

| Factor | OP Unit Income | Rental Income |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Partnership distributions from a REIT | Rent from your own property |
| Stability | Smoothed across a diversified portfolio | Tied to one asset’s occupancy |
| Growth potential | Tracks portfolio and distribution policy | You can raise rents and reposition |
| Tax efficiency | Pass-through; partnership reporting | Sheltered by depreciation deductions |
| Effort | Fully passive | Active management or a hired manager |
| Cost | Embedded in the partnership | Maintenance, vacancy, management |

## Which should you choose?

OP unit income may suit an investor seeking steadier, passive income from a diversified REIT portfolio without landlord work or single-asset risk.

Rental income may suit an owner who wants hands-on control over rents and the property and is willing to manage it while accepting that a single vacancy has a larger effect. Each has different tax treatment, so compare the income net of tax rather than relying on a headline yield.
