# 721 Exchange Timeline and Closing Process


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing 1031 timing habits carried into a 721 discussion. The instinct is sensible: find the deadline, then work backward. But a direct 721 exchange is not governed by that same clock.

Unlike a 1031 exchange, with its strict 45-day identification and 180-day completion deadlines, a 721 exchange has no fixed statutory deadline. It is a contribution of property to a partnership under Section 721, not a like-kind exchange. Its schedule instead follows the transaction: finding a willing REIT, due diligence and valuation, documentation and negotiation, the closing contribution, and the post-closing holding period before units can be converted.

The first-order view is welcome relief from a 45/180-day scramble. The second-order view is that lack of a statutory clock does not make the process simple or certain. The parties must still agree on value, documentation, tax protection, and the property itself; the liquidity path may not start for about a year and differs sharply between traded and non-traded REITs. This is educational information, not tax, legal, or investment advice.

## No fixed statutory deadlines

A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) has no statutory timing rules comparable to the [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). The 1031’s [45-day identification](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) and 180-day completion deadlines apply to like-kind exchanges of real property. A 721 contribution produces a partnership interest under Section 721, so those 1031 deadlines do not apply.

There is no statutory clock requiring an owner to identify a REIT in 45 days or close in 180. Due diligence, valuation, negotiation, and documentation set the pace. That flexibility can reduce deadline pressure, but it leaves the parties responsible for building a realistic schedule.

The distinction matters in the two-step [1031-then-721 path](1031-to-721-exchange.html). The initial 1031 into a DST remains subject to the 45/180-day rules. The later 721 exit from the DST to a REIT does not have those statutory deadlines.

## The typical timeline phases

The usual process begins by identifying a willing REIT and reaching a preliminary agreement. The REIT must want the property, and the parties need agreement in principle that a contribution is feasible. In the two-step path, the initial work may instead involve identifying a DST structured for a later 721 exit.

Next come due diligence and valuation, then documentation and negotiation. The closing is the actual property contribution for [OP units](op-units-explained.html). After closing, a holding period usually precedes the right to convert units into shares.

There is no universal schedule. A straightforward transaction can progress quickly; a transaction with difficult valuation, property, structuring, or negotiation issues can take much longer. The property, the REIT, the complexity, and the parties’ pace govern the timing.

## Due diligence and valuation phase

Due diligence and valuation are substantial parts of the timeline. The REIT evaluates the property’s physical condition, title, financials including income and expenses, leases, environmental status, and other relevant matters. It is acquiring the property into its portfolio, so the examination is thorough and takes time.

At the same time, the parties establish the property’s value, which determines the number of OP units the contributor receives. Valuation is typically negotiated and informed by appraisals and the property’s financial results. Reaching agreement is a major economic milestone, and it can require back-and-forth negotiation.

This phase confirms whether the REIT will proceed and sets the economics of the contribution. Both due diligence and a settled valuation are needed before documentation and closing.

## Documentation and negotiation

Once diligence and value are settled, the parties prepare the legal agreements. The key documents commonly include a [contribution agreement](721-exchange-closing-documents-explained.html), which transfers the property to the operating partnership for OP units; a tax-protection agreement, if negotiated, that can protect deferred gain if the partnership sells the property during a defined period; and the partnership terms that govern the unit holder’s rights.

The terms require negotiation: the contribution mechanics, the scope and duration of tax protection, and property-specific or deal-specific provisions. Tax protection can be especially important to the contributor. The owner’s attorney and the REIT’s counsel prepare and negotiate the documents with input from other advisors.

The paperwork determines the units, rights, and protections for the long term. It can be slow work, but close legal attention is essential to a sound contribution.

## The closing (contribution)

At closing, the owner transfers the property to the operating partnership and receives the agreed number of OP units. The documents, including the contribution agreement, are executed and the transfer is completed. That is the Section 721 transaction.

Closing changes the owner from a direct property owner into an OP unit holder. The property becomes part of the REIT portfolio, the contributor owns a stake through the units, and deferred gain is in place rather than recognized at contribution. The holder may receive distributions declared by the operating partnership, but distributions are not guaranteed and may be reduced or suspended.

Closing is a defined event, but it is the endpoint of prior work on diligence, valuation, and documents. Once those matters are complete and the terms are agreed, the closing finalizes the contribution.

## Key Takeaways
- A direct 721 exchange has no fixed statutory deadline, unlike a 1031’s 45/180-day rules; its timing is driven by the parties.
- The typical phases are a willing REIT, diligence and valuation, documents and negotiation, closing, and then a holding period.
- Due diligence, agreed valuation, and documents—especially tax protection—can take substantial time.
- Closing contributes property for OP units. A holding period, often about 1 year, generally follows before conversion.

## After closing: the holding period

After closing, the owner enters the [holding period](721-exchange-lock-up-periods-explained.html) before conversion to REIT shares. As the [liquidity guide](liquidity-after-721-exchange-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html) explains, OP units commonly have a holding period of around one year, though the length varies by REIT. During that period, the investor holds units, may receive distributions, and retains deferral, but cannot yet convert for share-based liquidity.

When the holding period ends, [the investor can convert units and trigger tax](tax-consequences-of-converting-op-units-to-reit-shares.html) as needed. The owner may hold units indefinitely for income and continued deferral toward a possible step-up at death, or may convert gradually or fully. The post-closing timeline is therefore open-ended.

The investor should plan around the lock-up if liquidity may be needed. A public REIT can offer market liquidity after conversion. A non-traded REIT may provide only program-based redemptions on its own schedule, so accessing cash can take longer even after conversion eligibility begins.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a 721 exchange have deadlines like a 1031?

No. A direct 721 has no fixed statutory deadlines. The 1031’s 45-day identification and 180-day completion rules apply to like-kind exchanges, while a 721 is a contribution to a partnership under Section 721. The timeline follows the parties and the transaction: REIT selection, diligence, valuation, documentation, and closing. A 1031 into a DST still carries its own deadlines in a 1031-then-721 path.

### How long does a 721 exchange take?

It varies. The contribution can take weeks to months, depending on a willing REIT, diligence, valuation negotiation, documentation, and complexity. A straightforward deal may move quickly, while a transaction with negotiation or structuring issues may take longer. After closing, a holding period, often about 1 year, adds time before conversion to shares.

### What are the phases of a 721 exchange?

They are preliminary agreement with a willing REIT, due diligence and valuation, documentation and negotiation, closing with the property contribution for OP units, and the post-closing holding period before conversion. The length of each phase depends on the property, REIT, complexity, and parties rather than fixed deadlines.

### What happens during due diligence?

The REIT reviews physical condition, title, financials, leases, environmental status, and other property facts to decide whether and on what terms it will acquire the property. The parties also establish value, using appraisals and financial information, to determine the OP units issued. Diligence confirms the deal; valuation sets its economics.

### How is the closing of a 721 exchange handled?

At closing, the owner transfers the property to the operating partnership and receives the agreed OP units. The contribution documents are executed and the transfer is completed. The property enters the REIT portfolio, the contributor becomes a unit holder, and gain is deferred at contribution. Closing completes the earlier diligence, valuation, and documentation phases.

### Is there a holding period after closing?

Yes. OP units commonly have a holding period, often around one year and varying by REIT, before they may be converted to REIT shares. During the period, the investor holds the units, may receive distributions, and retains deferral, but cannot use conversion for liquidity. After it ends, the owner can hold or convert as appropriate.

### Why is the documentation phase important?

The contribution agreement, any tax-protection agreement, and partnership terms establish the property transfer, the holder’s tax protection, units, rights, and long-term protections. Careful negotiation, especially of tax protection, protects the contributor’s interests. The owner’s attorney and REIT counsel draft and negotiate these documents.

### Can the timeline be faster than a 1031?

It can be, but it can also be slower. A 1031 has urgency and a defined 45/180-day calendar; a 721 has no statutory clock and moves at the parties’ pace. Lack of a deadline-driven scramble can speed matters, while extended diligence or negotiation can slow them. A 721 is neither inherently faster nor slower.

### What if I’m doing the 1031-then-721 path?

The 1031 portion into the DST uses the standard 45/180-day deadlines: 45 days to identify the DST and 180 days to close. The later 721 exit, in which the DST is acquired by a REIT, has no statutory 1031 deadline. Deadlines apply to the first step, not the later 721 step.

### Does the absence of deadlines make a 721 easier?

It removes the stress of identifying and closing within 45/180 days, so it is less deadline-pressured. It does not necessarily make the transaction easier overall. Finding a willing REIT, handling securities requirements, and negotiating documents can be complex. The 721 trades deadline pressure for other demands.

### How do I plan for the 721 exchange timeline?

Recognize that timing is party-driven, allow time for each phase, and account for the post-closing holding period before conversion liquidity. Work with an advisor, CPA, and attorney, set expectations based on the property and REIT, and treat liquidity timing as a separate planning item. Baker 1031 helps owners navigate the sequence from initial REIT discussions through closing and beyond.

### Can a 721 exchange fall through during the process?

Yes. The parties may fail to agree on value, tax protection, or other terms; due diligence may uncover property problems; or circumstances may change. In a standalone 721 that does not close, the owner generally still owns the property because it was not separately sold, so the owner can pursue other options without the tax consequences of a failed 1031 after sale. Advisors can address the specific issues.

### Should I sell my property first or wait for the 721?

In a direct 721, the owner contributes the property to the REIT; there is no separate sale first. The contribution itself replaces the sale. In a 1031-then-721 route, the owner sells the original property and completes a 1031 into a DST first, then a later 721 exit can occur. The appropriate sequence depends on the path and should be structured with advisors.

### Does the timeline differ for traded vs. non-traded REITs?

The contribution process—diligence, valuation, documents, and closing—is broadly similar. The post-closing liquidity timeline differs: after the holding period, a traded REIT’s shares can generally be sold relatively quickly in the market, while a non-traded REIT may offer only limited, program-based redemptions on its own schedule. The distinction matters where liquidity timing is important.

## Glossary

- **721 Exchange Timeline:** The party-driven schedule of a 721 exchange without fixed deadlines.
- **No Statutory Deadlines:** The absence of the 1031’s 45/180-day rules in a direct 721.
- **45/180-Day Rules:** The 1031 deadlines that do not apply to a 721 contribution.
- **Due Diligence:** The REIT’s examination of property before acquiring it.
- **Valuation:** Agreement on property value, which determines OP units.
- **Contribution Agreement:** The document that effects a property contribution for units.
- **Tax Protection Agreement:** A negotiated agreement intended to protect deferred gain.
- **Partnership Terms:** Provisions governing rights as a unit holder.
- **Closing:** Execution of documents and transfer that completes the contribution.
- **Holding Period:** Post-closing time before OP units may convert to shares.
- **Phases:** The recognizable stages of the 721 process.
- **Parties-Driven:** Timing set by the parties rather than statutory deadlines.
- **1031-then-721 Path:** The two-step route in which the 1031 step has deadlines.
- **Conversion:** Exchange of units for shares after the holding period.
- **Open-Ended Timeline:** The flexible, deadline-free 721 schedule.
- **Preliminary Agreement:** Initial agreement with a willing REIT.
