# REIT Leverage & Debt Coverage Metrics

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep coming back to the same balance-sheet question: two REITs can own similar buildings, so why can one become fragile when rates rise while the other keeps operating? The answer is usually debt—how much, when it matures, what it costs, and how comfortably cash flow covers it.

First-order thinking looks only at asset value or a dividend yield. Second-order thinking reads net debt-to-EBITDA, coverage, maturities, floating exposure, liquidity, and access to capital together. A sturdy balance sheet needs more than one good ratio.

## Key Takeaways

- Debt-to-EBITDA, ideally net of cash, is the main leverage gauge; lower multiples usually mean more flexibility.
- Interest coverage and stricter fixed-charge coverage measure how comfortably cash flow services interest, preferred dividends, and other fixed obligations.
- A laddered maturity schedule spreads refinancing risk. A concentrated near-term maturity wall is a serious vulnerability, especially in a high-rate environment.
- Lower leverage, strong coverage, laddered maturities, and mostly fixed or hedged debt together signal a lower-risk balance sheet.

A [REIT's balance sheet is central to its risk](how-to-analyze-a-reits-balance-sheet.html). Real estate uses debt, and comparable properties can produce very different risk profiles depending on borrowing, coverage, maturities, rate mix, liquidity, and access to capital. ## Debt-to-EBITDA for REITs

Debt-to-EBITDA divides total debt by EBITDA—earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. It expresses borrowings as a multiple of recurring operating cash flow before financing costs: roughly how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay the debt. A lower multiple generally means less leverage and more room to absorb a downturn; a higher multiple can mean more vulnerability if revenue falls or debt becomes costly to refinance.

Net debt-to-EBITDA subtracts cash and equivalents from debt first, recognizing that cash can repay debt. It is generally the preferred gauge. Compare the ratio with sector peers and the REIT’s own history; there is no single safe level across every property type or strategy.

## Interest & Fixed-Charge Coverage

Coverage asks whether a REIT can service debt rather than how much it owes. Interest coverage divides EBITDA by interest expense. A ratio of 4x means EBITDA covers the interest bill four times. Higher and stable coverage usually means a greater cushion; falling coverage can warn that rising rates, debt, or weaker earnings are tightening that cushion.

Fixed-charge coverage is stricter. It divides EBITDA by interest expense plus preferred dividends and sometimes other fixed obligations. Preferred dividends matter because they are payments owed ahead of common dividends. Review both leverage and coverage; low debt is not enough if coverage is thin, and high coverage can be temporary if debt is about to reset.

## Debt Maturity Ladders

A maturity ladder shows when loans and bonds come due. A well-laddered profile spreads maturities over many years, reducing the chance that a large amount must be refinanced during one difficult market.

A maturity wall is a concentration of debt maturing in a year or two. If rates are high or credit is tight, the REIT may refinance at far higher cost, issue equity at unfavorable prices, sell assets under pressure, or struggle to roll debt at all. Refinancing risk is an important reason to examine the debt schedule, not merely the total-debt figure.

## Floating vs. Fixed Debt

Fixed-rate debt locks interest cost for the loan term. Floating-rate debt resets with a benchmark, so the interest bill rises when rates rise and falls when they fall. A mostly fixed or effectively hedged debt stack can protect cash flow and coverage against rate increases; heavy floating-rate exposure can increase expense, reduce coverage, and pressure dividends even when the properties themselves are performing.

An interest-rate swap or cap can convert or limit floating exposure, but investors should understand the hedge’s duration and not assume it lasts for the whole debt term. Same assets, different debt mix, materially different risk.

## Assessing Balance-Sheet Risk

Read the metrics together: moderate net debt-to-EBITDA, strong stable interest and fixed-charge coverage, a laddered maturity schedule, limited floating exposure or effective hedges, reasonable loan-to-value, liquidity, and access to capital. Compare each to sector norms and the company’s own trend. LTV measures debt as a share of property value and indicates the equity cushion, but it should not replace cash-flow coverage analysis.

No single number is decisive. For example, debt equal to 40% of estimated property value produces a 40% LTV. A low leverage multiple can coexist with a dangerous maturity wall; high coverage can deteriorate as floating-rate debt resets; a healthy LTV can rise if property values decline even when debt does not. Read liquidity, lease and property fundamentals, debt covenants, and asset quality alongside the ratios.

## Liquidity and Access to Capital

Liquidity is cash plus undrawn credit lines available to meet obligations. It is the shock absorber when markets tighten, tenants pay late, or a maturity must be addressed. Access to capital asks whether the REIT can raise debt or equity on reasonable terms.

Investment-grade ratings, lender relationships, unencumbered assets, and the ability to issue equity or sell assets can improve access. But a REIT whose [shares trading at a deep discount (making equity issuance dilutive)](reit-nav-premiums-and-discounts-explained.html) has fewer attractive options. Liquidity and capital access determine whether a REIT can refinance from strength rather than under pressure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is debt-to-EBITDA for a REIT?

It divides total debt by EBITDA, a measure approximating recurring operating cash flow before financing costs. It shows debt as a cash-flow multiple—roughly how many years of EBITDA it would take to repay the debt. Lower multiples generally indicate less leverage, but sector and business-model context matter.

### Why use net debt-to-EBITDA instead of total debt-to-EBITDA?

Net debt subtracts cash and equivalents from debt, reflecting cash that could repay borrowing. That makes it the preferred leverage gauge, although investors should also examine whether the cash is truly available and how the ratio compares with peers.

### What is interest coverage for a REIT?

It divides EBITDA by interest expense. It measures how many times cash flow covers interest. Higher stable coverage suggests more cushion; declining coverage can signal stress from rising rates, additional debt, or weaker property cash flow.

### What is fixed-charge coverage, and how does it differ from interest coverage?

It divides EBITDA by interest plus preferred dividends and other fixed obligations where applicable. It is stricter because preferred payments sit ahead of common dividends. Interest coverage looks only at interest; fixed-charge coverage captures more of the financing burden.

### What is a debt maturity ladder?

It is the schedule of when debt comes due. A ladder spreads maturities across years and reduces the risk of refinancing too much debt in a poor credit market at once.

### Why does a debt maturity wall matter, especially in a high-rate environment?

A wall concentrates maturities in a short period. High rates or tight credit can force refinancing at much higher costs, asset sales, dilutive equity issuance, or difficulty rolling debt. It can turn a manageable leverage level into a liquidity problem.

### What's the difference between floating-rate and fixed-rate REIT debt?

Fixed-rate debt holds its interest cost for the term. Floating-rate debt resets with a benchmark, so its interest cost rises when market rates rise and falls when they fall. Fixed or hedged debt provides more cost certainty; floating debt provides more immediate exposure to rate increases.

### How does floating-rate debt increase a REIT's risk?

Rates can reset upward, increasing interest expense, squeezing cash flow, lowering interest and fixed-charge coverage, and potentially pressuring dividends. Interest-rate swaps and caps can mitigate exposure, but their terms and duration matter.

### What is loan-to-value (LTV) for a REIT?

It is debt divided by property value. Lower LTV means a larger equity cushion; higher LTV means less protection if values fall. LTV should be reviewed with cash-flow leverage, coverage, liquidity, and covenants.

### How do I assess a REIT's overall balance-sheet risk?

Combine net debt-to-EBITDA, interest and fixed-charge coverage, maturity timing, fixed versus floating debt, LTV, liquidity, access to capital, sector norms, and the company’s history. A single favorable ratio cannot answer the question.

### What leverage level is safe for a REIT?

There is no universal number. Property type, lease duration, revenue stability, development activity, and rate mix matter. The key is moderate leverage relative to peers, stable coverage, manageable maturities, and liquidity sufficient for stress.

### How do interest rates affect a REIT's balance sheet?

They raise floating-rate expense immediately, make refinanced fixed-rate debt more expensive at maturity, reduce coverage, and can pressure property values and equity access. Higher rates can push cap rates up, lowering property values and increasing LTV even if debt is unchanged; they also raise the hurdle for new investments because borrowing costs more. Fixed-rate debt, hedges, and laddered maturities reduce but do not eliminate exposure.

### Do credit ratings matter for REITs?

Yes. Rating agencies evaluate leverage, coverage, asset quality, liquidity, and access to capital. An investment-grade rating generally lets a REIT borrow more cheaply and access the unsecured bond market; a lower or non-investment-grade rating typically means higher borrowing costs and more reliance on secured or private debt. Ratings can lag events, but they affect flexibility when the REIT must refinance or raise capital.

### Can a REIT with low leverage still be risky?

Yes. It can have weak coverage, looming maturities, poor liquidity, heavy floating debt, troubled tenants or properties, or limited capital access. A REIT with modest debt can still have a dividend stretched relative to AFFO or weak growth prospects; conversely, a moderately leveraged REIT with strong coverage, laddered fixed-rate debt, ample liquidity, and excellent assets can be sound. The balance sheet must be evaluated alongside operating fundamentals.

## Glossary

### Debt-to-EBITDA

Total debt divided by EBITDA; leverage as a multiple of cash flow.

### Net Debt-to-EBITDA

Debt minus cash, divided by EBITDA; the preferred leverage gauge.

### EBITDA

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization.

### Interest Coverage

EBITDA divided by interest expense; ability to pay interest.

### Fixed-Charge Coverage

EBITDA divided by interest plus preferred dividends and fixed charges.

### Debt Maturity Ladder

The schedule of when a REIT's debt comes due.

### Maturity Wall

A large concentration of debt maturing in a short window.

### Refinancing Risk

The risk of rolling over debt at higher rates or in tight credit.

### Fixed-Rate Debt

Debt with an interest rate locked for the term.

### Floating-Rate Debt

Debt whose rate resets with a benchmark, rising with rates.

### Interest-Rate Hedge

A swap or cap converting floating exposure to effectively fixed.

### Loan-to-Value (LTV)

Debt as a percentage of property value; an equity-cushion gauge.

### Preferred Dividends

Fixed payments to preferred holders, ahead of common dividends.

### Liquidity

Cash plus undrawn credit lines available to meet obligations.

### Credit Rating

An agency assessment affecting a REIT's borrowing cost and access.

### Balance Sheet

A REIT's assets, liabilities, and equity used to assess risk.
