# REIT Investing for Beginners: How to Get Started

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking this week about how a brokerage app can turn a building full of tenants into a few letters and a Buy button. That convenience is real. It is also where a beginner can mistake access for understanding.

Real Estate Investment Trusts, or REITs, are among the easier ways to add income-producing real estate to a portfolio without buying, financing, or managing a property yourself. The work is not in opening the account. The work is knowing what you own, why you own it, and how much risk belongs in the position.

First-order thinking says a REIT is a simple dividend play: open an account, find a high yield, and buy. Second-order thinking asks what is inside the yield. Is the portfolio diversified? Is the dividend covered? How much leverage is involved? What is the price relative to value? How will the holding behave if rates rise or one property sector falls out of favor?

For most beginners, the sensible route is straightforward: understand the ways to invest, open and fund a brokerage account, start with a broad, low-cost REIT ETF rather than a single name, and build from there through diversification, dividend reinvestment, calm monitoring, and appropriate sizing. This is educational information, not investment advice. REITs carry real risk, past performance does not guarantee future results, and current rules and individual circumstances should be checked with your advisers.

Once the basics are familiar, our [comprehensive REIT primer](reits-guide.html) goes deeper into the property types and structures you may eventually choose among.

## What REITs are, briefly

Before buying, it helps to [know what you're buying](reits-explained-how-real-estate-investment-trusts-work.html). A REIT is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate: apartments, warehouses, offices, shopping centers, data centers, and more. It distributes most of its income to shareholders as dividends.

In exchange for paying out at least 90% of its taxable income, a REIT avoids corporate-level income tax. Its income is therefore taxed mainly at the shareholder level. The practical result is that an investor can gain real estate exposure through shares rather than direct ownership.

There are no down payments, mortgages, tenants, or repairs for the shareholder to manage. You buy shares much as you would shares of another company and receive a portion of the rental income, or mortgage interest, that the REIT earns through dividends. Because REITs must distribute most of their income, they tend to pay higher dividends than the broad stock market. A single REIT also commonly owns many properties, which can provide diversification.

A REIT lets an investor own a slice of warehouses, apartments, and data centers without fixing a leaky faucet. The dividend is the distribution mechanism, not a promise.

## The three ways to invest

There are three main routes. They are not equally suited to a first investment.

### Publicly traded REITs

Publicly traded REITs are individual companies whose shares trade on a stock exchange. You buy them through a brokerage account just as you buy a stock. They are liquid, meaning you can generally sell on any trading day, and their market prices are transparent.

That accessibility does not remove the need for research. Selecting one company means taking responsibility for its property sector, balance sheet, leverage, dividend coverage, and valuation.

### REIT ETFs and mutual funds

REIT ETFs and mutual funds hold baskets of many REITs. One low-cost purchase can spread exposure across dozens of companies and property sectors. For most beginners, a broad REIT ETF is the most sensible starting point because it diversifies automatically and removes the immediate need to analyze individual REITs.

It is not a risk-free route. It is simply a way to avoid making a large early bet on one company or sector.

### Non-traded REITs

Non-traded REITs are unlisted and illiquid. They are offered through a broker-dealer to accredited or otherwise suitable investors after a suitability review. They have historically carried higher fees and are generally not a beginner’s first step.

For that reason, beginners usually start with a diversified REIT ETF or a publicly traded REIT. Non-traded REITs are a later, adviser-assisted consideration for a suitable investor who understands the liquidity, fee, and holding-period trade-offs.

## Opening a brokerage account

For publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs, a brokerage account is the practical gateway. It is an account used to buy and sell securities such as stocks, ETFs, and REITs.

Opening one is usually straightforward and free. You provide personal and financial information, link a bank account, fund the account, and can often be ready to invest within a day. Many brokerages have no account minimums and charge no commissions on stock and ETF trades.

When comparing brokerages, look for low or no trading commissions, no account minimums, access to the REIT ETFs and individual REITs you want, useful research tools, and a setting for automatic dividend reinvestment.

You can also hold publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs in a tax-advantaged account such as an IRA. That can be attractive because REIT dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income. An IRA can defer or shelter that taxation, but the right account depends on your circumstances. Once the account is open and funded, buying a REIT or REIT ETF is generally a matter of entering its ticker symbol and placing an order.

## Picking a first REIT

For most beginners, the first holding should be a broad, low-cost REIT ETF rather than an individual REIT. An ETF can hold dozens of REITs across many property sectors, so one company or one sector is less likely to dominate the outcome. It offers a diversified foundation and keeps the initial decision simple.

If you choose an individual REIT now or later, consider three things.

1. **Sector and demand drivers.** Residential, industrial, retail, healthcare, data centers, and other sectors do not behave alike. Ask what supports demand for the property type and what could weaken it.
2. **Quality.** Review the balance sheet, leverage, and whether the dividend is sustainable. For REITs, dividend sustainability is commonly judged against adjusted funds from operations, or AFFO, rather than by chasing the highest yield.
3. **Price relative to value.** Avoid paying steep premiums to net asset value, or NAV. Price-to-FFO can also help frame valuation.

Starting small matters. So does spreading individual holdings across sectors. A high yield can be a warning rather than a gift, particularly when it reflects a falling share price or a dividend that the business may not be able to sustain. We do not recommend specific funds or companies; the appropriate choice depends on your goals and the options available to you.

## Key takeaways

- A REIT owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and pays most of its income as dividends, giving investors real estate exposure without owning property directly.
- The three routes are publicly traded REITs, REIT ETFs or funds, and non-traded REITs. ETFs are generally the most beginner-friendly route; non-traded REITs are not usually a first step.
- Opening and funding a brokerage account is the quick, usually free gateway to buying traded REITs and ETFs.
- Most beginners are best served by a broad, low-cost REIT ETF, then by diversification, dividend reinvestment, sensible monitoring, and appropriate sizing.

## Building from there

After the first purchase, good habits matter more than constant trading. If you start with a broad REIT ETF, you already have a diversified base. If you add individual REITs, spread them across sectors rather than concentrating in one property type.

Reinvesting dividends can also matter. A large part of a REIT’s return can come from income rather than price appreciation. A dividend reinvestment plan, or DRIP, uses distributions to buy additional shares; those shares may in turn pay their own dividends. Most brokerages let you activate this automatically with one setting.

Reinvestment is not automatically right for everyone. It is generally most useful to a long-term investor who does not need the current cash flow. If you rely on dividends for income, you may take them in cash. And reinvested dividends in a taxable account are generally taxable in the year received even if the cash never reaches your bank account.

Monitor holdings periodically, not compulsively. Check whether dividends remain covered by AFFO, whether leverage is reasonable, and whether the overall REIT allocation still fits your plan. REITs are volatile and rate-sensitive; short-term price changes alone are not a plan for action.

Keep REITs in proportion. They are one component of a diversified portfolio, not the entire portfolio. Over time, you can add positions, refine the mix, and decide—with professional guidance—whether a non-traded REIT is appropriate for part of the allocation.

The useful moves are boring on purpose: diversify, reinvest when it fits your needs, monitor calmly, and size the position sensibly.

## Common beginner questions

You can often start with very little money. Many REIT ETFs and individual public REITs trade at modest share prices, commonly from tens to a few hundred dollars per share. Many brokerages have no account minimum and some offer fractional shares, letting investors invest a specific dollar amount, even a few dollars, instead of buying a whole share. That is very different from direct property ownership, which can require a down payment, financing, and meaningful capital. The right amount is the amount that fits your overall financial plan and risk tolerance.

No investment is “safe,” and REITs are no exception. Share prices can fluctuate or fall, sometimes sharply, even when underlying properties and rents are stable. Exchange-traded REITs are still equities, affected by market sentiment and risk appetite. Rising interest rates can pressure valuations and borrowing costs. Distributions are not guaranteed and can be cut if income falls, and individual property sectors can face structural headwinds.

That does not rule REITs out. It means risk should be managed rather than ignored: a broad ETF, a suitable allocation, dividend reinvestment when appropriate, a long horizon, and resistance to the temptation to chase yield can help. None eliminates risk. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

Tax treatment also needs care. Most REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income, rather than at the lower qualified-dividend rates, because the REIT did not pay corporate tax on the income before distributing it. A 20% deduction under Section 199A applies to qualified REIT dividends and was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. Some distributions may be return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than being taxed currently, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the breakdown on Form 1099-DIV.

Because dividends are mostly ordinary income, many investors consider tax-advantaged accounts. A traditional IRA can allow tax-deferred growth; a Roth IRA can allow tax-free growth if its rules are met. Account choice depends on your tax situation, contribution limits, and goals. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the easiest way for a beginner to invest in REITs?

For most beginners, the simplest starting point is a broad, low-cost REIT ETF through a brokerage account. An ETF holds many REITs across sectors, so one purchase provides diversification, reduces concentration risk, and avoids the immediate need to select individual companies. Opening and funding an account is usually free and does not usually require a minimum; you buy the ETF by entering its ticker symbol, as you would a stock. This start-broad approach also reduces two common errors: concentrating in one sector and chasing the highest-yielding individual name. Individual REITs can be added later after you understand the research involved. REITs still carry risk, so the allocation should remain suitable within a diversified portfolio.

### What is a REIT in simple terms?

A REIT, or Real Estate Investment Trust, is a company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate and pays most of its income to shareholders as dividends. Buying its shares can give you exposure to apartments, warehouses, shopping centers, offices, data centers, and other property types without buying, financing, or managing property yourself. The REIT is required to distribute at least 90% of taxable income, which lets it avoid corporate-level income tax. A shareholder receives dividends and can benefit from diversification when the REIT owns many properties.

### How much money do I need to start investing in REITs?

You can start with very little. Many REIT ETFs and publicly traded REITs trade from tens to a few hundred dollars per share, many brokerages have no account minimum or stock/ETF commission, and some firms offer fractional shares. The meaningful limit is not a stated minimum; it is an amount that fits your financial plan and risk tolerance. Start small, build over time, and keep the allocation appropriate within a diversified portfolio.

### Should I buy individual REITs or a REIT ETF?

For most beginners, a diversified REIT ETF is the better first choice. It holds many companies across sectors in one low-cost purchase and avoids the need to analyze individual balance sheets, dividend coverage, and valuation. An individual REIT can provide targeted exposure to a sector or company you have researched, but it requires more work and has greater single-company risk. A reasonable path is to begin with a broad ETF and, if desired, later add carefully selected individual names across sectors, evaluating quality, AFFO coverage, and price relative to NAV.

### Do I need a brokerage account to buy REITs?

Yes, for publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs. A brokerage account lets you buy and sell securities. You provide personal and financial information, link and fund a bank account, and are often ready within a day; many firms have no minimum and no stock/ETF commissions. You then enter the ticker and place an order. Public REITs and ETFs can also be held in an IRA. Non-traded REITs are different: they are offered through a broker-dealer to accredited or otherwise suitable investors following a suitability review.

### What should I look for when picking my first REIT?

The simplest sound choice is often a broad low-cost ETF. If you select an individual REIT, evaluate the property sector and its demand drivers, the company’s balance sheet and leverage, whether distributions are covered by AFFO, and price relative to value. Avoid steep premiums to NAV, and use price-to-FFO as one valuation lens. Start small, diversify across sectors, and do not buy solely for a high yield. We do not recommend specific funds or companies.

### Are REITs safe for beginners?

No investment is risk-free. REIT share prices can fluctuate with the market and fall even when underlying properties are stable. Rising interest rates can pressure valuations and borrowing costs; distributions can be cut if property income falls; and particular sectors can face structural headwinds. A broad ETF, appropriate sizing, reinvestment when suitable, and a calm response to short-term volatility can help manage risk, but not eliminate it. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### How are REIT dividends taxed for a beginner investor?

Most REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income because the REIT paid no corporate tax before distributing the income. Qualified REIT dividends receive a 20% deduction under Section 199A, which was made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA legislation. A distribution can instead be return of capital, reducing cost basis rather than being taxed currently, or a capital-gain distribution, taxed at capital-gains rates; Form 1099-DIV shows the allocation. Because dividends are mostly ordinary income, an IRA may be useful, but the right treatment depends on your situation. Confirm current rules with your tax adviser; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### Should I reinvest my REIT dividends?

Automatic reinvestment can be useful for a long-term investor who does not need current income. Each reinvested distribution purchases more shares, which may generate further dividends. Most brokerages offer this as a DRIP setting. If you need cash flow, you may take distributions in cash instead. Remember that reinvested dividends are generally taxable in a taxable account in the year received, which is one reason some investors consider tax-advantaged accounts.

### What are the three ways to invest in REITs?

The three routes are publicly traded REITs, REIT ETFs or mutual funds, and non-traded REITs. Public REITs trade on exchanges, are liquid and transparently priced, but require research. ETFs and funds offer a low-cost basket of companies and sectors and are generally the best beginner starting point. Non-traded REITs are unlisted and illiquid, have historically carried higher fees, and are offered through a broker-dealer after a suitability review to accredited or otherwise suitable investors. Liquidity needs and goals should inform the choice.

### Can I hold REITs in an IRA?

Yes. Public REITs and REIT ETFs can be held in a traditional or Roth IRA. A traditional IRA may defer tax on dividends, while a Roth IRA may shelter growth from tax if its rules are met. That can be attractive for income that is mostly ordinary income. The appropriate account depends on your tax situation, contribution limits, and goals, so confirm it with your tax adviser.

### How do I build a REIT portfolio over time?

Start with a diversified base, often a broad low-cost ETF. If you add individual names, spread them across sectors. Reinvest dividends if that fits your needs, periodically review AFFO coverage, leverage, and overall allocation, and avoid reacting to every short-term, rate-sensitive price movement. Keep REITs as one component of a broader diversified portfolio. You can add and refine holdings over time; potential non-traded exposure merits professional guidance.

### Will I lose money investing in REITs?

You can lose money. REIT prices can fall significantly even when property operations remain stable because publicly traded REITs are subject to market sentiment and risk appetite. Rising rates, distribution cuts, and sector-specific headwinds can also hurt results. A broad ETF, suitable sizing, a long horizon, reinvestment when appropriate, and avoiding yield chasing can help manage the risk. They cannot guarantee an outcome. Invest only what fits your plan and risk tolerance.

### Do I need a financial advisor to invest in REITs?

You do not necessarily need an adviser to buy publicly traded REITs or REIT ETFs. Many beginners open and fund a regular brokerage account and start with a broad ETF on a self-directed basis. An adviser can be useful as circumstances become more complex—for allocation decisions, fund-versus-individual selection, integration with a broader financial and tax plan, or specialized options. Non-traded REITs require a broker-dealer and suitability review, so they are not self-directed and are generally not a beginner’s first step.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
- **Dividend:** The income distribution a REIT pays its shareholders.
- **Publicly Traded REIT:** An exchange-listed REIT bought through a brokerage account.
- **REIT ETF:** A fund holding many REITs for instant diversification.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** An unlisted, illiquid REIT offered through a broker-dealer.
- **Brokerage Account:** An account used to buy and sell securities like REITs.
- **Ticker Symbol:** The code used to identify and trade a REIT or ETF.
- **Diversification:** Spreading investments to reduce concentration risk.
- **Dividend Reinvestment (DRIP):** Automatically using distributions to buy more shares.
- **AFFO:** Adjusted funds from operations—a proxy for distributable cash.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** Per-share value of a REIT’s property net of liabilities.
- **Dividend Yield:** Annual distribution divided by share price.
- **IRA:** A tax-advantaged retirement account that can hold REITs.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
- **Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a non-traded REIT fits an investor.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

## A practical stance for a first allocation

I would begin with the question that comes after “Can I buy this?”: “What can go wrong if I own it?” For a first allocation, broad diversification, modest position sizing, and the willingness to learn before adding complexity are more useful than a clever ticker or a headline yield.
