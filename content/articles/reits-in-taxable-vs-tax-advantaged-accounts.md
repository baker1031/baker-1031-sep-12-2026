# REITs in Taxable vs. Tax-Advantaged Accounts

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the REIT placement question reduced to a slogan: put income assets in an IRA. That is a useful starting point. It is not an answer.

REIT distributions are mostly ordinary income, which makes the question practical. Should the income sit in a taxable account, where it is taxed each year, or in a tax-advantaged account, where it is not? The answer depends on your tax bracket, the size of the REIT allocation, the accounts you already have, and what the distribution is made of. It is educational information, not tax advice; verify current rules with your tax advisor.

The first-order observation is that an IRA shelters annual ordinary income. The second-order observation is that moving a REIT there also gives up the taxable-account benefits that can matter: the 20% Section 199A deduction and, in some cases, return-of-capital deferral. That is why account placement is a trade-off, not a universal rule.

For the underlying structure, see our [complete REIT investing guide](reits-guide.html).

## Why REIT Dividends Are Tax-Heavy

A REIT generally pays no corporate income tax when it distributes at least 90% of its taxable income. The trade-off is that most of the dividend it passes through is ordinary income, taxed at full ordinary rates. A typical corporate dividend may qualify for lower long-term-capital-gains rates; the bulk of a REIT distribution is more like wages or interest for tax purposes.

Not every dollar of a REIT distribution is ordinary income. A distribution can include a smaller qualified-dividend portion, capital-gain distributions, and return of capital. Qualified dividends and capital-gain distributions can receive capital-gains-rate treatment. Return of capital is not currently taxed, but it reduces basis. The 20% Section 199A deduction can lower the effective top federal rate on the ordinary REIT-dividend portion to roughly 29.6%. Even with that help, the ordinary portion is usually more tax-heavy year after year than qualified dividends or long-term gains.

That is the tax drag the placement decision is trying to manage.

## Holding REITs in IRAs

Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, and 401(k)s can shelter this current ordinary income. In a traditional IRA, REIT distributions are not taxed in the year received; tax is deferred until withdrawal, when withdrawals are generally taxed as ordinary income. In a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Either account can spare the investor the annual ordinary-rate tax that a taxable account would impose.

This follows the usual asset-location framework: tax-inefficient income assets, including REITs and taxable bonds, often go in tax-advantaged accounts. Tax-efficient buy-and-hold stocks, which may produce qualified dividends and long-term gains, often remain in taxable accounts where their favorable treatment is preserved.

For most investors, ordinary publicly traded REIT shares inside an IRA generally do not create an unrelated business income tax, or UBIT, issue. Public REIT dividends are generally excluded from unrelated business taxable income. Entity-level leverage used by a REIT does not normally pass through to shareholders as unrelated debt-financed income. UBIT concerns more often arise in certain private or partnership structures or directly debt-financed investments. Confirm the details with the IRA custodian and a CPA.

The classic asset-location move is simple: shelter tax-inefficient, ordinary-rate REIT income in an IRA rather than paying tax on it every year. Whether that is the right home still depends on the investor's situation.

## Taxable-Account Considerations

A taxable account is not merely the inefficient choice to avoid. Its central advantage is the 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends. The deduction is available only in a taxable account and can lower the effective top federal rate on the ordinary portion to roughly 29.6%. Inside an IRA, there is no current taxable REIT income for the deduction to reduce.

There are other taxable-account features that matter. Return of capital reduces basis and defers tax until sale, often reappearing as lower-rate capital gain. Capital-gain distributions can receive favorable rates. A taxable account can also permit loss harvesting, control over the timing of sales, and access to funds without retirement-account withdrawal rules.

Those features do not erase the annual tax on ordinary income. They do mean a taxable account can be a sensible home for part or all of a REIT allocation, depending on the investor's bracket, goals, and distribution mix. Verify the current rules with your tax advisor.

## The QBI Deduction Wrinkle

The 20% Section 199A deduction is the wrinkle. An eligible taxpayer can deduct 20% of qualified REIT dividends in a taxable account, reducing the effective top federal rate on that income to roughly 29.6%. The same deduction has no current benefit inside an IRA or 401(k), because the REIT dividends are not taxed as received there.

The tension is real. An IRA fully shelters ordinary income from annual tax, but it forfeits both the 20% deduction and the taxable-account value of return-of-capital deferral. A taxable account preserves those benefits, but the post-deduction ordinary income is still taxed annually.

The 2025 OBBBA made the deduction permanent, so it is a durable factor to include in the analysis rather than a temporary footnote. Its value depends on the investor's bracket and return. Confirm the current rules with a tax advisor.

## Optimal Placement Strategy

There is no formula that wins in every case. A higher tax bracket makes annual ordinary-income taxation more painful, which favors the IRA. It also makes the lost deduction more valuable, which favors the taxable account. A REIT that distributes a large return-of-capital portion may already be tax-deferred in a taxable account. A REIT that distributes mostly ordinary income creates a stronger case for a retirement account, subject to the lost deduction.

Start with asset location, then adjust for the ordinary-income-versus-return-of-capital mix, your REIT allocation, your available account space, your goals, and your tax bracket. Some investors hold the same REIT or REIT fund in both account types to balance the competing effects. A CPA can model the after-tax outcome using actual numbers. ## Key Takeaways

- REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income taxed at full rates, making them relatively tax-inefficient in a taxable account.
- A traditional IRA, Roth IRA, or 401(k) shelters that annual income; traditional-IRA tax is deferred and qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free.
- The 20% Section 199A deduction and the taxable-account value of return-of-capital deferral are lost inside an IRA.
- The right placement weighs bracket, distribution mix, allocation size, account capacity, liquidity needs, and a CPA's after-tax model.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are REIT dividends taxed more heavily than other dividends?

Most REIT dividends are ordinary income because a REIT pays no corporate income tax when it distributes at least 90% of taxable income. The trade-off for that single layer of tax is that most of the payout is taxed at full ordinary rates, unlike the lower qualified-dividend rates that apply to many corporate dividends. Smaller portions can be qualified dividends, capital-gain distributions, or return of capital. The 20% Section 199A deduction can reduce the effective top federal rate on the ordinary part to roughly 29.6%, but it usually does not make the annual tax as efficient as qualified dividends or long-term gains. This is educational information, not tax advice; verify current rules with your tax advisor.

### Should I hold REITs in a taxable account or an IRA?

Neither account wins automatically. An IRA shelters mostly ordinary REIT income from annual tax; tax is deferred in a traditional IRA and qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free. A taxable account preserves the 20% Section 199A deduction, return-of-capital deferral, favorable capital-gain treatment, loss-harvesting opportunities, sale timing, and access without retirement-account withdrawal rules. The right choice depends on tax bracket, the ordinary-versus-return-of-capital mix, allocation size, liquidity needs, and account mix. Model it with a CPA.

### What is asset location?

Asset location means deciding which account should hold which investment to improve after-tax results. A common framework puts tax-inefficient assets, such as REITs and taxable bonds that generate ordinary income, in IRAs or 401(k)s. It keeps tax-efficient buy-and-hold stocks, which can produce qualified dividends and long-term capital gains, in taxable accounts. It can reduce tax drag without changing the underlying investments. For REITs, the taxable-only 20% QBI deduction complicates the usual IRA answer, so the framework is a starting point rather than an absolute rule.

### Do I avoid taxes on REIT dividends in an IRA?

You do not pay tax on REIT dividends in the year they are received in an IRA. In a traditional IRA, that postpones rather than eliminates tax: the dividends grow untaxed inside the account, but withdrawals are generally ordinary income. In a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals are tax-free if the rules are met. Both shelters avoid the annual taxable-account tax on mostly ordinary REIT income. Neither receives the 20% Section 199A deduction because there is no current income to deduct against. Verify current rules with your tax advisor.

### Why does the QBI deduction only matter in a taxable account?

The 20% Section 199A deduction reduces currently taxable qualified REIT dividend income. In a taxable account, it can reduce the effective top federal rate on the ordinary portion to roughly 29.6%. Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, and 401(k)s do not tax REIT dividends as received, so there is no current taxable income for the deduction to offset. This is the central trade-off: an IRA shelters the annual tax but leaves the deduction behind.

### Are REITs in an IRA subject to UBIT?

Generally, ordinary publicly traded REIT dividends inside an IRA are not subject to UBIT. Dividends are generally passive, excluded income, and public REIT dividends generally fall into that category. The REIT's own leverage does not normally become unrelated debt-financed income for its shareholders. Certain private or partnership structures, or directly debt-financed investments, can require a different analysis. Coordinate with the IRA custodian and CPA; this is not tax advice.

### What are the advantages of holding REITs in a taxable account?

The main advantage is the 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends, which only works in a taxable account and can produce an effective top federal rate of roughly 29.6% on the ordinary portion. Return of capital reduces basis and defers tax until sale, often as lower-rate capital gain. Capital-gain distributions can receive favorable rates. Taxable accounts also allow loss harvesting, sale-timing control, and access without retirement-account withdrawal rules. These benefits make the taxable account a genuine alternative, not an automatic mistake.

### Does it matter whether my REIT pays return of capital for placement?

Yes. Return of capital is not currently taxed in a taxable account; it reduces basis and defers tax until sale, often resurfacing as lower-rate capital gain. A REIT with a large return-of-capital portion may therefore fit comfortably in a taxable account. A REIT paying mostly ordinary dividends may strengthen the case for an IRA, except that an IRA gives up the 20% deduction. The distribution mix should be reviewed with a CPA using the actual distributions.

### Is a Roth IRA a good place for REITs?

It can be. Qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free, so mostly ordinary REIT income can compound without annual ordinary-rate tax and, unlike a traditional IRA, without tax on qualified withdrawals. The trade-off is that the 20% Section 199A deduction and return-of-capital deferral have no current value inside a Roth. Whether a Roth fits depends on the investor's current versus expected tax bracket, total account mix, and goals. A CPA can model the choice.

### How does my tax bracket affect REIT placement?

It cuts both ways. A higher bracket increases the cost of annual ordinary-income tax in a taxable account, favoring an IRA shelter. It also increases the value of the 20% deduction that exists only in a taxable account. The ordinary-versus-return-of-capital split, allocation size, and account space determine which side matters more. Lower-bracket investors face the same trade-off with less annual tax pressure. Model it with a CPA.

### Can I hold the same REIT in both account types?

Yes. There is no rule prohibiting a REIT or REIT fund in both taxable and tax-advantaged accounts. Some investors keep a portion taxable to preserve the 20% deduction and return-of-capital deferral, while sheltering additional ordinary income in an IRA. The appropriate split depends on bracket, distributions, allocation size, and space in each account. A CPA can compare after-tax outcomes of different splits.

### Does asset location matter more for REITs than for stocks?

It often does because REIT income is more tax-inefficient. A buy-and-hold stock producing qualified dividends and long-term capital gains is already treated favorably in a taxable account; moving it to an IRA can sacrifice those rates and the taxable-account step-up-in-basis benefit. Most REIT distributions are ordinary income each year, so placement can have more effect on after-tax returns. The 20% QBI deduction partly offsets that inefficiency, so the answer still is not automatic.

### What is the best account for REITs overall?

There is no single best account. The asset-location principle often points toward an IRA or 401(k) because they shelter mostly ordinary income. The taxable account preserves the 20% Section 199A deduction and return-of-capital deferral. The balance turns on bracket, distribution mix, allocation size, available account space, liquidity, and goals. Some investors split the allocation. Use a CPA's after-tax model rather than a blanket rule.

## Glossary

- **Asset Location:** Choosing which accounts hold which investments for tax efficiency.
- **Tax-Advantaged Account:** An IRA or 401(k) where income is not taxed annually.
- **Taxable Account:** A standard brokerage account where income is taxed each year.
- **Traditional IRA:** A tax-deferred account; withdrawals taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth IRA:** An account funded after-tax; qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
- **Ordinary Income:** Income taxed at full ordinary rates, like most REIT dividends.
- **Qualified Dividend:** A dividend taxed at lower capital-gains rates.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends, taxable accounts only.
- **Return of Capital:** A distribution that reduces basis and defers tax to sale.
- **Capital-Gain Distribution:** A REIT payout taxed at favorable capital-gains rates.
- **Tax Efficiency:** How lightly an investment's income is taxed each year.
- **UBIT:** Unrelated business income tax, generally not triggered by traded REIT shares.
- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing tax to a later year, as inside an IRA.
- **Loss Harvesting:** Selling at a loss to offset gains, available in taxable accounts.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The rule that lets a REIT avoid corporate tax by paying out income.
- **Effective Rate:** The real tax rate after deductions, about 29.6% on qualified REIT dividends.

## REITs in taxable vs. tax-advantaged accounts at a glance

| Factor | Taxable Account | Tax-Advantaged (IRA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dividend taxation | Mostly ordinary income each year | Tax-deferred, or tax-free in a Roth |
| QBI deduction | May apply to REIT dividends | Not relevant inside the account |
| Annual tax drag | Higher because income is taxed yearly | None until withdrawal |
| Withdrawal | Anytime; basis already taxed | Taxed in a Traditional IRA or tax-free in a Roth |
| Best account fit | If QBI or liquidity matters | Often an efficient home for REIT income |

## Which should you choose?

Because REIT dividends are largely ordinary income, a tax-advantaged account frequently improves after-tax results, and a Roth can remove tax on qualified withdrawals. A taxable account can still make sense when liquidity matters or when the QBI deduction and return-of-capital treatment materially offset the annual drag. Coordinate placement across accounts with a CPA.
