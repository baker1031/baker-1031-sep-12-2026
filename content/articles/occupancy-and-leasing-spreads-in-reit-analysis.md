# Occupancy & Leasing Spreads in REIT Analysis

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been looking at how easily a steady reported NOI number can hide a change already underway in the leasing market. Occupancy and leasing spreads are leading indicators in REIT analysis: they show demand, pricing power, and the likely direction of income before the income statement catches up.

First-order thinking stops at current NOI and FFO. Second-order thinking asks what is happening on leases being signed or renewed today, what rents are resetting to, and how much of the portfolio is about to roll. This guide explains occupancy, leasing and renewal spreads, sector context, and early warning signs.

## Key Takeaways

- Occupancy—the percentage of space leased—directly drives REIT revenue and is a leading indicator of tenant demand and future income.
- Leasing and renewal spreads measure rent changes on new and renewing leases versus prior rents. Positive spreads signal pricing power; negative spreads signal weakness.
- Both measures lead reported NOI and FFO, offering a read on where income may be headed several quarters out.
- Benchmark them by sector. Industrial spreads have been strong in certain periods while office has been softer; falling occupancy and negative spreads together are an early warning.

By the time reported NOI turns down, the cause may have been visible for some time in occupancy and leasing spreads. Occupancy measures the share of a property's space that is leased; leasing and renewal spreads measure the rent change on new or renewing leases relative to the expiring rent. Together they show demand, pricing power, and the likely direction of NOI and FFO. ## Why Occupancy Matters

Occupancy is a fundamental REIT health measure because it drives rental revenue. If a building is 95% occupied, 95% of its leasable space is generating rent and 5% is vacant. For an equity REIT, rent is the primary revenue source, so occupancy shows how much earning capacity is being used and flows through to revenue and NOI.

It is also a demand signal. Rising occupancy suggests tenants are filling space, supporting current revenue and future pricing power. Falling occupancy tends to precede pressure on rents and NOI because a landlord with vacancy has less leverage to push rates. Analysts should distinguish physical occupancy—space under lease—from economic occupancy—space actually paying rent after free-rent periods and concessions—because the two can diverge.

## Leasing & Renewal Spreads

A leasing spread is the percentage change in rent on a newly signed lease versus the prior expiring lease on the same space. A renewal spread makes the same comparison when the existing tenant renews. Re-leasing at a 10% higher rent is a positive 10% spread; taking a lower rent is a negative spread. Companies may report the figures on a cash basis or a straight-line/GAAP basis, and the results can differ.

Positive spreads indicate pricing power: the REIT is signing leases above the rents they replace, lifting revenue as the portfolio rolls over. Negative spreads can mean weakening market rents or above-market in-place rents that must reset lower to keep space leased. Because leases turn over gradually, today’s spreads preview rental revenue over coming quarters and years.

Occupancy tells you how full a property is; the spread tells you whether it is being filled at higher or lower rent. The second question can matter more.

## Leading-Indicator Value

Occupancy and spreads move before reported NOI and FFO. Reported earnings reflect leases signed in the past. Current occupancy and leasing activity reflect the market today, and those new or renewed leases shape revenue as the portfolio rolls over. That creates lead time before the income statement shows the effect.

Rising occupancy and strongly positive spreads can point to growing NOI and FFO even while current reported results look ordinary. Slipping occupancy and turning-negative spreads can warn of weakening income while reported earnings still look fine. These indicators inform the direction; they do not guarantee an outcome.

## Sector Benchmarks

Read occupancy and leasing spreads against the relevant property sector, not a universal standard. Lease terms, demand drivers, and supply conditions create very different norms. A number that is healthy in one property type can signal trouble in another.

Industrial and logistics REITs have, in some periods, posted strong spreads as e-commerce demand outpaced supply. Office has generally been softer in many markets, with weaker demand and pressure on occupancy and rents. Residential, retail, healthcare, data centers, and self-storage have their own occupancy ranges, spread patterns, demand drivers, and cycle sensitivities. A 5% positive spread can be routine in one sector and impressive in another. Compare a REIT with its sector peers and its own history.

## Spotting Trouble Early

The classic warning pattern is falling occupancy alongside deteriorating leasing spreads. Losing tenants while re-leasing at lower rents points to future NOI pressure even if current results remain healthy. Watching this pattern can give investors time to reassess before weakness reaches the income statement and often the share price.

Focus on trajectory rather than a single level: consecutive quarters of occupancy declines, spreads shifting from positive to negative, rising concessions or free rent, shorter lease terms, and a large group of impending expirations into a soft market. Any one item can be noise, but several pointing in the same direction are more meaningful. Improving occupancy and strengthening spreads can likewise indicate recovery before reported earnings reflect it.

## Using Occupancy and Spreads Together

These measures are most useful together and alongside broader fundamentals. A REIT can raise occupancy by cutting rents, producing higher occupancy and negative spreads. It can also push rents higher and accept some vacancy, producing positive spreads and lower occupancy. Strong occupancy and positive spreads together are the healthiest combination; weakness in both is the clearest warning.

Link the leading indicators to lagging results: [same-store NOI](same-store-noi-explained.html) and [funds from operations](how-to-read-a-reits-ffo.html) show the reported outcome that occupancy and spreads foreshadow. Also assess lease-expiration schedules, concession trends, local supply, the sector position, and [a close read of the balance sheet](how-to-analyze-a-reits-balance-sheet.html). A strong spread has less near-term impact when few leases expire than when a large portion of rent rolls in the current period.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is occupancy in REIT analysis?

It is the percentage of leasable space currently leased. At 95% occupancy, 95% of the space generates rent and 5% is vacant. Occupancy therefore measures earning capacity in use and affects revenue and NOI. Physical occupancy measures signed, occupied space; economic occupancy adjusts for free rent, concessions, and defaults to show space actually paying rent. Rising occupancy supports demand and pricing power; falling occupancy can precede rent and NOI pressure.

### What are leasing spreads?

They measure the percentage rent change on a new lease compared with the expiring lease on the same space. A positive spread means a higher replacement rent; a negative spread means a lower one. Renewal spreads make the same comparison for existing tenants who renew. Companies can report cash or straight-line/GAAP spreads. The figures show pricing power and, as leases roll, point toward future rental revenue.

### What is the difference between a leasing spread and a renewal spread?

A leasing spread applies to a new tenant; a renewal spread applies to an existing tenant extending a lease. Companies often report them separately because market demand for vacant space and a renewing tenant’s willingness to pay can differ. A blended figure may mix both. Positive means higher replacement rent and negative means lower rent in either case; viewing both gives a fuller picture.

### Why are occupancy and leasing spreads leading indicators?

Current earnings reflect older leases. Occupancy and today’s new and renewal leases reflect the current market and drive revenue later as leases roll. Rising occupancy and positive spreads can indicate momentum before NOI and FFO show it; falling occupancy and negative spreads can warn of weakness several quarters before reported results.

### What does a negative leasing spread mean?

It means the REIT is signing new or renewal leases below the rent they replace. That can reflect soft demand and falling market rents or in-place rents above the market. Persistent negative spreads can pressure revenue and NOI as leases roll, particularly with falling occupancy or rising concessions. Investigate the trend, peer context, and whether other measures confirm the weakness; one quarter can be specific to a lease or temporary.

### How do sector benchmarks affect occupancy and spreads?

They determine context. Industrial and logistics have at times had strong spreads as demand exceeded supply, while office has often had weaker demand and more pressure on rents and occupancy. Residential, retail, healthcare, data centers, and self-storage differ too. Compare each REIT to sector peers and its own history rather than applying one standard.

### How can occupancy and spreads help spot trouble early?

Falling occupancy and deteriorating spreads together signal tenants leaving and replacement rents falling, which can precede NOI weakness. Watch consecutive occupancy declines, positive-to-negative spreads, rising free rent and concessions, shorter lease terms, and a wave of expirations into a soft market. A cluster is more meaningful than one data point. Improving measures can flag recovery early as well.

### What is the difference between physical and economic occupancy?

Physical occupancy is space leased to tenants. Economic occupancy is the share actually paying rent after free-rent periods, concessions, and defaults. A building can be physically 100% occupied while collecting less than full rent. A widening gap can mean headline occupancy is being supported by concessions rather than real pricing strength.

### Do leasing spreads predict future NOI?

They are a useful directional signal, not a guarantee. Consistently positive spreads point toward higher future rent and NOI; persistently negative spreads point toward pressure. The rollover share, occupancy, expenses, concessions, expiration schedule, and broader conditions also matter. Positive spreads affect results sooner when more leases are expiring.

### Where do I find occupancy and leasing-spread data?

Look in a REIT’s quarterly supplemental operating and financial package and earnings materials, not only on the financial statements. The supplemental often breaks out occupancy by property or region, new and renewal spreads, cash versus straight-line presentation, expirations, and operating detail. Earnings releases and periodic SEC filings can summarize key numbers. Find 10-Qs, 10-Ks, 8-K exhibits, and related materials through [EDGAR — Search Company Filings](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/searchedgar/companysearch) and the REIT’s investor-relations site. Read the company’s definitions.

### Can a REIT have high occupancy but weak fundamentals?

Yes. A REIT can fill space by cutting rent or granting large concessions. High physical occupancy with negative spreads and rising concessions can conceal declining effective rents and weaker future revenue. A REIT can also accept some vacancy while pushing rent higher. Read occupancy with economic occupancy, spreads, and concessions.

### How do occupancy and spreads relate to same-store NOI?

They are leading indicators; same-store NOI is the reported result they help drive. Same-store NOI measures the organic NOI change in continuously owned properties. Rising occupancy and positive spreads tend to support future same-store NOI growth, while falling occupancy and negative spreads tend to precede declines. Together they show leasing activity today and reported organic growth later.

### Are leasing spreads reported the same way by all REITs?

No. They can be reported on cash or straight-line/GAAP bases, differ in new versus renewal breakouts, use a blended result, include different lease populations, and treat tenant improvements and leasing commissions differently. These differences can make cross-company comparisons imperfect. They are particularly useful for a single REIT over time; read methodology notes before comparing companies.

## Glossary

### Occupancy Rate

The percentage of a property's leasable space that is leased.

### Physical Occupancy

The percentage of space physically leased to tenants.

### Economic Occupancy

The percentage of space actually paying rent, net of concessions.

### Leasing Spread

The percentage rent change on a new lease versus the prior expiring rent.

### Renewal Spread

The rent change when an existing tenant renews its lease.

### Pricing Power

The ability to raise rents without losing occupancy.

### Leading Indicator

A metric that moves before the reported result it foreshadows.

### Cash vs. Straight-Line

Two bases for reporting rents and leasing spreads.

### Concessions

Free rent or incentives that lower effective rent to attract tenants.

### Lease Expiration Schedule

When and how much rent rolls over to renewal or re-leasing.

### Net Operating Income (NOI)

Property revenue minus operating expenses, before debt service and depreciation.

### Same-Store NOI

The organic NOI change occupancy and spreads foreshadow.

### FFO

Funds from operations, a core REIT earnings measure.

### Sector Benchmark

Property-type norms for occupancy and spreads used for comparison.

### Re-Leasing

Signing new leases on space as prior leases expire.

### Supplemental Package

A REIT's detailed quarterly operating and financial data report.
