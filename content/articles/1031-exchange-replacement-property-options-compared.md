# 1031 Exchange Replacement Property Options Compared

I have been thinking about how often the 1031 decision gets reduced to a deadline problem. It is a deadline problem, of course. But the more consequential choice is what the investor owns after the deadline has passed: a building to run, a net-lease asset, a fractional interest in institutional real estate, or qualifying oil and gas royalties.

First-order thinking says, “Find something before day **45**.” Second-order thinking asks whether the replacement's income, leverage, liquidity, concentration, and control fit the capital for the full holding period. A tax deferral can preserve flexibility. It cannot make a weak replacement property strong.

## The Breadth of Like-Kind Choice

Like-kind treatment for U.S. investment real estate is broad. An owner selling a fourplex is not confined to another fourplex. The replacement could be a net-lease store, raw land, a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), or qualifying royalties.

That breadth is why an exchange can be more than a tax mechanic. It can be a chance to move from active ownership to passive ownership, from one concentrated position to several, or from hands-on operations to a structure with less day-to-day work.

The choices sit on a spectrum. Fee-simple ownership offers the most control and demands the most effort. DSTs typically offer more passivity and diversification, with less control. Most investors end up deciding where they want to sit on that spectrum at this point in their lives.

## Fee-Simple Whole Ownership

Fee-simple ownership is direct, complete ownership. You hold title, make the decisions, keep the upside, and carry the responsibility, risk, and management. It suits an investor who wants to operate property and create value.

The attraction is control and economics: no sponsor layer, no program fees beyond the investor's own costs, and full participation in any value created. The cost is concentration. Equity rests in one asset, one market, and one tenant base, while the owner remains the landlord.

Fee-simple also requires execution under pressure. The buyer must find and close a specific property inside the **45**/**180-day** timetable and qualify for new financing personally when debt is needed.

## Net-Lease (NNN) Property

A single-tenant net-lease, or NNN, property is leased to one tenant that pays rent plus property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, usually under a long lease. It can provide fairly hands-off, predictable income for owners who want fewer management duties.

The quality of that income rests on tenant credit and lease length. Investment-grade tenants, long terms, and rent escalations are generally preferred. The trade-off is single-tenant concentration: one property and one tenant can mean income falls to zero if that tenant leaves or defaults.

An investor can buy NNN property directly, retaining control but accepting concentration and a fast closing. Or the investor can use a net-lease DST, which may diversify tenants and properties and close quickly, but comes with fees and no operating control.

## Tenants-in-Common (TIC)

A tenants-in-common (TIC) arrangement is direct, deeded co-ownership. Up to **35** investors may hold undivided fractional interests and vote on major decisions. Because the interest is direct real-property ownership, it can qualify as replacement property.

TIC investors have more control than DST investors, including voting rights and a direct deed. The trade-off is complexity. Lenders must underwrite multiple owners, and important decisions can require unanimous consent, creating the possibility of deadlock.

Those frictions are why the DST structure has largely displaced TICs in fractional 1031 exchanges. TICs remain relevant where an investor wants a vote or a particular financing arrangement.

## Delaware Statutory Trusts (DST)

A Delaware Statutory Trust holds title to one or more institutional properties and issues fractional beneficial interests. Under Revenue Ruling **2004-86**, those interests are treated as direct real-property ownership for 1031 purposes. DSTs have become a common passive replacement option.

Their appeal is practical as well as structural. They can close in days, which can make them a **45-day** backup; they remove direct management; many hold multiple properties across sectors and markets; and a leveraged DST brings pre-arranged, non-recourse debt. Exact-dollar investment can also help satisfy equal-or-greater-value targets.

The costs are a sponsor load, illiquidity through the trust's life—typically several years—and no control over the sponsor's decisions. DSTs are speculative securities offered only to [accredited investors](insights.html) through a [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html). Read the offering documents and assess the underlying property; speed is not a substitute for underwriting.

## Oil & Gas Minerals & Royalties

Qualifying oil and gas mineral and royalty interests can be real property for 1031 purposes. A perpetual royalty is a cost-free share of production, and percentage depletion can shelter part of the income.

The attraction is often current yield. Royalty interests and royalty DSTs may target more current income than real estate, with the added depletion feature. The risk is variability: income moves with commodity prices and with the natural decline of producing wells.

For that reason, many exchangers use minerals as a yield sleeve rather than as the entire replacement portfolio. Pairing a royalty DST with real-estate DSTs can balance income potential with a different risk profile.

## Debt Replacement Across the Options

Debt replacement is easy to overlook. To fully defer, an exchanger generally needs to replace debt that was paid off, and the available structures handle that requirement differently.

With fee-simple or direct NNN property, the investor must qualify for and take on new financing. A leveraged DST has pre-arranged, non-recourse debt that can provide replacement leverage without a personal loan application. A debt-free DST or all-cash purchase works only when the exchanger had no former debt or adds cash to avoid mortgage [boot](insights.html).

For an owner with changing income, or no wish to take on new personal debt, that built-in leverage can be a deciding factor.

## Liquidity and Hold Period

Fee-simple property can be sold when the owner chooses, subject to market conditions, which gives the owner timing control. Fractional interests are less liquid. DSTs, in particular, are designed to be held through a full life cycle that is typically several years, with only a limited and sometimes nonexistent secondary market.

That illiquidity is the price of some of the convenience and diversification a DST offers. An investor should be comfortable holding through the projected period before committing capital.

Many DSTs end with a full-cycle sale. Increasingly, some may use a **721** UPREIT roll-up into a REIT, which can extend the investment into REIT units. The projected hold and the intended exit both belong in the initial decision.

## Matching Options to Your Goals

The mapping is straightforward, though the answer is personal. Fee-simple can fit a desire for control and value creation. Direct net-lease can fit a desire for simple income from a credit tenant. A DST can fit a need for passive, diversified institutional real estate and easier debt replacement. A mineral or royalty sleeve can fit a desire for higher, depletion-sheltered income paired with tolerance for commodity risk.

Effort tolerance, income needs, risk appetite, debt, and estate planning all matter. An owner approaching retirement who wants passive income and easier estate division may lean toward DSTs. An active investor in prime earning years may remain fee-simple.

There is rarely one right answer. A fee-simple anchor with a DST sleeve, or several DSTs across sectors, can be more sensible than forcing the full exchange into a single structure. The exchange is a way to build the portfolio an investor wants, not merely to defer tax.

### Key takeaways

- Broad like-kind rules allow a change in both property type and ownership model.
- Fee-simple ownership trades more control and upside for more effort and concentration; DSTs trade control for passivity and diversification.
- Match the option to effort, income needs, debt, risk, and estate goals. Blends are common.

## Income Profiles Compared

The structures pay owners differently. Fee-simple income depends on one property and the owner's management. It can be high when the asset is run well, but it is concentrated and hands-on. Net-lease income is more contractual: a credit tenant's rent, often with modest escalation over a long lease, can be steady while the tenant performs.

DST distributions come from rents on one or more underlying properties. They are typically paid monthly, fall in a moderate range, and can be smoothed by diversification across tenants. Royalty income is the outlier: it can have the highest current yield, but it varies with commodity prices and well decline, with part of the income sheltered by depletion.

An investor who needs more predictable monthly income may favor net-lease property or real-estate DSTs. An investor pursuing more current yield and able to live with variability may add a royalty sleeve. A blend can set a broader income profile.

## Risk Profiles Compared

Every option has a distinct risk signature. Fee-simple and direct net-lease property concentrate risk in one asset, and NNN adds dependence on one tenant. A tenant default or a local-market turn can affect the entire investment. Diversification is the owner's responsibility and requires more than one holding.

DSTs reduce some single-asset risk through diversification, while adding sponsor-management and sponsor-integrity risk, illiquidity, and fees. TICs add co-owner decision risk, because a deadlock can block important action. Royalties add commodity-price and production-decline risk that direct real estate does not carry.

There is no risk-free replacement option. The discipline is to identify the risks, size them appropriately, and diligence the specific property, tenant, sponsor, or operator.

## Tax Reporting Differences

After the exchange, fee-simple and direct NNN property generally use familiar Schedule E rental reporting. The owner tracks income, expenses, depreciation, and basis directly.

DST and TIC interests are treated as direct ownership of underlying real estate for tax purposes. Their reporting reflects the owner's share of income, expenses, and depreciation, generally flowing to Schedule E. Royalty interests add royalty-income reporting and depletion.

In every case, the carryover basis from the relinquished property follows into the replacement property and the CPA tracks it. The mechanics differ; the deferred gain still follows the investment.

## A Sample Diversified Replacement Mix

Consider an illustrative **$1,000,000** exchange for an investor seeking passive, diversified income with some yield. A portion might go to a multifamily DST for stability and inflation sensitivity, another portion to a net-lease DST for credit-tenant income, and a smaller sleeve to a royalty DST for higher, depletion-sheltered income.

That combination can diversify across residential, retail, and energy; across structures and sponsors; and across geographies. Exact-dollar DST investments can help the investor hit equal-or-greater-value and debt-replacement targets rather than forcing all proceeds into one building.

This is illustrative, not a recommendation. The appropriate mix depends on income needs, risk tolerance, debt, and goals, and it should be developed with the investor's advisers. The point is that an exchange can construct a portfolio rather than merely replace one property.

## Matching Options to Where You Are in Life

Active investors in their prime earning years often stay fee-simple or pursue value-add direct property. They may have the time, expertise, and willingness to manage assets and accept concentration in exchange for control and upside.

As retirement approaches, income, simplicity, and diversification can take priority. Net-lease property and DSTs can convert active rental work into hands-off cash flow without tenants, toilets, and **2** a.m. calls, while deferring the gain.

Late in life, estate considerations can dominate. DST interests may be divided among heirs more easily than a single building, and deferred gain can be eliminated through a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death. Some investors take a further **721** UPREIT step, contributing DST property into a REIT for operating-partnership units that may be easier to divide and convert to liquidity over time.

None of this is prescriptive. Plenty of retirees keep managing property they enjoy, and younger investors may prefer passive DSTs from the start. The useful test is whether the replacement fits the investor's stage of life, management energy, income needs, and estate plan.

## How to Decide

Start with the next chapter of ownership: more income or more growth, more control or less work, one asset or several, new debt or none. Then map those preferences to the available structures.

Run the numbers on realistic paths, including fees, projected income, and after-tax outcomes. Stress-test single-tenant exposure, commodity variability, and illiquidity. The replacement must make sense as an investment even if the tax deferral is removed from the first screen.

Do the work before the sale. The **45-day** clock is short. An independent, sponsor-agnostic advisor can compare the options that fit a given dollar amount, debt profile, and goals and surface the trade-offs before identification is due.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are my 1031 replacement property options?

The principal options are fee-simple whole-property ownership, direct single-tenant net-lease (NNN) property, fractional TIC and DST interests, and qualifying oil and gas mineral and royalty interests. They can all be like-kind real property, while differing in control, income, diversification, debt replacement, and liquidity.

### What's the difference between a TIC and a DST?

Both are fractional ownership structures. A TIC is direct deeded co-ownership with voting rights, but it brings complex financing and the possibility of unanimous-consent deadlock. A DST is a trust in which the sponsor manages decisions and pre-arranges non-recourse debt, making it more passive and the more common current option.

### Which replacement option is the most passive?

DSTs and net-lease properties are the most hands-off. A DST can add diversification across multiple properties and pre-arranged debt, while a single NNN property remains concentrated in one tenant.

### How does debt replacement differ across options?

Fee-simple and direct NNN property require the investor to qualify for new financing. A leveraged DST supplies replacement debt through its pre-arranged, non-recourse loan without a personal application. Debt-free options require the exchanger to have had no debt or add cash to avoid mortgage boot.

### Are DSTs liquid?

No. DSTs are intended to be held for the full life cycle, typically several years, and they have only a limited and sometimes nonexistent secondary market. Be comfortable with the projected hold before investing.

### Can I use oil & gas royalties as 1031 replacement property?

Yes, where the perpetual mineral or royalty interest qualifies as real property. It can offer higher, depletion-sheltered income, but commodity-price and production-decline risk are material. It is often used as part of a diversified replacement mix.

### Can I combine replacement options?

Yes. Within the identification rules, exchangers can diversify among several options, such as a DST, a net-lease asset, and a royalty sleeve, to balance income, growth, and risk.

### Which option is best for a 1031 exchange?

There is no single best choice. Fee-simple can suit control and value-add work; NNN can suit simple income; DSTs can suit passive diversification and debt replacement; and minerals can suit higher income with commodity risk. The right structure follows the investor's situation.

### How do income profiles differ across replacement options?

Fee-simple income can be high but is concentrated and hands-on. Credit-tenant net-lease income can be steady and contractual. DST distributions are commonly monthly and moderated by diversification. Royalty income can be higher but varies with commodity prices and well decline, with depletion sheltering part of it.

### Which replacement option carries the most risk?

They carry different risks rather than a single ranking. Direct property and NNN concentrate asset and tenant risk. DSTs add sponsor, illiquidity, and fee risk. TICs add co-owner decision risk. Royalties add commodity and production-decline risk. Diligence and sizing are essential in each case.

### How are the options taxed and reported after the exchange?

Fee-simple and direct NNN property generally use Schedule E reporting. DST and TIC interests report the owner's share of underlying income, expenses, and depreciation. Royalties add depletion. In all cases, the CPA tracks the carryover basis.

### How does my stage of life affect which option fits?

Active prime earners may prefer fee-simple or value-add control. Owners near or in retirement may favor net-lease or DST structures for steady, hands-off income. Late-life owners may value divisibility through DSTs or a **721** UPREIT and consider step-up-at-death planning.

### Can I build a diversified replacement portfolio in one exchange?

Yes. Within the identification rules, proceeds can be split among properties or DSTs, such as multifamily, net-lease, and royalty allocations, diversifying sectors, sponsors, and geographies. Exact-dollar DST interests can help meet value and debt targets.

### How does debt replacement differ between fee-simple and a DST?

Fee-simple and direct NNN require the investor to qualify for new financing and assume it personally. A leveraged DST supplies pre-arranged, non-recourse debt without a personal application or guarantee. A debt-free option requires either no old debt or additional cash.

### How liquid are the different replacement options?

Fee-simple property can be sold when market conditions permit, giving the owner timing control. DST and TIC interests are much less liquid. DSTs are intended for full-cycle holds, typically several years, and resale may be limited or unavailable.

### What happens to a DST at the end of its hold?

Many DSTs end in a full-cycle sale that returns capital and gain to investors. Others may use a **721** UPREIT roll-up into a REIT for operating-partnership units. Understand both the projected hold and the planned exit before investing.

### Should I put my whole exchange into one option?

Not necessarily. Putting the entire exchange into one asset or structure can recreate concentration risk. A fee-simple anchor with a DST sleeve, or several DSTs across sectors, can balance income, growth, and risk. An independent advisor can compare what fits the available dollars and deadline.

## Glossary

- **Fee-Simple:** Direct, whole ownership of a property with full control and responsibility.
- **Net-Lease (NNN):** A long-term property lease in which the tenant pays most operating expenses.
- **Tenants-in-Common (TIC):** Direct fractional co-ownership of a property by multiple investors, with voting rights.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust issuing passive fractional real-property interests usable as 1031 replacement property and treated as direct ownership under Revenue Ruling **2004-86**.
- **Royalty Interest:** A cost-free share of oil and gas production that is real property when perpetual.
- **Leveraged DST:** A DST with pre-arranged, non-recourse debt that provides replacement leverage without personal qualification.
- **Single-Tenant Concentration:** Dependence on one tenant for all of a property's income.
- **Sponsor Load:** Total fees and costs in a DST or similar program as a percentage of the raise.
- **Secondary Market:** A limited, often illiquid market for reselling fractional interests before the full cycle.
- **721 UPREIT Roll-Up:** Contribution of DST property into a REIT operating partnership for OP units, a common DST exit.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** The requirement to acquire value at least equal to relinquished property for full deferral.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Debt relief not offset by new debt or cash; it is taxable.
- **Schedule E:** The federal schedule for rental real-estate income and expenses, including DST/TIC pass-through reporting.
- **Full-Cycle Sale:** The eventual sale of a DST's property that returns capital and gain at the end of the hold.
- **Credit Tenant:** A tenant with strong, often investment-grade, creditworthiness that can support reliable net-lease income.
