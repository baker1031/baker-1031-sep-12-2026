# How to Read a REIT's FFO

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing investors start a REIT review with the GAAP income statement, then stop at a low earnings number. It is an understandable instinct. It is also often the wrong starting point.

For a REIT, **Funds From Operations (FFO)** is usually the operating measure that makes the reported numbers intelligible. It adjusts GAAP net income for a large non-cash depreciation charge and for one-time property-sale gains or losses. The result can offer a clearer view of recurring real-estate operating performance.

First-order thinking says a high or low net-income figure tells the story. Second-order thinking asks what created the number: non-cash depreciation, a one-time sale, share issuance, maintenance needs, leverage, and the price already paid for the cash flow. FFO is useful because it starts that second question. It does not finish it.

This is educational information, not investment advice. Verify current details and each REIT's own definitions before relying on them. FFO is reported by listed and unlisted REITs alike; our [2026 REIT investing guide](reits-guide.html) explains where each type reports its operating metrics.

## Why Net Income Misleads for REITs

GAAP net income is the standard bottom-line profit measure for most companies. A company depreciates its buildings over time, recording an expense that reduces income each year. For equipment that genuinely wears out, that can approximate economic reality.

Real estate is different. Buildings may require upkeep, but the underlying property can hold or gain value over time. The real-estate depreciation charge is non-cash, and it can overstate economic cost while understating a REIT's earning power.

Because most REIT assets are real estate, the charge can be large enough to leave a profitable, rent-generating REIT reporting low or even negative GAAP net income. Property sales add another distortion. A gain or loss on a building sale can make a period look unusually strong or weak even though it says little about recurring operations.

That is the central problem FFO was designed to address: depreciation can make healthy operations look weaker, while sale gains and losses make reported earnings lumpy.

## What FFO Adds Back

FFO is the REIT industry's standard operating-performance measure, defined by Nareit. The shorthand is:

> **FFO = GAAP net income + real-estate depreciation and amortization − property-sale gains + property-sale losses**

Some reconciliations also add back real-estate impairment write-downs, another non-cash charge. The purpose is not to erase every accounting judgment. It is to reverse the major depreciation distortion and remove one-time sale activity so an investor can focus more closely on recurring real-estate operations.

The whole point is a single large correction: GAAP subtracts depreciation, while a property may not be losing value in the economic sense implied by that entry. FFO reverses that particular accounting effect and removes sale noise. It is more comparable across REITs because Nareit provides a common base definition.

## Calculating and Finding FFO

The arithmetic is straightforward once the inputs are in hand. In practice, investors rarely need to calculate FFO from scratch. Look first at a REIT's quarterly or annual **earnings release**, and especially at its **supplemental financial package**. The supplemental normally includes a reconciliation beginning with net income and showing every depreciation add-back, amortization add-back, impairment adjustment, and sale gain or loss adjustment.

That reconciliation matters. It shows whether the figure is Nareit FFO or a company-defined measure such as core, adjusted, normalized, or recurring FFO. It also commonly includes FFO per share, AFFO, property operating detail, and debt schedules.

## FFO Per Share and Growth

Total FFO is not the same thing as value created for an existing shareholder. **FFO per share** equals total FFO divided by shares outstanding—the REIT version of EPS. A REIT can increase total FFO simply by issuing new shares and purchasing more property. If FFO per share does not rise, existing shareholders may not be better off.

Track per-share FFO across quarters and years. Steady growth can support dividend growth. Flat or declining FFO per share is a warning that deserves an explanation.

The same number anchors valuation through **P/FFO**, or share price divided by FFO per share. It is the REIT analogue of P/E. Use it against a REIT's own history and similar companies, not as a universal score. A higher multiple may reflect expected growth, quality, or a premium sector; it may also mean much of the good news is already priced in. [FFO, AFFO, and NAV together](how-to-evaluate-a-reit-ffo-affo-and-nav.html) provide a broader framework.

## Key takeaways

- GAAP income can understate REIT earning power through non-cash depreciation and can be distorted by sale gains or losses.
- FFO begins with net income, adds real-estate depreciation and amortization, removes sale gains, and adds sale losses.
- Read the earnings release and supplemental reconciliation, then focus on FFO per share and its trend.
- FFO does not subtract maintenance capex, company-defined variants differ, and it is not exact cash flow.

## Using FFO to Compare REITs

Nareit standardization makes FFO the workhorse for comparing recurring operating performance. An investor can line up FFO-per-share growth, dividend coverage, and P/FFO across several REITs more cleanly than GAAP net income, which is affected differently by depreciation schedules and sale activity.

The comparison still needs discipline. Compare data-center REITs with data-center REITs, net-lease REITs with net-lease REITs, and companies with reasonably similar growth and risk profiles. A higher P/FFO multiple can be deserved; it can also be a warning that the market is assuming a great deal. The number is a starting point for judgment, not a substitute for it.

## Limitations of FFO

FFO has real limits. It does not subtract recurring maintenance capital expenditures—the cash needed for roofs, HVAC, tenant improvements, leasing commissions, and other spending that keeps properties competitive and leased. These are real recurring costs, so FFO can overstate the cash available for a dividend.

**Adjusted FFO (AFFO)** attempts to close that gap by subtracting maintenance capex and straight-line-rent adjustments, sometimes with other normalizations. See [whether AFFO or FFO matters more](affo-vs-ffo-which-metric-matters-more.html).

There is a second limitation: while base Nareit FFO is broadly standardized, core, adjusted, normalized, and recurring FFO are company-defined. Acquisition costs, non-cash items, and other adjustments can vary. FFO is also an accounting proxy, not exact cash flow; it may include non-cash straight-line rent while excluding other costs. Read it with AFFO, payout ratio, and [a careful balance-sheet review](how-to-analyze-a-reits-balance-sheet.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is FFO?

FFO, or Funds From Operations, is the Nareit-defined REIT measure of recurring operating performance. It starts with net income, adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization, removes property-sale gains, and adds back losses. It is commonly found in an earnings release and supplemental financial package. It offers a more useful operating view than net income, but it is not exact cash flow.

### Why is net income misleading for REITs?

GAAP depreciation is a large non-cash charge on real estate, which can hold or gain value rather than wear out like ordinary equipment. That can make a profitable REIT look weak on net income. One-time sale gains and losses also make earnings difficult to compare between periods.

### How do you calculate FFO?

Start with GAAP net income. Add real-estate depreciation and amortization, subtract gains on property sales, and add back sale losses. Some presentations also adjust for real-estate impairment. A REIT's reconciliation normally lays out the calculation.

### Where do I find a REIT's FFO?

Check quarterly and annual earnings releases and the supplemental financial package. The supplemental usually reconciles net income to FFO and may also show FFO per share, AFFO, operating metrics, and debt detail. Confirm whether the result is base Nareit FFO or a company-defined variant.

### What is the difference between FFO and net income?

Net income is the GAAP bottom line, depressed by depreciation and affected by one-time property-sale gains or losses. FFO reverses those effects to focus more closely on recurring real-estate operations. Its common Nareit definition also supports better comparison among REITs.

### What is FFO per share?

It is total FFO divided by shares outstanding. It matters more than headline FFO because a REIT can grow total FFO by issuing shares. Rising per-share FFO may support a dividend; falling or flat per-share FFO is a signal to investigate. It also underlies P/FFO valuation.

### What is P/FFO?

P/FFO is share price divided by FFO per share, the REIT analogue of P/E. Compare it with peers in the same sector and with a REIT's historical range, taking account of expected growth, quality, and risk.

### Do all REITs report FFO the same way?

The headline Nareit measure is broadly comparable. But core, adjusted, normalized, and recurring FFO can reflect company-specific adjustments, and impairment treatment can differ. Compare Nareit FFO with Nareit FFO and read each reconciliation before relying on another label.

### Why is FFO better than net income for REITs?

It corrects the two major distortions: non-cash real-estate depreciation and one-time sale gains or losses. That makes it a more useful measure of recurring operating performance and allows more meaningful comparisons across REITs.

### Does FFO measure cash flow exactly?

No. FFO is an accounting-based proxy. It does not deduct recurring maintenance capex and can include non-cash straight-line rent. For dividend coverage, read AFFO and the cash-flow statement alongside FFO.

### What are the limitations of FFO?

It omits maintenance capex, company-defined variants are not directly comparable, and it is not exact cash flow. Use it with AFFO, payout ratio, the balance sheet, leverage, debt maturities, and property operating data.

### What is the difference between FFO and AFFO?

AFFO builds on FFO by subtracting maintenance capex, straight-line-rent adjustments, and sometimes other normalizations. FFO is more standardized and useful for P/FFO and peer comparison. AFFO is closer to distributable cash but requires more scrutiny because definitions vary.

### Should I rely only on FFO to evaluate a REIT?

No. Add AFFO and its payout ratio, leverage, debt maturities, interest coverage, NAV, occupancy, same-store NOI growth, and lease expirations. A single metric can miss a great deal of the economic picture.

### Is higher FFO always better?

Not necessarily. Focus on per-share, sustainable growth rather than the total. Consider leverage, maintenance needs, AFFO coverage, and valuation. Strong FFO growth may already be reflected in a high P/FFO multiple.

## Glossary

- **FFO:** Funds From Operations, a REIT's standard recurring-operating-performance measure.
- **Net Income:** The GAAP bottom line, which depreciation and sale gains can distort for REITs.
- **Real Estate Depreciation:** The large non-cash charge FFO adds back because property often holds value.
- **Amortization:** A non-cash charge on intangibles that is also added back in FFO.
- **Gains on Property Sales:** One-time gains removed in FFO; losses are added back.
- **Nareit:** The trade association that defines the standardized FFO measure.
- **FFO Per Share:** Total FFO divided by shares outstanding, the REIT version of EPS.
- **P/FFO:** Price-to-FFO, the REIT analogue of P/E.
- **Supplemental:** Financial package showing the net-income-to-FFO reconciliation.
- **Earnings Release:** Quarterly report where a REIT presents FFO.
- **Reconciliation:** The walk from net income to FFO showing each adjustment.
- **Core / Adjusted FFO:** Company-defined variants that are not directly comparable.
- **Maintenance Capex:** Recurring property capital that FFO ignores and AFFO subtracts.
- **AFFO:** Adjusted FFO, generally FFO minus maintenance capex and straight-line rent.
- **Straight-Line Rent:** A non-cash rent-averaging adjustment included in FFO.
- **Impairment:** A non-cash real-estate write-down sometimes added back in FFO.
