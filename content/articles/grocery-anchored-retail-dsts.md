# Grocery-Anchored Retail DSTs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep seeing “defensive retail” used as if it were a finish line. It is not. A grocery-anchored shopping center can have a sounder starting point than a discretionary retail center, but the investment still turns on a small set of concrete questions: Is the grocer healthy? Is the location working? What happens to the inline tenants if the anchor goes dark?

Grocery-anchored retail is an enduring commercial-real-estate category and a common property type within Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs) used in 1031 exchanges. A grocery-anchored DST owns a shopping center whose largest tenant, the anchor, is a grocery store. Smaller inline tenants might include a pharmacy, quick-service restaurant, salon, coffee shop, dry cleaner, or other service business.

The appeal is understandable. Groceries are needs-based, and a healthy store can generate frequent traffic that supports the entire center. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, fractional beneficial interests in a DST are treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. That can allow an exchanger to defer capital-gains tax while holding passive, income-producing retail real estate.

But none of this makes the income certain. Demand and return statements are general and non-promissory. Distributions are never guaranteed, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. Investors should verify the current rules and evaluate suitability with their own professionals. For wider DST context, see [our comprehensive Delaware Statutory Trust primer](dst-guide.html).

First-order thinking says, “People always need groceries, so this center is safe.” Second-order thinking asks whether this particular store is competitive, whether the trade area is improving or weakening, how much of the rent roll depends on that store staying open, and whether the price and structure compensate for those risks. The label matters less than the underwriting.

## Why Grocery Anchors Are Defensive

Food is a non-discretionary, needs-based purchase. Consumers may cut back on travel, dining out, and large-ticket purchases in a downturn, but they still need to buy groceries. That creates recurring demand which tends to hold up better than demand for discretionary retail.

That is why grocery-anchored centers are often described as recession-resilient. The wording matters. Resilient does not mean recession-proof. A store can still lose customers, a chain can still weaken, and a trade area can still become over-stored.

Grocery is also relatively resistant to e-commerce. Most grocery sales still occur in physical stores, where shoppers can select fresh produce and meat, avoid delivery fees and substitutions, and shop when they choose. Pickup and delivery have grown, but the physical store commonly remains the fulfillment hub.

Those facts help explain why a well-located grocer can produce frequent repeat visits. They do not establish a guarantee about any given center’s rent, value, or distribution. The practical point is narrower: essential, largely physical grocery demand can give surrounding real estate a more durable traffic source than a center built around a discretionary tenant.

## Essential-Tenant Demand

The grocery store’s importance is not limited to the rent it pays. It is a destination and frequency tenant. Shoppers commonly visit once or twice a week, creating a recurring stream of people moving through the rest of the center.

That traffic is the reason inline space can be valuable. A pharmacy, dry cleaner, nail salon, sandwich shop, or coffee bar benefits from being where people already shop for food. Some of those businesses will pay higher per-square-foot rents for the chance to capture that spillover traffic.

There is a reciprocal benefit. The grocer can benefit from a more complete and convenient shopping trip, while the inline tenants benefit from the grocer’s customer flow. The resulting ecosystem can support leasing demand, occupancy, and rent across a broader income base than one lease alone.

Still, “broader” is not the same as independent. The center can have many tenants and remain economically reliant on one traffic engine. The grocer does not merely pay its own rent; its steady, needs-based visits can be what makes the smaller shops around it viable.

## Center Structure & Co-Tenancy

A grocery-anchored center is not one lease. It is a collection of leases with different economics. The anchor commonly occupies the largest share of the square footage under a long-term lease and at a lower rent per square foot. Inline tenants generally occupy smaller spaces, often under shorter leases and at higher rents per square foot.

That arrangement reflects the anchor’s role: the grocer brings the traffic. The smaller tenants rely on that traffic, so the anchor’s continued operation can influence the whole rent roll.

Co-tenancy provisions formalize that dependence. Many inline leases provide that if the grocery anchor goes dark for a defined period, an inline tenant may pay reduced rent, pay percentage rent, or in some cases terminate its lease. The clauses protect tenants who leased space for the anchor’s traffic. For the property owner, they can create a cascade: the anchor closes, inline rent is reduced, and departures become more likely.

The right underwriting question is not simply whether co-tenancy exists. It is what the provisions say, which tenants receive the benefit, how long the cure periods are, and how the property would perform if the anchor stopped operating. The grocer’s lease and health are structural linchpins of the center’s income.

## Risks to Weigh

The defensive case does not eliminate risk. It concentrates much of it in the anchor. If the grocer’s sales decline, its parent chain has financial problems, or the store closes or relocates, the center can lose its primary traffic source. Co-tenancy provisions can then reduce inline rents or contribute to inline departures, amplifying the effect on property income.

Inline turnover is another ordinary risk. Restaurants, service businesses, and local retailers can fail or move. Vacancies, re-leasing costs, and rent roll-downs are part of owning a multi-tenant center.

Competition matters too. A new grocery store, superstore, or expanded online-grocery option in the trade area can take traffic from the anchor. A recognizable grocery brand cannot, by itself, answer the question of whether the individual location is thriving.

The DST structure adds its own limits. These interests are illiquid, fee-bearing securities offered only to accredited investors after a suitability review. Distributions are not guaranteed, and trustee restrictions limit active management. A passive structure can reduce the investor’s day-to-day burden; it can also limit the investor’s ability to respond when property conditions change.

## Key Takeaways

- Grocery-anchored DSTs can be comparatively defensive because groceries are essential, needs-based, and relatively e-commerce resistant, creating recurring foot traffic.
- The grocery anchor’s traffic can support inline tenants, so its health affects the income of the whole center.
- Co-tenancy clauses can reduce inline rents or permit termination if the anchor goes dark, which concentrates risk in the grocer.
- Due diligence should cover grocer credit and store sales, co-tenancy terms, location and demographics, lease structure, and the DST itself.

## Evaluating a Grocery-Anchored DST

Start with the anchor. Review the financial health and credit of the grocery chain, the individual store’s sales performance, its remaining lease term, and its rent escalations. Store-level sales per square foot are a particularly useful indicator of whether a location is thriving. A high-performing store run by a healthy chain with a long lease is not the same risk as a marginal store approaching a short lease expiration.

Then study the rest of the center. Look at the inline tenant mix: diversity, credit, and the balance between needs-based services and discretionary retail. Read the co-tenancy clauses. Map lease expirations across the rent roll. A center can look diversified on a tenant list and still be vulnerable if several leases react to the same anchor event.

Location and demographics are central. Population, household income, daytime population, traffic counts, site visibility, accessibility, and competing stores in the trade area influence whether the grocer can keep performing and whether the center can remain leased. A dense, growing, higher-income trade area with strong traffic is different from a thin, declining, or over-stored market.

Finally, review the DST vehicle. Consider the sponsor’s record, fees, projected hold period, projected distributions, and debt. Projected distributions are not guaranteed. A disciplined review joins the real estate and the structure; a good-looking center can still be a poor fit if the financing, fees, or liquidity terms do not work for the investor.

The practical standard is simple: start your due diligence at the anchor. A thriving store from a sound grocery chain on a long lease can support a more defensive case. A fragile store can make a property look safer than it is.

## Grocery-Anchored DSTs in a 1031 Exchange

Grocery-anchored DSTs are frequently used as replacement property in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). After selling appreciated investment real estate, an exchanger generally has 45 days to identify replacement property and 180 days to close. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) holds the proceeds.

Because a DST property is already acquired and packaged, it can often be identified and closed within days. That can make it useful to an exchanger working against the deadlines or needing a backstop when another identified property may fail to close. It does not remove the need to follow the exchange rules precisely.

An offering can also matter for debt replacement. If the relinquished property carried a mortgage, a leveraged grocery-anchored DST can use non-recourse financing to help replace that debt and value. If there is no debt to replace, a debt-free grocery-anchored DST can avoid creating mortgage [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html).

Some exchangers use a grocery-anchored DST as one part of a replacement portfolio, pairing it with industrial, multifamily, or net-lease DSTs. That can spread exposure among property types while keeping the exchange capital in deferred real-estate replacement property. It does not make diversification a guarantee against loss.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a grocery-anchored retail DST?

A grocery-anchored retail DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust that holds a shopping center whose largest tenant is a grocery store. Smaller inline tenants can include a pharmacy, restaurant, salon, coffee shop, or service business. Investors own fractional beneficial interests in the trust.

Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, those interests are treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. That can allow an exchanger to defer capital-gains tax while moving into passive, income-producing retail real estate. The case for the property type is that an essential grocer can generate steady traffic for inline tenants and center income. The structure remains a passive, illiquid, fee-bearing security, offered only to accredited investors after suitability review, with no guaranteed distributions.

### Why are grocery anchors considered defensive?

Food is a needs-based purchase, so grocery spending can hold up better in a downturn than discretionary retail categories. People may reduce travel, dining out, and large-ticket spending, but they still need groceries. Grocery sales are also relatively physical: shoppers often want to select fresh food, avoid delivery fees and substitutions, and shop on their own schedule. Pickup and delivery commonly still run through the store.

That is why the term “recession-resilient” is more accurate than “recession-proof.” A well-located store can draw repeat visits, but grocery competition, a weak chain, online delivery pressure, or a poor trade area can still hurt performance.

### How does the grocery anchor support the inline tenants?

A grocery store is a high-frequency destination tenant, commonly visited once or twice a week. Its traffic passes the smaller businesses around it, including pharmacies, dry cleaners, nail salons, sandwich shops, and coffee bars. Those tenants gain potential customers where people already shop for food and may pay higher per-square-foot rent for that traffic.

The grocer benefits from a more convenient shopping experience, while the inline tenants benefit from the traffic. The result can support leasing demand, occupancy, and rents across the center. It also means a healthy grocer matters to the whole property, not merely to its own lease.

### What is co-tenancy in a grocery-anchored center?

Co-tenancy refers to lease provisions that tie an inline tenant’s obligations to the anchor’s continued presence and operation. If a grocery anchor goes dark for a defined period, an inline tenant may be able to pay reduced or percentage-based rent, or even terminate its lease.

The clauses protect the inline tenant because it leased the space in part for the traffic the anchor brings. They create a property-level risk because the loss of the anchor can produce rent reductions and departures at the same time. Review the co-tenancy terms closely when evaluating an offering.

### Are grocery-anchored DSTs 1031-eligible?

Grocery-anchored retail DST interests can be 1031-eligible. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, the fractional beneficial interests in a DST are treated as direct interests in like-kind real property. An investor who sells appreciated investment real estate may be able to reinvest into a grocery-anchored DST and defer capital-gains and depreciation-recapture tax while moving to passive ownership.

The DST can use non-recourse financing to help replace debt from relinquished property. To preserve deferral, the exchange must follow the 45-day identification and 180-day closing timelines through a qualified intermediary. Baker 1031 does not give tax advice; investors should confirm their facts with a tax advisor.

### What are the main risks of a grocery-anchored DST?

The primary risk is anchor concentration. If the grocer’s sales decline, its parent chain weakens, or the store closes or relocates, the center can lose its traffic driver. Co-tenancy can then reduce inline rent and increase departures.

Inline turnover, vacancies, re-leasing costs, rent roll-downs, and new grocery, superstore, or online-grocery competition are additional risks. DSTs are also illiquid, fee-bearing, accredited-only securities with no guaranteed distributions and limited active-management flexibility. The defensive traits reduce certain risks; they do not remove them.

### How do I evaluate the grocery anchor?

Review the grocery chain’s financial health and credit, then focus on the individual store. Sales per square foot can indicate whether that particular location is thriving. Also examine the remaining lease term and whether the lease has scheduled rent escalations.

A high-performing store operated by a financially sound grocer, with a long lease and escalations, is generally a stronger profile than a marginal store on a short lease with uncertain renewal. These measures help evaluate whether the center’s traffic engine is durable.

### Why does location matter so much for these centers?

Location affects whether the grocer can keep producing sales and whether the center can stay leased. The relevant trade area includes nearby population, household income, daytime population, traffic counts, visibility, access, and competing grocery options.

A dense, growing, higher-income trade area with good traffic may support sales and foot traffic more reliably than a thin or declining market. A newer or larger grocer nearby can still pull customers away. A recognizable anchor name does not solve a weak or over-stored location.

### How long do grocery-anchored DSTs typically last?

Like many DSTs, a grocery-anchored DST may have a projected hold of roughly five to seven years. The actual period depends on the sponsor’s business plan and market conditions. During the hold, the DST owns and operates the center, collects rent, and distributes net income. Those distributions are projected, not guaranteed.

At the end of the hold, the sponsor generally sells the center and investors receive their share of the proceeds. A 1031 investor can then choose another exchange into a DST or other like-kind property, or cash out and recognize gain. Because a DST has little or no secondary market, an investor should plan for the full hold and confirm the specific plan in the offering documents.

### Are the distributions from a grocery-anchored DST guaranteed?

No. A DST distributes net rental income after expenses and debt service. That depends on the anchor staying open and paying rent, inline tenants remaining leased and current, and costs remaining in line with projections.

If the anchor weakens or closes, co-tenancy lowers inline rent, vacancies rise, or expenses increase, the available distribution can decline or be suspended. A projected distribution rate is an estimate based on assumptions, not a promise. DST illiquidity means an investor cannot simply sell because income disappoints. Review assumptions and size and diversify the position accordingly.

### How is a grocery-anchored DST different from a single-tenant net-lease DST?

A single-tenant net-lease, or NNN, DST holds a property leased to one tenant that commonly pays taxes, insurance, and maintenance under a long lease. The income can be simple and bond-like, but it is entirely dependent on one tenant.

A grocery-anchored DST holds a multi-tenant center. The grocer is the anchor, but inline tenants also pay rent, creating a broader income base. That can cushion the loss of a single inline tenant, but it also introduces turnover, co-tenancy provisions, and rent-roll-management issues. The center is not as diversified as it may first appear because much of its performance still rides on the anchor.

### Who can invest in a grocery-anchored DST?

Grocery-anchored DSTs are generally Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors. Accredited status may be based on income over $200,000 individually, or $300,000 jointly, in each of the past two years with an expectation of the same, or net worth over $1 million excluding a primary residence, among other criteria.

An investor also completes a broker-dealer suitability review that considers financial circumstances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. DST interests are private placements with minimums and a subscription process; they are not traded on an exchange or sold to the general public.

### Does a grocery-anchored DST use debt?

It depends on the offering. A leveraged DST uses non-recourse mortgage debt. For a 1031 investor who needs to replace relinquished-property debt, that leverage can help match the exchange’s debt-replacement requirement. It can also amplify returns and risk.

A debt-free DST owns the center without mortgage debt. It avoids refinancing and interest-rate risk and may have a lower-risk profile, but it does not replace relinquished debt and may offer more modest returns. Match the debt structure to the exchange facts and risk tolerance, and review the offering documents.

### Is grocery-anchored retail really e-commerce-resistant?

Grocery is more resistant to e-commerce than many retail categories, but it is not immune. Physical stores retain demand because shoppers may want to choose produce, meat, and perishables, avoid delivery fees and substitutions, and buy items promptly. The store may also fulfill pickup and delivery orders.

Online grocery, curbside pickup, and delivery still create competitive pressure. Grocers need effective omni-channel operations. Evaluate how the anchor is adapting and whether the trade area faces heavy online or delivery competition.

## Glossary

**Grocery-Anchored DST:** A DST holding a shopping center anchored by a grocery store.

**Anchor Tenant:** The large primary tenant, here the grocer, that drives traffic.

**Inline Tenant:** A smaller store leasing space around the anchor.

**Co-Tenancy Clause:** A lease term tying an inline tenant’s rent to the anchor’s presence.

**Going Dark:** When an anchor closes its store but may still pay rent.

**Defensive Real Estate:** Property with demand that tends to hold up through downturns.

**Needs-Based Tenant:** A tenant selling essential goods such as groceries.

**Foot Traffic:** The flow of shoppers the anchor draws to the center.

**Trade Area:** The surrounding market from which a center draws customers.

**Sales Per Square Foot:** A store-level measure of how well a location performs.

**Rent Escalation:** A scheduled increase in rent over a lease term.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.

**Rev. Rul. 2004-86:** The IRS ruling treating DST interests as like-kind real property.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Regulation D offerings.

**Suitability Review:** The broker-dealer’s assessment of whether a DST fits an investor.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** DST mortgage debt that can replace a 1031 investor’s relinquished debt.
