# Self-Storage REITs Explained

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how ordinary the self-storage decision feels. Someone moves, clears an estate, merges households, or simply runs out of room. A unit gets rented, often one month at a time. Those small decisions become a large rent roll when they occur across thousands of units and many facilities.

A self-storage REIT owns and operates the facilities where households and businesses store belongings, inventory, and equipment. The appeal is a particular combination: demand that has tended to hold up across cycles, lean operating costs, and short leases that allow frequent rent adjustments. The counterweight is local supply. Storage can be relatively cheap and quick to build, so a strong market can attract enough new facilities to pressure occupancy and rents.

The first-order view is simple: life-event demand and high margins make storage a defensive income sector. Second-order thinking asks which markets have limited new supply, how rapidly existing-customer rents are changing, how durable the facility locations are, and whether a REIT's balance sheet and share price already reflect the good news. The distinction matters because a sound sector can still be a poor investment at the wrong price or in the wrong submarket.

## What Self-Storage REITs Own

A self-storage REIT is an equity Real Estate Investment Trust that owns and operates storage facilities. Those facilities may be climate-controlled or conventional buildings divided into individual units, rented to households and businesses to hold belongings, inventory, or equipment. Like other equity REITs, it collects rent, covers operating costs, and distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders. REITs must pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

The income engine is straightforward: thousands of small units across many facilities, generally rented on short, often month-to-month terms. That creates a diversified rent roll. No single customer usually supplies a large share of revenue, unlike a property that depends on a few large tenants.

The facilities range from drive-up units in suburban and rural areas to multi-story, climate-controlled buildings in dense urban markets. A storage REIT may own properties outright, manage third-party-owned facilities for a fee, acquire existing facilities, or develop new ones. Brand, location, and management quality all influence occupancy and the rates a facility can charge.

## Resilient, Recession-Tested Demand

Storage demand is often tied to life events that do not stop when the economy slows. The familiar shorthand is the four Ds: death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing. Settling an estate, separating a household, relocating for a job or move, and moving into a smaller home can all require temporary storage.

There is also the ordinary tendency to accumulate possessions and keep paying for space once belongings are in a unit. That can make occupancy sticky: a tenant may be slow to move items out, even when a modest rate increase arrives. In some downturns, relocation and downsizing can add demand as well.

This has given the sector a defensive, recession-tested reputation. It is not recession-proof. Occupancy and rates can still soften. A serious downturn can lead some tenants to reduce spending and clear units, slow household formation, or pause accumulation. Oversupply built during a strong period can make that downturn more difficult for the local operator. Treat resilience as a general historical tendency, not a guarantee.

## Low Op-Ex Economics

Storage is known for a lean cost structure. A facility may need only one or two onsite employees, and some locations use unattended, technology-run operations. There are no kitchens, complex tenant build-outs, or tenant-improvement allowances to fund. Routine maintenance is often limited to paved lots, roll-up doors, security systems, lighting, and basic upkeep.

That can leave a large share of each rent dollar available for operating income. Low capital-expenditure needs also mean less cash is consumed re-tenanting or renovating space, which can support cash flow and distributions when facilities remain full. This is a structural advantage relative to more labor- or service-intensive real estate.

It does not eliminate risk. Property taxes, insurance, marketing, and the cost of competing with new supply still matter. Margins compress if occupancy or rates decline. Lean costs improve the starting point; they do not turn income into a promise.

## Supply & Competition Risk

The main offsetting risk is supply. Storage generally requires less complicated construction, infrastructure, and entitlement work than many other property types. When a local market has high occupancy and rising rents, developers can add facilities quickly. New supply can exceed local demand and force operators to compete through lower rates or concessions.

The risk is intensely local because most tenants rent within a few miles of home. A REIT can look healthy at the national portfolio level while individual submarkets are struggling with new competitors. Lease-up at newly built facilities can also pressure nearby properties before the new space is absorbed.

Market selection and barriers to entry therefore matter. Scarce land or constraints on new development can support more durable pricing. Markets that are easy to build in may be more vulnerable to overbuilding during strong periods. The sector is also fragmented: large REITs compete with independent and regional operators, and existing owners can weaken street rates through aggressive pricing even without a new building.

Review permit data, the new-supply pipeline, and competitor pricing in key markets. They are part of testing whether occupancy and rate growth are likely to hold up.

### Key Takeaways

- Self-storage demand has a resilient, recession-tested reputation because the four Ds and consumer accumulation create demand across cycles.
- Few employees, light maintenance, and modest capital needs can support high margins and cash flow when facilities stay full.
- Short, month-to-month leases can allow frequent rate increases in a healthy market.
- Low barriers to construction create local supply and competition risk that can reduce occupancy and rents.

## Short Leases and Pricing Power

The defining feature of storage revenue is the short lease. A month-to-month agreement resets frequently, allowing a REIT to raise existing-tenant rates often, sometimes more than once a year, rather than waiting for a long lease to roll. That differs from a [net-lease REIT](net-lease-reits-explained.html), where an owner may wait years for a lease reset.

Sticky occupancy can strengthen that pricing power. Tenants who do not want to move stored belongings may accept a modest increase, so well-run operators can push rates in strong markets while holding occupancy. Operators commonly use street rates to attract new tenants, then raise existing-customer rates over time. The rent roll becomes responsive to local demand.

The flexibility cuts both ways. In an oversupplied or weak market, operators may need to lower rates quickly to preserve occupancy, and tenants can leave on short notice. Short leases reward favorable markets and capable management, while exposing an operator to fast downside when conditions deteriorate.

## Evaluating Storage REITs

Begin with the markets. Since demand is local and supply risk sits at the submarket level, examine the new-supply pipeline in the REIT's principal markets, the barriers to entry, and whether occupancy and rates are rising or weakening. A REIT concentrated in supply-constrained, high-barrier markets may have more durable pricing than one with heavy exposure to easily overbuilt areas.

Then look at occupancy levels and trends, realized rate growth, including street rates and existing-customer increases, and the quality and location of the facilities. Review FFO and AFFO—[the cash-flow measures REIT investors use](reit-investing-for-beginners-how-to-get-started.html)—rather than relying only on standard net income. Balance-sheet strength, debt, and distribution coverage matter too.

Management execution is central in this fragmented, operationally intensive business. Consider its record on acquisitions, development, and rate management. Storage REIT shares are securities, and both share prices and distributions can fluctuate. These are evaluation factors, not predictions. Verify current conditions before drawing conclusions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a self-storage REIT?

A self-storage REIT is an equity REIT that owns and operates storage facilities. Its buildings are divided into individual units rented, usually month-to-month, by households and businesses that need space for belongings, inventory, or equipment. It collects rent, covers operating costs, and distributes most of its taxable income to shareholders because REITs must pay out at least 90% of taxable income.

Its income is diversified across thousands of tenants rather than concentrated in a few. Storage REITs may own facilities, develop new ones, or manage third-party facilities for fees. The sector is known for life-event demand, lean operating costs, and short leases that allow frequent rate changes.

### Why is self-storage demand considered resilient?

Much demand comes from life events rather than only economic growth. Death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing can create temporary storage needs in any economic climate. People also accumulate possessions, and tenants can be slow to remove them once placed in storage. That can make occupancy sticky.

The combination has historically helped storage hold up reasonably well in recessions. In some downturns, relocation and downsizing can add demand. It is not recession-proof. Occupancy and rates can soften, so current market conditions should be checked rather than assumed.

### What are the four Ds of self-storage demand?

The four Ds are death, divorce, dislocation, and downsizing. Death may require settling an estate and sorting or storing belongings. Divorce separates a household and can create temporary storage needs. Dislocation includes relocations, job changes, and moves between homes. Downsizing occurs when a household consolidates into a smaller space and needs room for what does not fit.

The framework captures events that happen in good economies and bad. Along with consumer accumulation, they help establish a baseline of demand less tied to the economic cycle than many other property types. They do not guarantee an individual facility's result.

### Why do self-storage REITs have high margins?

The operating cost of a storage facility is often low. Some need only one or two onsite employees, and some use unattended, technology-run models. There are no kitchens, complicated build-outs, or tenant-improvement allowances. Maintenance is usually limited to lots, roll-up doors, security systems, lighting, and basic upkeep.

Low re-tenanting and capital-expenditure needs can let a large share of each rent dollar reach operating income, supporting cash flow when facilities stay full. Property taxes, insurance, marketing, new supply, and lower occupancy or rates can still compress margins. The lean profile is an advantage, not a guarantee.

### How do short leases give storage REITs pricing power?

Storage leases typically reset month to month. That permits frequent increases for existing tenants, often more than once a year, rather than waiting for a multi-year lease to expire. When demand is strong, sticky occupancy can help operators raise rates while retaining tenants. Street rates may be used to attract new customers, while existing-customer rates rise over time.

The same short structure can require quick rate cuts when a market is weak or overbuilt, and tenants can leave on short notice. Pricing power depends on healthy demand and disciplined supply. It rewards good markets and management, but it also creates fast downside exposure in poor ones.

### What is the biggest risk for self-storage REITs?

Supply and competition risk is the main sector-specific concern. Storage can be relatively cheap and quick to build, so developers can add facilities when a local market shows high occupancy and rising rates. New supply can exceed demand, forcing lower rates and hurting occupancy.

Most tenants rent within a few miles of home, so the risk is concentrated in the local trade area or submarket. A REIT's national portfolio may be healthy while specific markets face new competition. Review the new-supply pipeline, barriers to entry, and nearby lease-up activity in the REIT's key markets.

### Are self-storage REITs recession-proof?

No. The sector has a defensive, recession-tested reputation because life events and consumer accumulation have historically supported occupancy. Relocation and downsizing can even increase demand in some downturns.

But a serious recession can lead some tenants to clear units, slow household formation, and reduce accumulation. New supply constructed in a strong period can worsen the situation in affected markets. Storage has real cyclical and market risk. Returns are not guaranteed, and its historical resilience should not be treated as a promise.

### How do self-storage REITs make money?

They earn rent from thousands of small units across many facilities, usually on month-to-month terms. After low operating costs, they distribute most taxable income to shareholders as dividends under the 90% distribution rule. A REIT may also manage third-party facilities for fees and receive ancillary revenue from tenant insurance, locks, packing supplies, and late fees.

Growth can come from raising rates, lifting occupancy, acquiring existing facilities, and developing new ones. The core engine remains short-term rent from a diversified group of tenants, with much of the income passed through to shareholders.

### What should I look at when evaluating a storage REIT?

Start with the markets. Review the new-supply pipeline and barriers to entry in key submarkets, then ask whether occupancy and rates are rising or softening. Examine facility quality and location, occupancy levels and trends, street-rate changes, and existing-customer rate increases.

Also review FFO and AFFO, balance-sheet debt, and distribution coverage. Management's record on acquisitions, development, and rate management matters because execution drives results. Share prices and distributions can fluctuate, past performance does not guarantee future results, and current market conditions should be verified.

### What is FFO and why does it matter for storage REITs?

FFO, or funds from operations, is a primary cash-flow measure for REIT investors. It starts with net income, adds back real estate depreciation, a large non-cash expense that can understate a property's earning power, and removes gains or losses on property sales. The result offers a clearer view of recurring operating cash flow than standard net income alone.

AFFO, or adjusted funds from operations, refines FFO by subtracting recurring capital expenditures and certain other items. For a storage REIT, FFO and AFFO help show whether cash flow covers the dividend and how cash generation is changing with occupancy and rates. Compare those measures, their growth, and distribution coverage.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange into a self-storage REIT?

No. REIT shares, including self-storage REIT shares, are securities rather than like-kind real property, so they are not eligible for a direct 1031 exchange. An investor cannot sell investment real estate and directly reinvest the proceeds in storage REIT shares to defer capital-gains tax.

There is an indirect route to eventual REIT exposure. An investor may complete a 1031 exchange into a Delaware Statutory Trust, which can be 1031-eligible real property. A REIT may later acquire the DST property through a 721 (UPREIT) exchange, potentially converting the interest to operating-partnership units and eventually REIT shares while preserving deferral. Confirm the specifics and eligibility with a tax advisor; Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

### How are self-storage REIT dividends taxed?

They are taxed like other REIT dividends. Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income rather than at lower qualified-dividend rates because the REIT itself paid no corporate tax. A 20% deduction under Section 199A applies to qualified REIT dividends, which lowers the effective top federal rate on those dividends.

Some distributions may be return of capital, which reduces cost basis rather than creating current tax, or capital-gain distributions, which are taxed at capital-gains rates. The REIT reports the breakdown each year on Form 1099-DIV. The mix of ordinary income, return of capital, and capital gains varies year to year. Confirm current rules and their application with a tax advisor.

### Are self-storage REITs a good income investment?

They can be income-oriented because REITs distribute most taxable income as dividends, while storage's lean cost structure and high margins can support cash flow when facilities stay full. Its recession-tested demand profile also appeals to investors seeking income less tied to the broad economic cycle.

Income is not guaranteed. Distributions can be cut, occupancy and rates can fall in oversupplied markets, and share prices fluctuate. Storage should not be treated as bond-like. An investor should size and diversify any storage REIT allocation appropriately and verify current conditions rather than rely on past yields.

### How does competition affect self-storage REITs?

Competition is central because the sector is fragmented and barriers to entry are low. Large REITs compete with many independent and regional operators. A local market can change quickly when several owners seek the same tenants.

New development is the major threat: strong markets attract new facilities, which can exceed demand and pressure occupancy and rates. Existing operators can also drag down street rates through price competition. Since storage is local, competition plays out submarket by submarket. Review permits, supply pipelines, and competitor pricing before deciding how durable a REIT's occupancy and rate growth may be.

## Glossary

- **Self-Storage REIT:** An equity REIT that owns and operates self-storage facilities.
- **Equity REIT:** A REIT that owns property and earns income from rents.
- **Storage Facility:** A building of individual units rented to store belongings.
- **Climate-Controlled Unit:** A storage unit with regulated temperature and humidity.
- **Month-to-Month Lease:** A short lease that resets monthly, enabling frequent rate changes.
- **Street Rate:** The advertised rate used to attract new storage tenants.
- **Occupancy:** The share of a facility's units that are rented.
- **Sticky Occupancy:** The tendency of tenants to keep belongings stored long-term.
- **The Four Ds:** Death, divorce, dislocation, downsizing—drivers of storage demand.
- **Operating Margin:** Operating income as a share of revenue; high for storage.
- **Low Op-Ex:** The minimal staffing and maintenance costs of storage.
- **Supply Risk:** The risk that easy new building oversupplies a local market.
- **Barriers to Entry:** Factors that limit new storage development in a market.
- **FFO:** Funds from operations—a REIT's core cash-flow measure.
- **AFFO:** Adjusted FFO, net of recurring capital expenditures.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The requirement to pay out at least 90% of taxable income.
