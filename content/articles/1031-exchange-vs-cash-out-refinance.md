# 1031 Exchange vs. Cash-Out Refinance

By Jerry Baker · Updated June 2026 · 11 min read

I keep seeing equity decisions framed as though cash-out refinancing and a 1031 exchange are substitutes. They solve different problems. A refinance keeps the property and borrows against it; a 1031 sells it and moves the full value into another real-estate investment.

First-order thinking is, “Both avoid an immediate tax bill.” Second-order thinking is, “Do I need liquidity while retaining this asset, or do I need to change the asset itself—and can I accept more leverage, less liquidity, or strict exchange execution risk?”

## Two ways to unlock equity

A cash-out refinance replaces an old loan with a larger one, pays off the old loan, and delivers the difference as tax-free cash because loan proceeds are not taxable income. The investor retains the property, its income, appreciation, depreciation, and other tax attributes, but takes on more debt, debt service, rate-reset risk, and loan closing costs.

A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) sells the property and reinvests proceeds into replacement real estate. A full exchange reinvests everything and defers gain; it does not provide cash in hand. It is a tool for trading up, diversification, changing markets or property types, or becoming passive through a DST—not for liquidity from a property the investor wants to keep.

## Risk, tax, and goal comparison

Refinancing leaves embedded gain in the property for a future sale, exchange, or possible step-up at death. It gives tax-free cash, but not tax deferral. A 1031 defers the four-layer gain tax and redeploys pre-tax value, but requires meeting the deadlines, choosing a replacement, and accepting its new-asset risk. A partial exchange can distribute cash, but that cash is taxable boot; see [partial 1031 exchange taking cash out](partial-1031-exchange-taking-cash-out.html).

Refinance fits liquidity and retention. Exchange fits repositioning and growth. They can be sequenced: exchange first, then later refinance the replacement as a genuinely separate transaction. A refinance before a closely related sale has greater risk of being recharacterized as boot; review timing in [refinance before or after a 1031 exchange](refinance-before-after-1031-exchange.html) with CPA and tax counsel.

> A refinance gives tax-free cash and retains the property, at the cost of more debt. An exchange gives a new property and deferred tax, but not cash from the original asset.

## Key Takeaways

- Cash-out refinance means liquidity while retaining the asset, funded by more leverage.
- A 1031 means repositioning and growth with deferred tax, not cash in hand.
- Exchange first and refinance the replacement later is generally more conservative than a pre-sale refinance tied to the exchange.

## Comparison at a glance

| Factor | Cash-out refinance | 1031 exchange |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original property | Keep it | Sell and replace it |
| Cash today | Tax-free loan proceeds | None in a full exchange |
| Embedded gain | Remains in property | Deferred on qualifying trade |
| Principal risk | More debt, debt service, and rate-reset exposure | Deadlines, qualifying rules, and new-asset underwriting |
| Best fit | Liquidity while retaining | Repositioning, trade-up, diversification, or passivity |
| Transaction costs | Loan origination, appraisal, title, and closing costs | Sale and purchase costs plus QI fees |

## Which should you choose?

Choose refinance when you want cash for another investment, an expense, or an opportunity while retaining a property you want to own. Choose an exchange when you want to trade up, diversify, change markets or property types, or move toward passive ownership with the full value at work. Neither is universally better. The decision is cash and retention versus repositioning and deferral.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which gives cash?
Refinance. It produces tax-free loan proceeds while keeping the asset; a full exchange reinvests all proceeds, and cash from a partial exchange is taxable boot.

### Which defers gain?
The exchange. A refinance leaves the embedded gain in place.

### Which risk is greater?
Refinance adds leverage and debt-service risk. Exchange brings timing, qualification, and replacement-asset risk.

### Can I use both?
Yes—generally exchange first, then refinance the replacement later with appropriate separation and advice.

### Does refinance change basis or depreciation?
No. Borrowing does not reset basis or depreciation. An exchange carries basis into replacement under carryover/excess rules.

### Can I refinance a DST?
Generally not at the investor level; sponsor-controlled financing and illiquidity limit that route.

### What's the difference between a 1031 exchange and a cash-out refinance?
A refinance is keep-and-borrow: cash from a larger loan while ownership remains. An exchange is trade-and-defer: a new property with deferred gain rather than cash.

### How does a cash-out refinance work?
It replaces the existing loan with a larger loan, repays the old loan, and gives the owner the difference as non-taxable loan proceeds that must be repaid.

### How does a 1031 unlock equity?
It reinvests the entire qualifying value, including deferred tax, in replacement real estate. It unlocks equity through repositioning, not a cash distribution.

### Which should I choose?
Choose refinancing for cash while keeping the asset; choose exchange for tax-deferred repositioning or growth. They can be used in sequence.

### Is it safe to refinance after a 1031 exchange?
Generally, refinancing the replacement after completion and reasonable separation is the safer sequencing. A pre-sale refinance closely tied to the exchange has greater boot risk.

### What happens to deferred gain if I refinance and never exchange?
It remains embedded until a future taxable sale, later exchange, or death. If held until death, a step-up can erase gain and heirs may refinance or sell using the stepped-up basis.

### Which has lower transaction costs?
Refinance has loan costs but no sale or purchase costs. An exchange has sale and purchase costs and QI fees, but it changes the portfolio and defers tax.

## Glossary

- **Cash-Out Refinance:** Larger replacement loan that releases tax-free borrowing proceeds.
- **1031 Exchange:** Tax-deferred exchange of qualifying investment real property.
- **Loan Proceeds:** Borrowed cash that is not taxable income but must be repaid.
- **Boot:** Cash or other non-like-kind value, taxable up to gain.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Debt relief not offset by new debt or cash.
- **Leverage:** Debt used against property, increasing debt-service risk.
- **DST:** Passive, illiquid securitized real-estate replacement.
- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing qualifying gain through an exchange while full proceeds remain invested.
- **Equity:** Property value above debt; refinancing borrows against it while exchange redeploys it.
- **Repositioning:** Exchanging into a different, larger, diversified, or passive real-estate asset.
- **Liquidity:** Cash available for use; refinancing creates it without selling.
- **Embedded Gain:** Appreciation still inside a retained property and taxable at a future sale unless exchanged or eliminated by a step-up.
- **Debt Service:** Required principal and interest payments on the larger refinance loan.
- **Post-Exchange Refinance:** Later borrowing against replacement property; safer when genuinely separate in time and purpose.
- **Trade-Up:** Exchange into a larger or better replacement using full pre-tax value.
- **Partial Exchange:** Exchange that distributes some cash; the cash is taxable boot.
- **Step-Transaction Risk:** Risk that connected steps, such as a pre-sale refinance, are treated together and cash as boot.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax deferred by a qualifying exchange.
- **DST:** Passive repositioning option; securities are illiquid and sponsor financing is not investor-refinanceable.
