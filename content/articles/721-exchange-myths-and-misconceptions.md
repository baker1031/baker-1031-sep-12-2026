# 721 Exchange Myths and Misconceptions


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I keep noticing how quickly a 721 exchange gets reduced to a slogan. It is either treated as a tax trick with no drawbacks or as a permanent loss of control and liquidity. Neither version is useful.

The 721 exchange has real benefits and real constraints. The common myths are that it is the same as a 1031 exchange, that money can never come out, that it is tax-free forever, that the owner loses everything to the REIT, that it is reserved for the ultra-wealthy, and that a step-up in basis is unreliable. The facts require more care than the slogans.

The first-order view is simple: tax deferral, passive ownership, and diversification sound attractive. The second-order view is what changes in exchange—direct property and control become a stake in a REIT portfolio, liquidity may require taxable conversion after a lock-up, and outcomes depend on current law, the REIT, and the investor’s own situation. The right approach is neither oversell nor dismissal. It is a clear view of the trade-offs. This is educational material, not tax, legal, or investment advice.

## Myth: It’s the same as a 1031 exchange

A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) and a 1031 exchange both can defer gain, but they are fundamentally different. A 1031 exchange trades like-kind real property for other real property. The owner remains in direct real estate, retains control, and may exchange again. A 721 exchange contributes property to a REIT operating partnership for [OP units](op-units-explained.html), creating REIT ownership that is generally a one-way move; an owner cannot 1031 out of OP units.

The resulting ownership is different. A 1031 preserves direct real-estate ownership, control, and flexibility. A 721 moves the owner into a diversified, passive REIT interest and a one-way commitment. The similarity in tax deferral should not obscure the difference in what is held, who manages it, and what flexibility remains.

## Myth: You can never get your money out

OP units are not directly tradable, and a 721 is generally one-way. That does not mean the capital is locked away forever. After the applicable lock-up, OP units can be converted into REIT shares. For a traded REIT, those shares can be sold for cash.

The important qualifications are timing and tax. Conversion is not immediate, and it triggers the deferred gain. The liquidity path can be real and can be used gradually with planning, but it is neither immediate nor tax-free. That is very different from saying no liquidity exists.

## Myth: It’s completely tax-free forever

A 721 exchange is tax deferral, not a permanent exemption at contribution. The gain is deferred while the units are held, but converting units to shares triggers the deferred gain and is taxable.

There is an estate-planning distinction. If an investor holds units until death, a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can erase the deferred gain for heirs. That can produce permanent elimination of the gain, but only if units are held until death rather than converted during life. The accurate description is deferral, with elimination possible through the step-up—not automatic tax-free treatment forever.

## Myth: You lose everything to the REIT

Contributing property does mean giving up the particular asset and direct control. It does not mean giving up the value. The contributor receives OP units worth the contributed property: an ownership stake in the REIT portfolio that carries the deferred gain, may pay distributions, may be converted for liquidity, and can be passed to heirs.

The asset has changed form. The owner becomes a passive REIT investor rather than a direct owner and operator. That is an important trade, but it is not the loss of wealth. The units are a valuable stake in the REIT.

## Myth: It’s only for the ultra-wealthy

The usual securities-based 721 path is available to [accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html), not only to the ultra-wealthy. Many apartment, commercial, and other real-estate owners with substantial appreciated property and meaningful equity meet the income or net-worth standards.

The strategy’s complexity and securities structure can make it look exclusive. The more accurate statement is that it is available to a broad group of accredited real-estate owners, while still requiring accredited-investor status and a suitability review.

## Key Takeaways
- A 721 and a 1031 are fundamentally different: a 721 produces REIT ownership and is generally one-way, while a 1031 keeps direct real estate and flexibility.
- Liquidity may be available through conversion after a lock-up, but the conversion is taxable.
- A 721 defers gain; elimination is possible through a step-up at death, not automatic.
- The owner receives a REIT stake rather than losing wealth, and the strategy is broadly available to accredited investors rather than only the ultra-wealthy.

## Myth: The step-up isn’t reliable

The step-up in basis is current, established law. It applies to inherited property, including OP units, and resets an heir’s basis to the date-of-death value, which can erase deferred gain. It is a standard, long-standing tax provision rather than a speculative benefit.

The qualification matters: tax laws can change. Future availability is not guaranteed indefinitely, just as no tax benefit is immune from legislative change. Under current law, however, the step-up is real and reliable. Investors should verify the current rules as part of any tax planning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 721 exchange the same as a 1031 exchange?

No. Both can defer gain, but a 1031 trades like-kind real property for other real property, preserves direct ownership and control, and can be repeated. A 721 contributes property to a REIT for OP units and generally cannot be reversed with a 1031 out of the units. The outcomes are direct real estate with flexibility versus diversified, passive REIT ownership with a one-way structure.

### Can I never get my money out of a 721 exchange?

No. OP units can be converted into REIT shares after a lock-up, and shares of a traded REIT can be sold for cash. The conversion triggers deferred gain and cannot occur immediately, so the liquidity path requires planning. It is available, but it is taxable and subject to the lock-up.

### Is a 721 exchange tax-free forever?

No. It is a deferral strategy. The contribution does not currently recognize gain, but conversion of units to shares triggers the deferred gain. Holding units until death can allow a step-up to eliminate gain for heirs, but that result is conditional and is not automatic when units are converted during life.

### Do I lose my wealth to the REIT in a 721 exchange?

No. The owner receives OP units worth the contributed property, representing a stake in the REIT. The units carry the value, may pay distributions, can be converted after the lock-up, and can pass to heirs. The owner gives up the specific property and direct control, but receives a valuable ownership interest rather than losing wealth.

### Is the 721 exchange only for the ultra-wealthy?

No. It is generally open to accredited investors, a group that includes many owners of substantial appreciated apartment, commercial, and other real estate. The strategy is sophisticated and requires accreditation and suitability, but it is not limited to an ultra-wealthy group.

### Is the step-up in basis reliable?

Under current law, yes. It is a well-established, long-standing provision that can apply to inherited OP units and erase deferred gain for heirs. Tax laws can change, so investors should verify current law, but the step-up is established rather than speculative today.

### Is a 721 exchange a scam or too good to be true?

No. It is a legitimate, established strategy based on Section 721 of the tax code and is used in the REIT industry. It has genuine benefits—deferral, diversification, passivity, and estate planning—and genuine limitations, including its one-way nature, conversion tax, and investment risks. It should be evaluated honestly with professional guidance, using facts rather than hype or fear.

### Does a 721 exchange avoid all real estate risk?

No. The investor remains in real estate through the REIT and is still exposed to real-estate markets, interest rates, and REIT performance. A diversified portfolio can reduce single-property concentration risk, but it does not eliminate systematic real-estate risk and adds dependence on the REIT’s performance.

### How do I separate the facts from the myths?

Use knowledgeable professionals—a financial advisor, CPA, and attorney experienced with 721 exchanges—and authoritative sources rather than simplified claims. Consider both sides: the potential benefits of deferral, diversification, passivity, and estate planning, and the limitations of a one-way structure, conversion tax, and investment risk.

### Myth: a 721 exchange lets me pick which properties I own. Is that true?

No. The investor contributes property for OP units, which represent a stake in the REIT’s whole portfolio. The investor does not choose specific properties to own. That pooled portfolio interest can be a diversification benefit, but it replaces property-specific ownership and control. An owner who wants to select and own particular properties should recognize that a 721 is not that structure.

### Myth: the REIT can take my units away. Can it?

No. OP units are the investor’s ownership interest in the operating partnership. The investor holds the units, receives distributions, and controls whether and when to convert after the lock-up. Partnership terms can include lock-up, conversion, and sometimes tax-protection provisions, but the REIT does not simply confiscate the units. The partnership agreement governs the holder’s rights and should be reviewed.

### Myth: doing a 721 exchange is quick and simple. Is it?

Not exactly. The idea can be easy to state, but execution—especially through a DST-to-721 path—requires structuring, securities suitability, 1031 deadlines when a DST bridge is used, tax analysis, and selection of the REIT or DST. It warrants careful planning with a financial advisor, CPA, and attorney. The work is manageable with the right team, but it is real and should not be rushed.
