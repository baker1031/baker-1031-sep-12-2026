# Hotel & Hospitality DSTs

I keep seeing hotel real estate presented as though a familiar brand name makes the income behave like rent. It does not. A hotel sells the next night again and again, and that is a very different economic engine from a building with long leases.

Hotels can offer higher return potential, but they are among the most variable, cyclical, and economy-sensitive real estate sectors. A hospitality Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can give an accredited investor passive, fractional exposure to one or more hotels. It does not turn a hotel into a fixed-income investment.

First-order thinking says that an established flag, a strong recent travel market, or an attractive projected distribution is the story. Second-order thinking asks what has to remain true for that projection to hold: demand, room rates, costs, new supply, financing, the operator, and the investor's ability to withstand a downturn. That is where the risk sits.

## How Hospitality DSTs Work

A hospitality DST works like other DSTs, but the real estate is hotel property. The trust holds title to one or more hotels. A sponsor structures the offering and arranges financing, and [accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html) buy fractional beneficial interests. A properly structured DST interest is treated as like-kind real property for a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. An investor selling other investment real estate may therefore exchange into a hospitality DST and defer capital-gains tax while gaining passive exposure to the hotel sector.

The investor is passive. The operating work belongs to professionals. That distinction matters because a hotel is not a simple property that collects fixed tenant rent. Rooms are repriced nightly. There are employees, brand standards, reservations, guest service, and meaningful operating expenses.

DST rules restrict the trust from actively operating a business. Hospitality DSTs therefore usually use an operating arrangement, often a master lease to an operating entity or a management structure. A professional operator runs the hotel under a brand or flag while the trust receives income from the arrangement.

The ordinary DST features still apply: trustee restrictions, a roughly five-to-seven-year hold, illiquidity, and fees. What changes is the character of the income. It depends heavily on how the hotel performs.

> A hotel is not a leased building collecting fixed rent. It is an operating business, and a hospitality DST's income rises and falls with that business.

## Why Hotel Income Is Variable

Most net-lease retail, multifamily, and industrial properties produce income under leases that set rent for a period of time. A hotel effectively leases each room for one night. The rooms are repriced and re-booked continually, so revenue resets with occupancy and room rates.

Hotel performance is commonly measured through revenue per available room, or RevPAR. RevPAR combines occupancy with average daily rate (ADR). Both move with demand. A full hotel at weak rates and a less-full hotel at premium rates can report very different underlying conditions, even before costs enter the picture.

Business and leisure travel link that demand directly to the economy. In a downturn, occupancy and room rates can fall quickly. Many costs—staff, maintenance, utilities, and brand fees—do not fall nearly as fast. The result is high operating leverage: profits can rise sharply in a strong travel market and compress just as sharply in a weak one.

This is why hotel income is more volatile than the income in long-leased property types. A hospitality DST distribution is a projection, not a promise. It can fluctuate or be interrupted as operating performance changes.

## Flag, Location & Operator Factors

Three questions drive much of a hotel's performance: what brand flies over the door, what market the hotel serves, and who runs it.

The flag is the hotel brand. A recognized flag can bring reservation systems, loyalty programs, marketing reach, and a reputation that can support occupancy and rates. Its segment matters, too. Luxury, upscale, midscale, and economy hotels address different customer bases and can perform differently through the cycle.

Location determines the demand base. A market supported by business travel, tourism, conventions, an airport, and major employers has more diverse demand than one reliant on a single driver. Supply is just as important. New hotel construction can pressure occupancy and rates even if overall travel demand remains healthy.

Then there is the operator. Hotels are management-intensive. An experienced operator manages staff, protects brand standards, controls costs, and adjusts pricing to demand. A poor operator can underperform even with a sound brand and market. A good operator cannot guarantee the outcome.

> In hospitality, the essential questions are the brand, the market, and the operator. They should be considered together, not one at a time.

## Risk Considerations

Hospitality is a higher-risk sector. The central risk is economic sensitivity. Because revenue resets nightly and depends on travel, a recession or downturn can reduce occupancy, rates, and revenue quickly. High operating leverage magnifies the effect on profit.

Travel disruptions are another defining risk. Economic shocks, health events, and other unexpected interruptions to travel can reduce demand suddenly and severely. Hotels have experienced these demand shocks before. They are not insulated from broader disruptions.

Supply, operating costs, and execution add more pressure points. New hotels can dilute a market. Labor- and service-intensive operations can see margins squeezed by rising costs. And the operator's execution remains central throughout the hold.

The trade-off is straightforward: a strong travel market can create more upside than a long-leased property type, while a weak market can create more downside. Hospitality DSTs are generally for risk-tolerant investors who can accept variability in distributions. Those distributions are projections, not guarantees, and they can be reduced or suspended.

## Key Takeaways

- Hospitality DSTs hold hotels operated by professionals, often under a recognized flag, with the trust receiving income through an operating arrangement.
- Hotel revenue is variable and cyclical because rooms reprice nightly. RevPAR, occupancy, and ADR respond directly to travel demand.
- Flag, location, and operator are central performance drivers, while operating leverage magnifies both upside and downside.
- Hospitality is a higher-risk, economy-sensitive sector. It may suit a risk-tolerant investor, but distributions are not guaranteed.

## Where Hospitality Fits in a 1031 Portfolio

Given that risk profile, hospitality often fits as a smaller, opportunistic portion of a 1031 strategy rather than a core holding. Many exchange investors put stability and income first, looking to net-lease property, [residential rental](single-family-rental-and-build-to-rent-dsts.html), or [warehouse and logistics](industrial-dsts-warehouse-and-logistics-investing.html). They may allocate only part of exchange proceeds, if any, to a cyclical hotel position.

DSTs can make this allocation more precise because an investor can divide exchange proceeds among multiple trusts and sectors. A hospitality DST may be the higher-return-potential, higher-risk slice beside more stable holdings. It should not become a concentration merely because the recent travel market has been favorable.

The right balance is personal. It should reflect risk tolerance, liquidity needs, overall portfolio goals, and a clear understanding that hospitality income is variable and no return is guaranteed.

## Evaluating a Hospitality DST

Start with the flag, market, and operator. Review the brand, its segment, and the strength of its reservation and loyalty systems. Review the market's business, tourism, convention, airport, and employer demand drivers, how diversified they are, and what new supply could arrive. Review the operator's track record, cost discipline, pricing capability, and experience with the specific brand and segment.

Then apply the standard DST questions with extra care. Review the amount and terms of non-recourse debt because leverage can magnify hotel volatility. Review fees, the projected hold period, and projected distributions. Hotel projections are especially uncertain because revenue resets so quickly; they are estimates, not promises.

Finally, assess suitability. Hospitality is a higher-risk, cyclical sector, and the interests are securities. A broker-dealer suitability review should determine whether the risk, expected hold, illiquidity, and allocation fit the investor's goals. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a hospitality DST?

A hospitality DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust that owns one or more hotels operated by professionals, often under a recognized brand or flag. Accredited investors own fractional beneficial interests. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a properly structured interest can be like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange, allowing an investor who sells other investment real estate to defer capital-gains tax while gaining passive hotel exposure. Because a hotel is an operating business, the DST uses an operating arrangement such as a master lease or management structure. Trustee restrictions, a roughly five-to-seven-year hold, illiquidity, and fees apply. Hospitality income is higher-risk, cyclical, and projected rather than guaranteed.

### How do hospitality DSTs work?

The trust holds the hotel, a sponsor structures the offering and financing, and accredited investors acquire fractional interests qualifying as 1031 like-kind property under Rev Rul 2004-86. A hotel has staff, brand standards, nightly pricing, and substantial operating expenses, while DST rules restrict the trust from actively operating a business. An operating entity or manager therefore runs the hotel under the flag and the trust receives income for passive investors. Standard DST restrictions, a roughly five-to-seven-year hold, illiquidity, and fees remain. Income depends on actual hotel results, not fixed lease rent.

### Why is hotel income variable?

Rooms reprice every night, unlike the multi-year leases in many other property sectors. Occupancy and ADR combine to create RevPAR, and both respond to business and leisure travel demand. In a downturn, occupancy and rates can drop quickly while staffing, maintenance, and brand fees remain relatively fixed. That high operating leverage makes profit swing more than revenue. A strong travel market can lift income; a weak market can compress it. Hospitality DST distributions are projections that may fluctuate or be interrupted, not guarantees.

### What is RevPAR?

RevPAR means revenue per available room. It combines occupancy—the share of rooms filled—with ADR, the average price paid for an occupied room. It shows how much revenue a hotel generates across all available rooms. A hotel can increase RevPAR by raising room rates, filling more rooms, or both. Because both drivers respond to demand, RevPAR can move materially with the travel and economic cycle. Reviewing the trend in a hotel's market is central to understanding its income picture.

### Are hotel DSTs higher risk?

Yes. Hotel revenue resets nightly and depends on travel, so economic weakness can reduce occupancy and rates quickly. High operating leverage amplifies the profit effect. Travel disruptions, new supply, rising operating costs, and operator execution can add to the pressure. Strong markets can create greater upside, but weak markets create greater downside. Hospitality DSTs generally suit investors who can tolerate cyclical income and potential distribution reductions or suspensions.

### What is a hotel flag?

A hotel flag is the brand under which a hotel operates. It can provide reservation systems, loyalty programs, marketing reach, and a quality reputation that support occupancy and rates. The flag also identifies a segment—luxury, upscale, midscale, or economy—with its own customer base and cycle behavior. The flag is important, but it is not enough on its own. A well-branded hotel can struggle in a weak, oversupplied market or with poor operating execution.

### Why does location matter for hotel DSTs?

Location determines where demand comes from. Business travel, tourism, conventions, a major airport, and large employers can create a more diverse demand base than a market dependent on one source. Location also shapes the supply outlook. Heavy new construction can pressure occupancy and room rates for existing hotels. An investor should ask who travels to the market, why they travel there, how diversified that demand is, and what competing supply is expected, alongside the flag and operator.

### What role does the operator play in a hospitality DST?

The operator runs the hotel day to day: staffing, brand standards, operating costs, and pricing designed to maximize RevPAR. In a hospitality DST, the operator usually works through a master lease or management structure while the trust receives income. Experience with the specific brand and segment, cost control, and pricing skill can materially affect results. A weak operator can impair a sound asset; a skilled operator still cannot remove market or travel risk.

### How do travel disruptions affect hotel DSTs?

Hotels depend directly on people traveling. Economic shocks, health events, and other disruptions can reduce occupancy and rates almost immediately because rooms are repriced nightly. Many costs remain relatively fixed, so operating leverage can make the profit impact severe. A serious travel downturn may reduce or suspend DST distributions and affect value. This vulnerability is a core reason hospitality is higher risk. No distribution or return is guaranteed against such events.

### Can I use a hotel DST in a 1031 exchange?

Yes. Fractional beneficial interests in a properly structured DST can be like-kind real property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. The usual 1031 rules still apply: use a qualified intermediary, identify replacement property within 45 days, close within 180 days, reinvest proceeds, and replace debt as needed for full deferral. The hotel operating structure is designed so the professional operator runs the business while the trust receives income. DST interests are accredited-investor securities offered through a broker-dealer, and investors should confirm exchange mechanics with their tax advisor and qualified intermediary.

### Are distributions from a hospitality DST guaranteed?

No. Distribution estimates rely on assumptions about occupancy, room rates, expenses, and financing. Actual outcomes can differ materially. Nightly revenue resets, while many costs remain fixed, so operating leverage can make cash flow available for distribution especially variable. In a serious downturn, distributions can be reduced or suspended. Treat projected distributions as estimates, not promises, and size any hospitality allocation knowing that income can fluctuate. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Where does hospitality fit in a 1031 portfolio?

For many investors, it is a measured, opportunistic allocation rather than a core position. A 1031 portfolio may prioritize the relative stability of net-lease, multifamily, or industrial property and reserve only part of proceeds, if any, for hospitality. DSTs can permit a split among multiple sectors, so hospitality may provide higher-return potential beside more stable holdings without becoming the entire position. The allocation should reflect individual tolerance, goals, and the fact that no return is guaranteed. A broker-dealer suitability review is important.

### How do I evaluate a hospitality DST?

Focus first on flag, market, and operator. Examine the brand segment and its reservation and loyalty systems; the location's business, tourism, convention, and employer demand plus new supply; and the operator's track record, cost discipline, pricing, and brand experience. Then assess debt, non-recourse financing terms, fees, projected hold period, and projected distributions. Leverage can magnify volatility and hotel projections are estimates. Confirm that an illiquid, higher-risk, cyclical sector fits the investor's portfolio and risk tolerance through a broker-dealer suitability review.

### Who can invest in a hospitality DST?

Hospitality DSTs are generally Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors. Accreditation can be based on specified income or net-worth thresholds, commonly excluding a primary residence, or on certain professional credentials. Beyond accreditation, a broker-dealer reviews financial circumstances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. That review carries added weight for hospitality because income is variable and cyclical. An investor should expect to verify accredited status and complete a thorough suitability review.

## Glossary

**Hospitality DST:** A DST owning hotels run by operators, often under a brand.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in 1031-eligible fractional beneficial interests.

**Flag:** The brand a hotel operates under, bringing systems and reputation.

**RevPAR:** Revenue per available room—occupancy times average daily rate.

**Occupancy:** The percentage of a hotel's rooms that are filled.

**Average Daily Rate (ADR):** The average price charged per occupied room.

**Operator:** The company that runs a hotel day to day.

**Master Lease:** A lease to an operating entity used to structure hotel DSTs.

**Operating Leverage:** Fixed costs against variable revenue magnify profit swings.

**Cyclical Sector:** A sector whose income rises and falls with the economy.

**Brand Segment:** A hotel's tier—luxury, upscale, midscale, or economy.

**Beneficial Interest:** A DST investor's fractional ownership treated as real property.

**Rev Rul 2004-86:** The IRS ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible like-kind property.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** Financing repaid only from the property, not investors personally.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.

**Suitability Review:** A broker-dealer's check that a DST fits the investor.
