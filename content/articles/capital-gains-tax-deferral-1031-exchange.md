# Capital Gains Tax Deferral Through a 1031 Exchange

I keep seeing a 1031 exchange described as a way to “defer capital gains.” That is true, but it is incomplete in the way a headline rate is incomplete. A properly structured exchange can postpone as many as four taxes that an outright sale may trigger. The capital that would have gone to the government remains in the investment instead.

That matters over time. Pre-tax equity can keep compounding through a series of exchanges, while the investor who sells and pays tax starts each next investment with less capital. This article is general education, not tax, legal, accounting, or investment advice. A CPA and attorney should apply the current rules to the actual property and taxpayer.

First-order thinking is to ask whether a 1031 avoids a capital-gains bill today. Second-order thinking is to calculate the whole tax stack, understand that the liability continues in the replacement property, and decide whether the replacement is still worth owning after accounting for leverage, liquidity, suitability, and risk. A tax benefit can improve a good decision. It cannot make an inferior replacement property good.

## What taxes a 1031 exchange can defer

An exchange may defer four layers that can arise on a sale of investment real estate:

- Federal long-term capital gains tax.
- State capital-gains or income tax.
- [Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), including unrecaptured Section 1250 gain.
- The **3.8%** [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), or NIIT, when it applies.

Together, those layers can consume a quarter to well over a third of a gain in a high-tax state. The federal capital-gains rate is the one people know, but a long-held, depreciated property can carry a larger recapture component, and a state tax can add another double-digit layer. The full stack—not one rate—is the economic case for deferral.

## Federal capital gains tax

Property held for more than one year is generally subject to federal long-term capital-gains rates of **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**, based on taxable income. Most investment-property sellers with meaningful appreciation are in the **15%** or **20%** bracket for the appreciation portion of gain.

The **20%** top rate applies to higher-income taxpayers. It applies to appreciation—sale price less adjusted, depreciated basis—after separating the recapture portion. A qualifying 1031 carries that federal gain into the replacement property rather than recognizing it in the current sale.

## Depreciation recapture

Depreciation deductions reduced taxable income during ownership, but they also lowered basis. At a taxable sale, the depreciation-related portion of gain, commonly called unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, is recaptured at a maximum **25%** rate. On a long-held rental, it is often the largest tax component.

That rate recovers part of the benefit of prior depreciation deductions. The longer the ownership and depreciation history, the more recapture may be embedded in the property. A 1031 can defer that exposure with the rest of the gain by carrying the basis into the replacement property rather than recognizing recapture now.

## The **3.8%** net investment income tax

Higher-income taxpayers can also owe the **3.8%** NIIT on capital gains. It sits on top of capital-gains and recapture rates when modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable thresholds. A single large sale can move a seller over a threshold even if ordinary income normally does not.

The NIIT often gets noticed only when it appears on the return, but it adds nearly four percentage points to an effective rate. When a 1031 recognizes no gain at the exchange, it can defer NIIT as well; see [how a 1031 exchange defers the NIIT](1031-exchange-net-investment-income-tax-niit.html).

## State capital-gains tax

State treatment varies. Texas, Florida, and several other states have no state income tax, leaving a seller with federal layers. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income and its top rate exceeds **13%**. For a high-income seller in a high-tax state, the state layer can exceed the federal capital-gains tax.

Some states also impose withholding or use clawback rules that affect deferred gain. Those details belong in the CPA review before the sale. A qualifying 1031 may defer the state layer too, which makes the strategy more consequential where the combined burden is highest.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - A 1031 can defer federal capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax.
> - Recapture, up to **25%**, is often the largest layer on long-held property.
> - In a high-tax state, the combined rate can exceed a third of gain; the exchange defers, rather than erases, that amount.

## How carryover basis creates the deferral

The mechanism is carryover basis. Broadly, basis is what an investor paid, plus improvements, minus depreciation. It does not reset in an exchange. The relinquished property’s adjusted basis carries into the replacement property, so the old low basis carries the deferred gain with it.

No gain is recognized at the exchange because tax law treats the investor as continuing the investment rather than cashing out. The taxes that an outright sale would have produced do not come due at that point. They remain inside the replacement property until a future taxable sale.

That is why a 1031 is deferral, not an exemption. During life, the tax is postponed. It becomes permanent elimination only if the investor keeps exchanging and a later step-up in basis at death eliminates the built-in gain.

## Why the compounding effect matters

The useful feature is not a one-time postponement. It is the ability to keep the pre-tax dollars working. Each exchange begins with a larger equity base than an equivalent sale after tax, and each subsequent return is earned on that larger amount.

Over years and repeated exchanges, the difference can become substantial. The investor who reinvests the full gain can build a meaningfully larger portfolio than one who repeatedly pays a third of the gain and reinvests the remainder. The Joint Committee on Taxation has estimated that like-kind exchanges defer billions of dollars of tax annually, evidence that investors of many sizes use the mechanism.

## “Swap till you drop” and estate planning

The estate-planning version is often called “swap till you drop.” An owner can [exchange repeatedly across a lifetime—trading up, diversifying, or moving into passive ownership—without paying the deferred tax](1031-exchange-exit-strategies-swap-till-you-drop.html), provided each exchange satisfies the rules.

When an owner dies holding the final replacement property, heirs generally receive a stepped-up basis at the property’s fair-market value on the date of death. They may be able to sell soon after inheritance with little or no taxable gain. In that circumstance, deferred gain accumulated through decades of exchanges can be eliminated for the heirs.

Late-life planning sometimes uses passive, divisible ownership to make an estate easier to divide: [Delaware Statutory Trusts](delaware-statutory-trusts.html) can be split among heirs, and a 721 UPREIT can move ownership into a REIT. Those are planning choices with separate tax, investment, suitability, and offering-document questions—not automatic answers.

## Deferral is different from elimination

A 1031 defers tax during life. It does not eliminate tax merely because an exchange occurred. If the owner sells the replacement property without another exchange, the deferred capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and applicable state tax generally come due.

Elimination comes through a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death, or in limited other circumstances. The deferred gain is therefore best treated as a liability that travels with the investment until it is recognized, reduced, or eliminated under the applicable rules. Deferral buys flexibility and time; an estate plan is needed if the objective is a step-up.

## A worked example of deferred tax

Consider a California investor selling a long-held rental with a **$500,000** gain, including **$150,000** of depreciation recapture. At **20%**, federal capital gains on the **$350,000** appreciation equals **$70,000**. Recapture at **25%** on the **$150,000** equals **$37,500**. The **3.8%** NIIT on **$500,000** equals **$19,000**. California tax at roughly **13%** on **$500,000** is about **$65,000**.

That is about **$191,500** of combined tax on a **$500,000** gain—roughly **38%**—that an outright sale would trigger and a 1031 may defer. Instead of reinvesting **$308,500** after tax, the investor can reinvest the full **$500,000** gain, plus original basis, and keep the whole amount working. The related [tax-deferral calculation](how-much-tax-does-a-1031-exchange-defer.html) illustrates the same point.

The figures are illustrative. Income, basis, and current rates control the real result. Still, the example explains why highly appreciated, heavily depreciated property in a high-tax state is often where a 1031 has its greatest deferral value.

## Exchange versus an outright sale

After an outright sale, four tax layers can leave only about two-thirds of a high-tax-state gain available for reinvestment. After a qualifying exchange, the full pre-tax amount goes back to work.

Imagine two investors, each with a **$500,000** gain and the same return over the next decade. The seller who paid roughly **$190,000** starts with about **$310,000** of that gain invested. The exchanger starts with the full **$500,000**. Ten years of compounding widens the gap beyond the original tax bill because the deferred tax also kept earning.

It can be useful to view deferred tax as an interest-free, indefinite loan from the government. It is repaid if the owner eventually sells without exchanging, and it may not be repaid if a step-up at death intervenes. That framing is economic, not a guarantee: it underscores why time horizon, replacement quality, and estate goals matter.

## How deferral varies by property type

The carryover-basis mechanism is the same across investment real estate, but the amount deferred depends on the property’s history. A long-held rental with substantial depreciation has a large recapture component, so more—and more highly taxed—gain is deferred than in a recent acquisition with little depreciation.

Raw land has no depreciation recapture if it generated no depreciation. Its deferred tax is primarily capital gain, plus NIIT and state tax when applicable. Commercial and multifamily property can accumulate recapture as they are depreciated. Oil-and-gas mineral and royalty interests that qualify as real property follow the same basic logic, although percentage depletion lowers basis and can create depletion recapture.

The strongest arithmetic case is therefore often long-held, heavily depreciated, highly appreciated property in a high-tax state. That does not answer the investment decision. The replacement needs to stand on its own.

## Common misconceptions

It is a mistake to call a 1031 permanent tax elimination. It is normally a lifetime deferral, with a possible step-up at death as a separate estate-planning outcome. It is also a mistake to count only federal capital gains and ignore recapture, NIIT, and state tax, which can be the largest pieces.

Cash is not tax-free simply because an exchange is underway. Cash taken out, or debt not replaced, can be taxable [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html). Deferral applies to value actually reinvested in like-kind property. And it is not restricted to large institutions; investors from single-rental owners to large portfolios use it when the facts fit.

## When deferral makes sense

Deferral tends to make sense when embedded gain is meaningful, the owner wants to remain in real estate, and there is a clear reason to preserve the capital: diversification, passive income, trading up, or estate planning. The larger the gain and the higher the tax state, the greater the potential value of postponement.

It may make less sense when basis is high, cash is genuinely needed, or the owner cannot find a replacement worth owning. The correct discipline is to run the specific numbers with a CPA, maintain the exchange rules, and decline a replacement that does not meet the investment standard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What taxes does a 1031 exchange defer?

A 1031 can defer federal capital gains tax at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**; state capital-gains tax from **0%** to over **13%**; depreciation recapture up to **25%**; and the **3.8%** NIIT. The conditions include reinvestment in like-kind property through a qualified intermediary.

### How much can a 1031 exchange save?

It defers combined federal and state capital gains, recapture, and NIIT. In a high-tax state, that can exceed a third of the gain. On a **$500,000** gain, the amount deferred can be roughly **$150,000–$190,000**.

### Does a 1031 exchange eliminate capital gains tax?

No. It defers the tax during life. The gain carries in the replacement basis and generally becomes due if the replacement is sold without another exchange. A step-up in basis at death can eliminate it for heirs, subject to the applicable rules.

### How does carryover basis work?

The relinquished property’s adjusted basis carries into the replacement rather than resetting. Because gain is value minus basis, that low basis carries deferred gain forward and no gain is recognized at the exchange.

### Why is compounding deferral so powerful?

Pre-tax equity remains invested. The equity base is larger each cycle, and the deferred tax keeps earning returns. Over repeated exchanges, that can produce more wealth than paying a third of gain and reinvesting the remainder each time.

### What is “swap till you drop”?

It is repeated exchanges throughout life, followed by holding the final property until death. A step-up in basis can then eliminate deferred gain for heirs, turning lifetime deferral into a permanent result for them.

### Does a 1031 defer depreciation recapture?

Yes. Recapture on the depreciated portion, potentially taxed up to **25%**, is deferred with capital gain and NIIT through carryover basis when the exchange qualifies.

### Does the **3.8%** NIIT apply to a property sale?

It can apply when modified AGI exceeds the relevant thresholds. It stacks on top of capital gain and recapture, and a qualifying exchange can defer it when no gain is recognized.

### Is a 1031 more valuable in high-tax states?

Often, yes. California’s high income-tax rates can make the state layer the largest tax on gain. In Texas or Florida, where there is no state income tax, the deferral is federal only.

### What happens to deferred tax when I eventually sell?

If the replacement property is sold without another exchange, deferred capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax generally come due. Repeated exchanges and a final hold through death can lead to a step-up in basis for heirs.

### Can I defer gain across multiple exchanges?

Yes. Each qualifying exchange can defer accumulated gain, permitting an owner to trade up, diversify, or shift to passive ownership over a lifetime without recognizing the deferred tax at each exchange.

### When does a 1031 exchange make sense for deferral?

When gain is meaningful, the owner wants to stay in real estate, and the use of retained capital is clear. It is less compelling with high basis, a need for cash, or no worthwhile replacement property.

### How does deferral pair with estate planning?

Holding the final replacement until death can give heirs a stepped-up basis. Passive, divisible DST interests or a 721 UPREIT into a REIT can ease estate division while the owner preserves deferral, but each option requires its own planning and review.

### Does a 1031 reduce my current income tax?

It does not reduce ordinary income tax. It can defer the capital gain, recapture, NIIT, and state tax that an investment-property sale would otherwise add to the tax bill.

### How do I estimate deferred tax?

Calculate appreciation at the capital-gains rate, recapture on the depreciated portion up to **25%**, the **3.8%** NIIT if applicable, and state tax. A calculator and CPA can refine the estimate.

### Is deferring tax really better than just paying it?

For an investor with meaningful gain and a suitable replacement, deferral preserves more capital to compound. In a high-tax state it may preserve about two-thirds versus a much smaller after-tax reinvestment amount. The trade-off is that the tax remains deferred rather than forgiven.

### Does deferral work the same for raw land as for a rental?

Carryover basis works the same. A heavily depreciated rental has a recapture layer; raw land has no depreciation and therefore no recapture, so its deferred tax is mainly capital gain, NIIT, and state tax when applicable.

### Will I ever pay the deferred tax?

Generally, only if the replacement is sold without another exchange. If exchanges continue and the final property is held to death, a step-up in basis can eliminate the deferred gain for heirs.

### Is the 1031 deferral only for big investors?

No. A single-rental owner and a large portfolio owner can both use a qualifying 1031 when they sell investment real estate with embedded gain.

### How does deferral compare to a Qualified Opportunity Fund?

Both can defer capital gain but operate differently. A 1031 defers gain from investment real estate by reinvesting all proceeds in like-kind real property and can pair with an estate step-up. A Qualified Opportunity Fund can defer—and potentially partly reduce—gain from any source by reinvesting the gain, and a decade of appreciation on the new investment can be tax-free. The appropriate tool depends on source of gain, horizon, and goals.

### Does a 1031 exchange affect depreciation going forward?

Yes. The carried-over, depreciated basis continues to be depreciated in the replacement property. Additional basis from new investment is depreciated separately. A CPA tracks both schedules because later depreciation again lowers basis and can create recapture that a subsequent exchange could defer.

## Glossary

- **Tax Deferral:** Postponing tax rather than eliminating it; the core benefit of a 1031 exchange.
- **Carryover Basis:** The relinquished property’s adjusted basis carried into the replacement property, preserving deferred gain.
- **Long-Term Capital Gains:** Gains on property held more than a year, federally taxed at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by income.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Gain from prior depreciation, up to **25%** as unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, which a 1031 can defer.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A **3.8%** federal surtax on certain investment income for higher-income taxpayers.
- **State Capital Gains Tax:** State-level tax on gains, from **0%** to over **13%** depending on the state.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** A reset of basis to fair-market value at death that can eliminate deferred gain for heirs.
- **Swap Till You Drop:** Repeated exchanges held until death, when a step-up can eliminate deferred gain.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Cost plus improvements minus depreciation; subtracted from value to find gain.
- **Realized Gain:** Sale proceeds minus adjusted basis; the amount potentially taxed without an exchange.
- **Compounding:** Earning returns on full pre-tax equity each cycle, the long-term effect of deferral.
- **721 UPREIT:** A contribution of property to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, often used as a later-life deferral path.
- **Deferral vs. Elimination:** Deferral postpones tax during life; elimination may occur through a step-up at death.
