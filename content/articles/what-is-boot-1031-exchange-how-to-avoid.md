# What Is 'Boot' in a 1031 Exchange (and How to Avoid It)?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing 1031 exchange planning framed as a binary choice: either the exchange works or it does not. That misses a very useful middle ground. An exchange can be valid and still produce current tax on part of the value.

That taxable piece is boot. It is usually cash an investor keeps or debt the investor does not replace. It does not undo the like-kind exchange; it is the portion that is not deferred. This guide covers what boot is, its two main forms, tax and netting rules, worked examples, partial exchanges, and ways to design a zero-boot result.

First-order thinking asks whether the sale price is high enough to make a cash withdrawal tempting. Second-order thinking asks what that withdrawal, a lower replacement value, or less debt will do to current tax, carryover basis, liquidity, and the choices still available after closing. Price is only one ledger. Equity and debt are the others.

## What Is Boot?

Boot is non-like-kind value received in an exchange. A fully deferred exchange moves the investment entirely into like-kind real property of equal or greater value. When the investor receives something else—cash, debt relief, or another kind of property—that additional value is boot.

The term comes from the old phrase “to boot,” meaning something given in addition. Here, it marks the part of the transaction that looks like a cash-out rather than a continuation of the investment.

Receiving boot does not blow up the exchange. The like-kind portion can still qualify for deferral. Only the boot is generally taxable, which is why a partial exchange can be a valid, intentional choice when an investor understands its tax cost.

## Why Boot Exists

The rule follows the equal-or-greater-value requirement. To defer the full gain, an exchanger generally reinvests all equity, acquires replacement property of equal or greater value, and replaces debt paid off at sale. Boot measures the shortfall from those targets.

Keep sale proceeds and you have cashed out value. Reduce debt without contributing equivalent cash and you have received economic value in the form of debt relief. Section 1031 defers gain only to the extent the investment continues; value removed from the investment is the part that is currently recognized.

That makes boot less mysterious than it sounds. Whenever something of value is received and not reinvested in like-kind real property, expect a boot question and plan for it before closing.

## Cash Boot

Cash boot is net sale equity that does not go back into replacement property. Assume a $600,000 sale leaves $400,000 of equity after debt is paid off. If only $350,000 is reinvested, the $50,000 kept is cash boot.

It can be intentional: perhaps the investor needs funds for a personal purpose. It can also happen by accident. A less-expensive replacement can leave funds with the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html), which the QI releases at the end of the exchange period. Those remaining funds are cash boot, generally taxable up to gain.

Precise reinvestment matters when full deferral is the objective. A DST can accept a specific dollar amount, which may help an investor invest a remainder rather than leave excess cash behind.

## Mortgage Boot

Mortgage boot, also called debt-relief boot, is quieter and often overlooked. It is the amount by which debt paid off on the relinquished property exceeds debt assumed on replacement property, after considering additional cash contributed.

Suppose the relinquished property has a $300,000 mortgage and the replacement has only a $200,000 loan. The investor has $100,000 of debt relief. Unless the investor contributes an additional $100,000 of cash, that $100,000 is mortgage boot even though no check arrived in the investor’s hands.

The reasoning is economic: being released from a liability is treated as value received. That is why “replace the debt” belongs beside “reinvest the equity.”

## How Boot Is Taxed

Boot is taxable up to realized gain. If realized gain is $300,000 and boot is $50,000, the $50,000 is generally recognized while the remaining gain remains deferred. If boot exceeds realized gain, recognition is capped at the actual gain; the exchange does not create gain beyond that amount.

The character of recognized gain matters. [Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), which can be taxed up to 25%, is generally recognized before lower-rate capital gain. A modest amount of boot can therefore cost more than an investor expects if much of the gain is recapture.

Higher-income taxpayers can also face the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html) on recognized boot, and state tax may apply. A CPA should evaluate the particular transaction and taxpayer. This is educational information, not tax, legal, accounting, or investment advice.

## The Boot Netting Rules

Cash and debt do not net symmetrically. Added cash can offset debt relief. New borrowing can also replace paid-off debt. But taking on more debt does not erase cash boot once cash has been taken out.

Put another way: an investor may contribute cash to cure a debt shortfall, but cannot borrow extra on the replacement to cure sale proceeds already withdrawn. Full deferral generally means no cash boot because all equity is reinvested, and no mortgage boot because debt is replaced with new debt, cash, or both.

The calculation applies to the exchange as a whole, not one property at a time. An investor may sell one property and buy several, or sell several and buy one. The IRS nets cash and debt across the full transaction. A second replacement—or a fractional [DST](1031-exchange-into-dst.html)—can absorb a remaining amount of equity or debt, but total cash taken out remains boot and cannot be cured by borrowing elsewhere.

## Common Sources of Boot

The usual sources are straightforward: buying a cheaper replacement, taking a smaller loan, or taking cash at closing. A cheaper purchase often creates both leftover cash and less replacement debt.

Other sources are easier to miss. Exchange funds used for non-transaction costs or unrelated obligations can be boot, even though some ordinary transaction closing costs may be permissible. Personal property bundled with a real-estate purchase, seller financing, and other non-like-kind consideration can also create boot.

Prorated rents, security deposits, and certain closing credits can produce small amounts when handled incorrectly. An experienced QI and CPA can review the settlement structure before funds move.

## Worked Example: Full vs. Partial Deferral

Consider a $600,000 sale with a $200,000 mortgage. The sale leaves $400,000 of equity, and realized gain is $350,000.

**Scenario A: boot.** The investor acquires a $500,000 replacement with a $100,000 loan. The investor has kept $100,000 of cash and reduced debt by $100,000. That is roughly $200,000 of taxable boot, recognized only up to the $350,000 gain and potentially taxed partly as recapture.

**Scenario B: zero boot.** The investor acquires a $600,000 replacement with a $200,000 loan, reinvests all $400,000 of equity, and replaces all $200,000 of debt. There is no boot, and the entire $350,000 gain is deferred.

The sale and gain are identical in each case. The replacement structure decides whether roughly $200,000 is currently taxable. These figures are illustrative, but the value-and-debt logic is the real issue.

### Key takeaways

- Boot is non-like-kind value received, most often cash kept or debt not replaced.
- It is taxable up to realized gain and can be recognized first as depreciation recapture.
- A zero-boot exchange generally reinvests all equity and replaces all debt.

## Strategies to Avoid Boot

Start with both ledgers. Reinvest all net equity, acquire replacement property of equal or greater value, and replace debt paid off through new financing, added cash, or a combination of the two.

Do the math before the closing table. Know the target value and the debt-replacement amount while evaluating property. A leveraged DST can be useful because its pre-arranged, non-recourse debt may replace leverage and its exact investment amount can help use all available equity.

When multiple replacements are involved, add their acquisition value, equity reinvested, and debt assumed. The combined amounts should meet or exceed what was relinquished, with no leftover cash and no unreplaced debt if full deferral is the goal.

## Intentional (Partial) Exchanges

Taking boot can be deliberate. A partial exchange allows an investor to take some cash for a personal need or portfolio rebalancing while deferring the reinvested portion. The price is current tax on the boot.

The useful distinction is between intentional liquidity and accidental liquidity. An investor considering a partial exchange should model the after-tax result with a CPA, including the possibility that recapture is recognized first. In some cases, deferring the full gain and accessing liquidity later through a replacement-property refinance may be more efficient than taking taxable boot now.

## Boot and DSTs

Delaware Statutory Trusts can address boot in two ways. A DST can accept an exact amount of equity, reducing the chance of leftover cash. A leveraged DST can also carry pre-arranged, non-recourse debt at the trust level, which may help replace debt without a new loan application.

That precision makes a DST a potential “top-off” investment. If a direct replacement leaves equity unused or debt unreplaced, a DST may absorb the remaining amount and provide needed leverage.

DSTs are sold only to accredited investors through a [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html). They are speculative, illiquid securities with substantial risk, including possible loss of principal. A tool that solves a value-matching problem is not automatically a suitable investment.

## Boot Mistakes to Avoid

The most common error is replacing the equity but forgetting the debt. Another is buying less expensive property without recognizing that both leftover cash and reduced debt may be boot.

Investors also sometimes assume more borrowing offsets cash taken out. It does not. Using exchange proceeds for unrelated obligations or improper expenses can create boot, as can allowing a QI-held remainder to be released at the end.

The remedy is simple but not casual: calculate value and debt needs before closing with the CPA and QI. Then either structure for zero boot or take boot knowingly as part of an intentional partial exchange.

## Boot in Special Situations

Seller financing requires care. A note received from the buyer of relinquished property is generally non-like-kind consideration and can be treated as boot unless the exchange is structured carefully.

Personal property is another issue. Since 2017, only real property is like-kind for Section 1031. Furniture, equipment, and other personal property included in a deal can be boot to the extent of their allocated value. Allocation between real and personal property at closing matters.

Excess borrowing is not itself boot, but cash extracted through that borrowing can be. Related-party and multi-property exchanges also require a full cash-and-debt calculation because results may not be apparent deal by deal. The common thread is the same: identify every form of consideration, then work with the CPA and QI before signing.

## Reporting Boot on Form 8824

Boot is reported on Form 8824, the IRS form for like-kind exchanges. It calculates realized gain, recognized gain (the taxable boot), and basis in the replacement property.

Because recognized gain may include recapture before capital gain, the character must be reported correctly. The calculation also affects carryover basis, which affects future tax. Keep closing statements showing values and debt on both sides, the QI’s accounting, and evidence of any cash released.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is boot in a 1031 exchange?

Boot is any non-like-kind value you receive—typically cash you keep (cash boot) or debt relief you do not replace (mortgage boot). It does not disqualify the exchange, but it is taxable up to the amount of realized gain.

### What's the difference between cash boot and mortgage boot?

Cash boot is sale equity you do not reinvest: money you keep. Mortgage boot is debt relief: the amount by which debt paid off exceeds the debt or cash put into the replacement. Both are taxable.

### How is boot taxed?

Boot is taxable up to realized gain and is generally taxed first as the highest-rate component: depreciation recapture, potentially up to 25%, before lower-rate capital gain. Higher-income taxpayers may also owe 3.8% NIIT, plus any state tax.

### How do I avoid boot in a 1031 exchange?

Reinvest all equity, acquire replacement property of equal or greater value, and replace all debt paid off with new financing or offsetting cash. A leveraged DST can supply matching debt and allow a precise investment amount with no leftover cash.

### Does taking boot disqualify my exchange?

No. The like-kind portion still qualifies for deferral, and only the boot is taxed. This is why a partial exchange—deliberately taking some boot—is a valid choice.

### Can I offset cash boot by taking on more debt?

No. The netting rules are asymmetric: added cash can offset debt relief, but increasing debt cannot offset cash already taken out. Cash boot stands on its own once cash is received.

### What is mortgage boot and how is it triggered?

Mortgage boot is debt relief, triggered when debt paid off exceeds debt or offsetting cash on the replacement property. Paying off a $300,000 loan and taking only a $200,000 loan creates $100,000 of mortgage boot unless $100,000 of cash is added.

### Is boot always taxable?

Boot is taxable up to realized gain. If there is gain and boot is received, the boot is generally taxed while the rest of the exchange stays deferred. If there is no gain, boot may not be taxed, though that is uncommon in an appreciated-property exchange.

### What is a partial 1031 exchange?

A partial exchange is one in which an investor deliberately takes some boot—for example, cash for a personal need—while deferring the rest of the gain. The reinvested portion is deferred and tax is paid on the boot; it is a legitimate choice when liquidity is worth the tax.

### How do DSTs help avoid boot?

A DST lets an investor invest a precise dollar amount, eliminating leftover-cash boot, and, if leveraged, supplies pre-arranged non-recourse debt that replaces old leverage and eliminates mortgage boot. DSTs are often used to top off an exchange and close the exact gap that would otherwise be boot.

### Are closing costs considered boot?

Many ordinary transaction closing costs paid from exchange funds are not boot, but using exchange funds for non-transaction expenses or unrelated obligations can create boot. The QI and CPA should structure the closing so legitimate costs do not inadvertently generate it.

### What happens to leftover exchange funds?

If all proceeds are not reinvested, the qualified intermediary releases leftover cash at the end of the exchange period, and it is taxable cash boot up to gain. Reinvest all equity to avoid this; a DST can accept the exact remaining amount.

### How is boot reported to the IRS?

Form 8824 calculates realized gain, recognized taxable gain equal to boot, and carryover basis in replacement property. A CPA should ensure the recognized gain’s character—recapture first—and basis are correct.

### Can boot be taxed at a higher rate than capital gains?

Yes. Boot is generally recognized first as depreciation recapture, taxed up to 25%, which can be higher than the long-term capital-gain rate. A modest amount of boot can therefore cost more than expected if much of the gain is recapture.

### Should I ever take boot on purpose?

Sometimes. If liquidity is the objective, a partial exchange permits cash while deferring the rest. Model the after-tax result with a CPA and compare it with full deferral followed by liquidity through a later replacement-property refinance.

### Does adding my own cash to the exchange help?

Yes. Additional cash can offset debt relief and help reach equal-or-greater value, supporting full deferral. Many investors contribute cash to replace debt they do not want to carry as new financing.

### Is seller financing considered boot?

It can be. A note taken back from the buyer of relinquished property is generally non-like-kind consideration and may be boot unless structured carefully within the exchange. Discuss seller financing with the QI and CPA before agreeing to it.

### Is personal property included in a deal boot?

Yes, to the extent of its allocated value. Since 2017, only real property is like-kind, so furniture, equipment, and other personal property bundled into replacement property are non-like-kind and count as boot. Careful allocation between real and personal property at closing can minimize it.

### Can I have boot in a multi-property exchange?

Yes. In an exchange into or out of multiple properties, cash and debt are netted across all of them, and shortfalls can produce boot in ways that are not obvious without the full calculation. A CPA should net the entire transaction.

### How do I know if I'll have boot before closing?

Run the value-and-debt math before closing: compare total replacement value, equity reinvested, and debt assumed with the relinquished property. Reinvesting all equity and replacing all debt into equal-or-greater value produces zero boot; the CPA and QI should confirm the figures.

## Glossary

**Boot:** Non-like-kind value received in an exchange, including cash kept and unreplaced debt, taxable up to gain.

**Cash Boot:** Sale equity not reinvested in replacement property.

**Mortgage Boot:** Debt relief not offset by replacement debt or added cash.

**Realized Gain:** Total gain on sale, which caps the taxable amount of boot.

**Recognized Gain:** Gain currently taxed—generally boot, up to realized gain.

**Netting Rules:** Rules under which cash can offset debt relief, while debt cannot offset cash boot.

**Depreciation Recapture:** Gain attributable to prior depreciation, generally recognized first and potentially taxed up to 25%.

**Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** Requirement that replacement value be at least the relinquished-property value for full deferral.

**Partial Exchange:** An exchange that deliberately takes taxable boot while deferring the rest.

**Leveraged DST:** A DST with pre-arranged, non-recourse debt that can help replace debt and accept a precise investment.

**Debt Relief:** Release from a liability, treated as value received and a source of mortgage boot.

**Form 8824:** IRS form used to report a like-kind exchange, including recognized gain and carryover basis.

**Carryover Basis:** Relinquished-property basis carried into replacement property, adjusted for boot.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% surtax that can apply to recognized boot for higher-income taxpayers.
