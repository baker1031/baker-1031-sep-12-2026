# Common Mistakes in a 721 / UPREIT Exchange


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

Tax deferral often captures the attention in a 721 decision. The harder questions are equally important: What happens if the owner later wants control back? What REIT is actually being accepted? What tax bill accompanies liquidity? A 721 / UPREIT exchange has real benefits, but its common pitfalls can lead to regret and poor outcomes.

First-order thinking says deferral, diversification, and future liquidity. Second-order thinking starts with the generally permanent commitment to REIT ownership: the loss of 1031 flexibility, the specific sponsor and portfolio now carrying the outcome, and the tax and planning work that still remains. The errors below are the ones worth avoiding.

## Not understanding the one-way nature

The most consequential mistake is entering a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) without understanding that it is largely one-way. The move shifts the owner into REIT ownership; it generally ends 1031 eligibility and does not permit a tax-free return to direct real estate. An investor who treats the 721 as tentative or reversible can regret it when circumstances or preferences change.

The error usually begins when the benefits—deferral and diversification—receive more attention than the commitment. The owner may not fully appreciate that they are [giving up the ability to keep exchanging real property or to return to direct ownership tax-free](721-exchange-risks-and-drawbacks-to-know.html). Once the contribution is made, the flexibility the investor wishes they had retained cannot be recovered tax-free.

The remedy is certainty before contribution. Treat the 721 as a generally permanent move into REIT ownership and confirm that this is the desired outcome, not an experiment. An owner who still needs flexibility can keep some assets in direct real estate. Committing without that clarity is costly because the one-way nature is the strategy's defining feature.

## Failing to evaluate the REIT

A 721 does not merely select a tax structure. It selects a particular REIT. That REIT's portfolio, management, performance, and financial health determine the holder's returns and value. Yet investors sometimes study the strategy's benefits and skip the diligence on the actual destination.

The consequences are straightforward. A weak, poorly managed, or unsuitable REIT can reduce returns and unit value, and the owner is largely committed to it. Evaluate portfolio quality and diversification, sponsor and management track record, [financial health](how-to-read-a-reits-ffo.html), fees, and whether the REIT is traded or non-traded. Those factors control both the investment outcome and the practical liquidity experience.

Choose a strong, well-managed REIT that fits the investor's goals. This diligence must happen before the contribution, because the specific REIT matters as much as the 721 structure. The investor is not doing a 721 in the abstract; the investor is accepting a proportional interest in that REIT.

> You are not just doing a 721 exchange. You are committing, largely irreversibly, to a specific REIT whose quality will shape your outcome.

## Overlooking the conversion tax

Liquidity through [OP units](op-units-explained.html) is not free liquidity. Converting OP units to REIT shares for liquidity generally recognizes deferred gain. An owner who assumes the units can be accessed without tax can be surprised by the bill at conversion.

That surprise creates poor liquidity planning: converting too much at once, or allocating cash needs without allowing for tax. Conversion can trigger the deferred four-layer tax, including recapture, so the cost can be substantial.

Plan this at the outset. Recognize that conversion is taxable, model the result with the CPA, convert gradually when appropriate, consider timing in favorable tax years, and hold units not needed for liquidity toward a potential step-up. The point is not to avoid the issue; it is to make liquidity access and its tax cost part of the same plan.

## Neglecting tax protection

Tax protection deserves attention in the contribution documents. If the operating partnership sells contributed property in a taxable manner, the owner's deferred built-in gain may be triggered unexpectedly. A tax-protection agreement is the REIT's agreement not to sell the contributed property taxably for a period, and it helps protect the deferral.

Investors can miss this by overlooking the term during negotiations or by not understanding why it matters. Without adequate protection, a partnership sale could accelerate tax before the investor intended, defeating the deferral sought through the 721.

Work with counsel to negotiate and understand an appropriate agreement: its scope, its duration, and whether it provides meaningful protection for a reasonable period. Tax protection is not boilerplate. It is a core term that guards the deferred gain, and neglecting it can undermine the tax strategy.

## Ignoring suitability and fit

The 721 is not for every owner. It can suit owners moving into passive REIT ownership, including tired landlords, estate planners, concentrated owners, and liquidity seekers. It can be a poor fit for [active, control-valuing investors](who-should-not-do-a-721-exchange.html) who still want to choose properties, operate them, or preserve 1031 flexibility.

The mistake is to be attracted by deferral and diversification without asking whether the loss of control and one-way commitment are acceptable. An active owner can be drawn by tax deferral, make the exchange, and later regret surrendering control—a poor fit that should have been visible before closing.

Assess fit honestly. Consider whether the investor's objectives match the 721 benefits and whether the trade-offs are tolerable. A 1031 may suit an owner who wants control and flexibility. The 721 should match the person, not merely the tax feature.

## Key Takeaways

- Not understanding the one-way nature—and committing without certainty—is the most consequential mistake. Be certain before contributing.
- Failing to evaluate the REIT, to which the outcome is tied, and overlooking taxable conversion are common errors.
- Neglecting tax protection leaves deferral exposed; ignoring suitability can create a poor-fit, regrettable commitment.
- Inadequate professional guidance compounds every other error. Use an advisor, CPA, and attorney.

## Inadequate professional guidance

Proceeding without qualified help is the sixth mistake, and it magnifies the other five. A 721 involves securities, tax mechanics, legal documentation, and partnership structure. It is significant and generally irreversible. The process calls for a financial advisor on suitability and fit, a CPA on tax, and an attorney on structuring, documentation, and tax protection.

An investor working alone or with inadequate advice may not understand the one-way limitation, vet the REIT properly, model conversion tax, negotiate protection, or test fit. Each missing piece can lead to the other mistakes.

Assemble the team before proceeding: an experienced advisor, CPA, and attorney who can address the strategy, its taxes, and the documentation. Professional guidance is not optional decoration around an irreversible choice. It is the safeguard that helps prevent the common errors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the biggest mistake in a 721 exchange?

Not understanding the generally irreversible one-way nature before committing. The owner loses 1031 eligibility and cannot tax-free return to direct real estate. Be certain before contributing; this mistake is hard to undo because the 721 is generally permanent.

### Why is evaluating the REIT important?

The REIT's performance, management, and financial health determine the holder's returns and unit value. A poor choice is difficult to reverse, so evaluate portfolio quality, diversification, management, track record, financials, fees, and structure before contribution. The decision is an investment in a specific REIT, not just a 721 strategy.

### What is the conversion tax mistake?

It is assuming that liquidity is freely available and overlooking that an OP-unit conversion to REIT shares recognizes deferred gain. This can produce poor liquidity planning, such as converting too much at once. Plan gradual conversions, timing, and units to hold for a potential step-up with the tax in mind.

### What is tax protection, and why not neglect it?

It is the REIT's agreement not to sell contributed property in a taxable manner for a stated period. It protects deferred gain from a partnership sale. Without it, that sale could trigger tax unexpectedly. Work with counsel to negotiate appropriate scope and duration.

### How do I know if the 721 exchange fits me?

Test the benefits against the trade-offs. It can fit owners ready for passive REIT ownership; it may not fit active owners who value control and flexibility. If the loss of control or one-way nature is unacceptable, a 1031 may be more suitable.

### Do I really need professional guidance?

Yes. The strategy needs a financial advisor for suitability and fit, a CPA for tax, and an attorney for structuring, documents, and tax protection. Without that help, an investor may miss the one-way limitation, REIT diligence, conversion tax, protection terms, or fit.

### Can I undo a 721 exchange if I make a mistake?

Generally not tax-free. Exiting REIT ownership requires unit conversion, which recognizes deferred gain, and reinvesting the after-tax proceeds. Prevention through upfront diligence is therefore more useful than relying on a later reversal.

### How can I avoid overlooking the conversion tax?

Start with the fact that conversion triggers deferred gain, including the four-layer tax and recapture. Model it with the CPA, spread conversions over years where appropriate, convert in lower-income years when appropriate, and hold unneeded units toward a potential step-up.

### What happens if I transition into a weak REIT?

Returns and unit value can suffer. Because the 721 commitment is largely irreversible and the holder is passive, recourse is limited. That is why REIT diligence before contribution is essential; select a strong, well-managed REIT that fits the objective.

### Is rushing a 721 exchange a mistake?

Yes. Rushing can cause every other error: misunderstanding the one-way commitment, incomplete REIT diligence, missed conversion tax, neglected tax protection, and ignored fit. Unlike a 1031, a standalone 721 has no statutory deadlines, so take the time for unhurried diligence.

### How do I avoid these mistakes?

Be certain about the one-way choice, evaluate the REIT, plan conversion tax, negotiate appropriate tax protection, assess fit honestly, and use a qualified advisor, CPA, and attorney. Careful, professionally guided diligence is the practical safeguard.

### Is choosing a non-traded REIT without understanding it a mistake?

It can be. A non-traded REIT can have program-based limited liquidity, valuation uncertainty, and often higher fees; these features materially affect the outcome. FINRA cautions investors to review non-traded REITs carefully. Understand liquidity limitations, valuation, and fees rather than assuming it behaves like a traded REIT.

### Can poor timing of the conversion be a mistake?

Yes. Converting a large amount in a high-income year, or converting all at once instead of gradually, can increase tax unnecessarily. Coordinate timing and amount with the CPA, including potential lower-income years, to manage bracket and other tax exposure.

### Is not having an exit or liquidity plan a mistake?

It can be. Before entering, decide whether the plan is to hold for income and a potential step-up, convert gradually for liquidity, or use a combination. A long-term units plan, developed with the advisor and CPA, helps manage both the holding and its tax.

### Is overlooking fees a mistake in a 721 exchange?

Yes. REIT management fees, expenses, and transaction costs reduce net returns, and structures vary by REIT. Non-traded REITs often have higher fees. Ask about and understand the fee structure as part of REIT diligence so net-return expectations are realistic.
