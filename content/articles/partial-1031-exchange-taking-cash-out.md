# Partial 1031 Exchanges: Taking Some Cash Out

I have been thinking about how often the word “exchange” makes a transaction sound all-or-nothing. It is not. A property owner can defer much of the gain in a 1031 exchange and deliberately take out some cash. The portion retained is taxable; the balance can remain deferred.

First-order thinking says cash is cash. Second-order thinking asks what cash was taken, what debt was replaced, how much gain is actually recognized, whether depreciation recapture is involved, and whether the tax cost is worth the use of the money. A partial exchange can be a practical tool. An accidental one is a surprise tax bill.

This article is educational information, not tax, legal, accounting, or investment advice. A CPA and qualified intermediary should review actual facts before an exchange is underway.

## What is a partial exchange?

A partial [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) is an exchange in which the investor does not reinvest all sale proceeds or does not replace all debt in the replacement property. The amount reinvested can remain tax-deferred. The cash retained, or the debt shortfall, is recognized and taxed. In effect, it is a deferred exchange for part of the transaction and a taxable sale for the remainder.

The mechanics still use a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html), replacement-property identification, and the usual exchange deadlines. The difference is that not every dollar is directed into the replacement. Cash or other non-like-kind value received is [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), taxable up to the amount of realized gain.

The flexibility is real. An investor may need funds for a non-real-estate purpose, a large expense, debt reduction, diversification, or simply to take some chips off the table. The trade-off is also real: the boot is taxed. Planning makes the trade-off intentional.

## How cash-out creates taxable boot

Cash boot arises when proceeds come to the investor instead of the replacement property. If an owner directs **$100,000** of sale proceeds to themselves and reinvests the rest, that **$100,000** is recognized gain and taxable, assuming realized gain is at least that amount. The reinvested portion remains deferred.

Cash boot can be chosen or accidental. It can arise because proceeds inadvertently reach the exchanger, because the replacement is bought at lower value, or because not all equity is reinvested. For a full deferral, all equity must be reinvested in [equal-or-greater-value property](zero-boot-exchanges-structuring-full-tax-deferral.html). Any equity not reinvested becomes cash boot.

Tax character matters. Recognized gain can include recaptured depreciation, which may be taxed at less favorable rates than long-term capital gain. The same dollar of boot can produce different tax depending on the property’s depreciation history. That is why a CPA should model the actual cash-out rather than apply a casual rule of thumb.

Cash kept, value not replaced, and debt not matched are all boot, taxable up to gain. The partial exchange simply makes the choice explicit.

## How reducing debt creates boot too

Cash is not the only source of boot. If debt on the relinquished property is paid off at sale, that debt relief is treated as if the investor received cash. To avoid mortgage boot, the investor generally needs to [replace the debt](1031-exchange-debt-replacement-mortgage-boot.html) with at least as much new debt or add equivalent cash out of pocket. A lower replacement loan creates a taxable shortfall.

For example, imagine **$400,000** of debt is paid off at sale and the replacement property has only a **$250,000** loan. The **$150,000** difference is mortgage boot unless the investor adds **$150,000** of cash to close the gap.

To fully defer, an investor must reinvest all equity and replace all debt, or substitute cash for debt. A partial exchange can intentionally use cash boot, mortgage boot through deleveraging, or both. The two forms combine to set total taxable boot. This is why the capital structure deserves the same attention as the check received at closing.

## When a partial exchange makes sense

A partial exchange can make sense when an investor wants some cash while still deferring the bulk of gain. That may mean funding a particular need, covering a major expense, paying down other debt, reducing real-estate exposure, or taking chips off the table. It is an alternative to choosing between no-cash full deferral and a fully taxable sale.

It can also support rebalancing. An investor overweight real estate may retain some proceeds for other assets while keeping much of the exchange deferred. An investor wanting less leverage may accept mortgage boot in return for lower replacement debt.

The decision is a comparison between the tax cost and the value of the cash. Boot is taxable and may contain depreciation recapture — for real property, unrecaptured §1250 gain, taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% rather than at ordinary rates. If the cash serves a genuine need better than the alternatives, accepting the tax can be sensible. If it does not, full deferral may preserve more wealth. A CPA can quantify the result before the investor decides.

## Calculating your deferred vs. taxed portion

The central formula is straightforward: recognized, taxable gain is the lesser of total boot or total realized gain. The rest of the gain is deferred. If total gain is **$400,000** and total boot is **$100,000**, the result is **$100,000** recognized and **$300,000** deferred. Boot cannot cause more gain to be recognized than actually exists.

The calculation still needs care. Work with a CPA to track realized gain—sale price less adjusted basis and selling costs—cash boot, and mortgage boot: debt paid off less replacement debt, net of additional cash. The recognized amount may include [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), and the replacement property’s basis changes because the exchange is partial. That later basis affects future depreciation.

The point of modeling is control. An investor can set a target for cash received, tax cost, or percentage deferred before closing, rather than discover it after the fact. A well-planned partial exchange has a known deferred-versus-taxed split.

## Key takeaways

- A partial exchange can defer most gain while retaining cash that is taxable boot.
- Boot comes from cash kept, replacement value not reinvested, and debt not replaced.
- Recognized gain is the lesser of total boot or total gain; it may include higher-rate depreciation recapture.
- Model the split with a CPA before closing so the cash and tax amounts are intentional.

## Planning a partial exchange

Start with the reason for taking cash and a target amount. Then model the tax on that amount with a CPA, including the interaction between cash boot, mortgage boot, and recapture. With the cash target and tax cost known, the investor can decide whether the partial exchange is worthwhile and structure it precisely.

The normal exchange deadlines still apply. The qualified intermediary must be engaged before closing. Replacement property must be identified within **45** days and acquired within **180** days. The intended boot should be directed to the investor while the rest of the proceeds flows to the replacement in a clear, planned structure.

Intentionality is the difference. An investor who sets a cash amount, models tax, and coordinates the structure with the qualified intermediary and CPA receives a controlled outcome. An investor who takes proceeds, buys down in value, or reduces debt without understanding the consequences can create unwanted boot.

## A worked partial-exchange example

Suppose a rental property has a net sale price of **$700,000**, a **$300,000** mortgage paid off at closing, and an adjusted basis of **$250,000**. The realized gain is **$450,000**. The owner wants **$80,000** in cash and plans to exchange the rest. The **$80,000** is cash boot, and the **$300,000** debt relief must be addressed on the replacement side.

If the owner reinvests the remaining **$620,000** of equity in replacement property worth at least **$620,000** and replaces the full **$300,000** of debt, there is no mortgage boot. Total boot is the **$80,000** cash amount, so **$80,000** is taxed and **$370,000** is deferred. Some of the **$80,000** may be depreciation recapture at higher rates; the CPA should quantify the actual mix.

Now assume the owner replaces only **$200,000** of the **$300,000** debt. The **$100,000** shortfall is mortgage boot. Added to **$80,000** of cash boot, total boot is **$180,000**. The investor recognizes **$180,000**, still below the **$450,000** realized gain, and defers **$270,000**. These figures are illustrative. The lesson is that cash and debt boot combine, so changing either changes the taxable split.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a partial 1031 exchange?

It is an exchange in which most proceeds are reinvested tax-deferred but some cash is kept, or some debt is not replaced. The retained portion is boot, taxable up to gain. The transaction is partly deferred and partly a taxable sale.

### Can I take cash out of a 1031 exchange?

Yes. That is a partial exchange. Cash retained is taxable boot, up to realized gain, while the reinvested portion stays deferred. A 1031 is not all-or-nothing, but the cash-out should be planned so the tax is the result intended.

### What is boot?

Boot is cash or other non-like-kind value received in the exchange. It includes cash kept, replacement value not reinvested, and debt not replaced. It is taxable up to the amount of gain.

### How does reducing my debt create boot?

If sale debt is paid off and the replacement carries less debt, the shortfall is mortgage boot. Debt relief is treated like cash received unless it is offset with added cash. To fully defer, replace all debt or substitute cash for it.

### How much of my gain is taxed in a partial exchange?

Recognized gain is the lesser of total boot and total realized gain. For **$100,000** of boot against **$400,000** of gain, **$100,000** is recognized and **$300,000** is deferred. Boot cannot recognize more than the actual gain, and some recognized amount may be higher-rate recapture.

### When does a partial exchange make sense?

It can fit a specific cash need, a decision to reduce real-estate exposure or leverage, or a desire to take some chips off the table while deferring the rest. It permits an amount of cash between a no-cash full exchange and a fully taxable sale.

### Is the tax on boot at the capital-gains rate?

Not always. Recognized boot can include recaptured depreciation. For real property that is unrecaptured §1250 gain, taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% — higher than the long-term capital-gain rate, but not ordinary income. A CPA should model the property’s history and the actual boot to determine the tax result.

### How do I calculate my deferred vs. taxed portions?

Track realized gain, cash boot, and mortgage boot with a CPA. Realized gain is sale price less adjusted basis and selling costs; mortgage boot is debt paid off less debt replaced, net of cash added. Recognized gain is the lesser of total boot or total gain, and the replacement basis is adjusted for the partial exchange.

### Can I accidentally trigger a partial exchange?

Yes. Proceeds reaching the investor, a cheaper replacement, or unplanned debt reduction can create boot. To fully defer, reinvest all equity into equal-or-greater value and replace all debt. Planning prevents a surprise tax.

### How do I plan a partial exchange?

Decide the cash need and target amount, model its tax cost with a CPA including cash-and-debt boot and recapture, then structure the exchange so the intended boot goes to the investor and the balance goes to the replacement. The qualified intermediary and CPA should coordinate the details.

### Is keeping cash through a partial exchange worth the tax?

It depends on the purpose of the cash and alternatives. Boot has a real tax cost, sometimes at higher recapture rates. A genuine need may justify it; if the cash is not needed, full deferral can preserve more wealth. A CPA can put the cost beside the benefit.

### Does a partial exchange affect my replacement property’s basis?

Yes. The replacement basis is adjusted for the partial nature of the exchange, generally through carryover basis plus any additional investment and the boot taken. That affects future depreciation, and the CPA calculates it for Form **8824**.

### Can I take cash and also keep debt low in the same exchange?

Yes. Cash boot and mortgage boot add together to determine recognized gain, up to total gain. With a CPA, an investor can set both the cash and debt choices to reach the intended deferred-versus-taxed split.

### Is there a limit on how much boot I can take?

There is no statutory limit. An investor can take any amount of boot, including all proceeds in a fully taxable sale. Recognized gain is capped at actual realized gain, but the practical limit is the cash needed because each dollar of boot carries tax.

### Will I still owe tax if my boot is less than my selling costs?

Selling costs reduce realized gain and can offset some boot, but the result is fact-specific. Recognized gain is generally the lesser of net boot or realized gain; do not assume selling costs eliminate boot tax. Model the exact numbers.

### Should I take cash now or do a full exchange and refinance later?

Compare the alternatives. Cash taken in a partial exchange is taxable boot now. A full exchange followed later by refinancing the replacement can create tax-free loan proceeds because a loan is not taxable, preserving the full deferral. Refinancing has costs and risks, so discuss both paths with a CPA and advisor.

## Glossary

- **Partial Exchange:** A 1031 where some gain is deferred and some recognized because proceeds or debt are not fully reinvested or replaced.
- **Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received; taxable up to gain.
- **Cash Boot:** Sale proceeds the exchanger keeps rather than reinvesting.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Taxable result of replacing less debt than was paid off, unless cash offsets it.
- **Realized Gain:** Total sale gain: proceeds less adjusted basis and selling costs.
- **Recognized Gain:** The taxed portion, equal to the lesser of total boot or realized gain.
- **Deferred Gain:** Gain not recognized and carried forward to replacement property.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation that can be included in recognized boot. For real property it is unrecaptured §1250 gain, capped at 25% federal.
- **Equal-or-Greater-Value Rule:** The requirement to acquire replacement value at least equal to net sale price for full deferral.
- **Debt Replacement:** New debt or added cash at least equal to debt paid off, to avoid mortgage boot.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Original cost reduced by depreciation, used in realized-gain calculation.
- **Carryover Basis:** Relinquished property basis transferred to the replacement and adjusted in a partial exchange.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** Independent party that holds proceeds and coordinates the intended cash-out.
- **Form 8824:** IRS form reporting the exchange, boot, and basis calculation.
- **Deleveraging:** Reducing replacement debt, which creates mortgage boot unless cash offsets it.
- **Net Sale Price:** Gross sale price less selling costs; the equal-or-greater-value baseline.
