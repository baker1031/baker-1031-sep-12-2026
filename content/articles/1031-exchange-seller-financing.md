# 1031 Exchange and Seller Financing

I keep seeing seller financing viewed as a simple way to get a deal over the line. In a 1031 exchange, the note can change the tax result just as much as the sale price does.

A seller-carried note is not like-kind real property. It is a debt instrument. If the seller receives and keeps it outside the exchange, its value can be boot, taxable up to gain. The good news is that deliberate pre-closing structure can keep the note’s value inside the exchange rather than letting it become unintended boot.

## When sellers carry a note

Seller financing means the seller accepts payment over time instead of all cash at closing. The seller effectively lends the buyer a portion of the price and carries back a promissory note for the deferred amount, often secured by a mortgage or deed of trust. The buyer pays principal and interest over the term. It is also called a seller carryback or an [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html).

It can expand the buyer pool, including buyers without conventional financing, support a higher price or interest rate, and spread gain recognition as payments are received. In a soft credit market or with an unusual property, it can be the difference between selling and not selling. Those are real commercial benefits, but a seller-financed [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) needs special care.

Full deferral generally means reinvesting proceeds in like-kind replacement property. A seller-carried note is a financial instrument, not real estate. If the seller retains it, it is non-like-kind value—[boot](insights.html)—and is taxable up to the seller’s gain. The sale tool can therefore reduce deferral unless the note is handled correctly.

## How the note can create boot

Boot is non-like-kind value received in an exchange: cash or other property that is not like-kind real estate. A promissory note is a financial instrument. If a seller takes a $200,000 note as part of the consideration for relinquished property, that $200,000 of note value is potentially boot, taxable up to total gain.

The reason is mechanical. In a normal exchange, cash proceeds flow through the qualified intermediary and into the replacement. A note retained by the seller represents proceeds that did not enter that like-kind reinvestment. The larger the carried note instead of reinvested cash, the larger the potential boot.

The note’s value is recognized gain up to total gain, though installment-sale rules may spread the timing of recognition over payments rather than require it all at once. That interaction is technical. The note can be boot under 1031 analysis even when the installment method affects the timing of its tax.

> A promissory note is a financial instrument, not real property. If received in the exchange, its value is non-like-kind boot, taxable up to gain.

## Including the note in the exchange

The cleanest path is to bring the note into the exchange rather than leave it with the seller. The note can be made payable to the qualified intermediary, which holds it as part of exchange funds. The seller has not received it as separate boot, and its value can be applied to the replacement.

The QI may sell the note to a third-party note buyer for cash, with that cash flowing into the replacement. The seller may buy the note from the QI using outside funds, placing cash into the exchange. Or the note itself may be used as replacement consideration if the replacement seller will accept it. Each approach keeps the note’s value in like-kind reinvestment.

Each comes with a trade-off. A note buyer may pay less than face value, a discount that reduces proceeds. A buyout requires outside cash. Replacement consideration requires a willing seller. Coordinate the answer with the QI so the note is properly held and resolved within the exchange.

## Structuring around the problem

One option is to accept the note as partial boot. The seller pays tax on the note, perhaps with timing governed by the installment method, while fully deferring the reinvested cash portion. This can be sensible for a relatively small note when simplicity is worth some tax.

Another option is to make the relinquished-property sale all cash. The buyer can obtain financing from a lender rather than from the seller. All proceeds can then flow into the replacement, avoiding the note-boot issue completely. That gives up the potential sale, pricing, or interest benefits of seller financing.

The crucial decision is made before the sale: exchange the note, accept partial boot, or avoid the carryback. First-order thinking says a note simply makes the buyer’s financing work. Second-order thinking asks where its value resides for exchange purposes and whether that value will be reinvested. The QI and CPA should help balance the sale benefit, full-deferral objective, and structure cost before closing.

## Working with your QI

The QI needs to be involved before the sale closes. If the note will stay in the exchange, it should be payable to the QI, held as exchange funds, and resolved through a sale, buyout, or replacement consideration. A QI experienced in seller-financed exchanges handles those mechanics.

The documents must place the note in the exchange from the outset. Receiving it personally and trying to contribute it later can be problematic. Its sale, buyout, or use must also fit the exchange timeline. That requires planning among the seller, buyer, QI, and any note buyer before closing.

Seller financing and a 1031 can coexist, but not through after-the-fact improvisation. Raise the issue with the QI and CPA in advance, choose the treatment, and document it. Done carefully, financing can help the sale without creating unintended boot. Done carelessly or too late, the note undercuts the deferral.

### Key Takeaways

- A seller-carried note is non-like-kind property and may be taxable boot.
- Avoid boot by making the note payable to the QI and then selling it, buying it out, or using it toward the replacement.
- Alternatives are accepting partial boot or avoiding seller financing on the relinquished property.
- Engage an experienced QI and CPA before the sale; post-closing improvisation rarely works.

## Seller financing on the replacement (buyer side)

Seller financing has a different effect when the exchanger is buying the replacement. In that case, the exchanger is taking on debt to acquire property, not receiving a note as sale proceeds. Replacement-side seller financing can help replace debt paid off on the relinquished property and support debt matching.

It can be useful when conventional financing is difficult to arrange inside the 180-day exchange period. The replacement seller carries a note, exchange proceeds cover the rest of the purchase, and the seller-carried debt can help meet the debt-replacement target more quickly than a conventional loan.

The acquisition still needs correct QI and CPA coordination, proper exchange proceeds, valid debt treatment, and normal replacement-property diligence. The distinction is the important point: seller financing on the sell side creates a boot issue; seller financing on the buy side can be a useful debt-matching tool.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does seller financing create boot in a 1031 exchange?

It can. A note on relinquished property is non-like-kind financial property. Unless it is brought into the exchange and used toward the replacement, it is boot taxable up to gain.

### Why is a seller-carried note boot?

It is a financial instrument rather than real estate, and it represents proceeds that do not flow into replacement property. More note and less reinvested cash means more potential boot.

### How can I avoid the note creating boot?

Make it payable to the QI so it remains in the exchange. The QI can sell it for cash, the seller can buy it out with outside funds, or it can be used as replacement consideration.

### Can the qualified intermediary hold the note?

Yes. QI ownership from the outset is the key structural step. It permits sale, buyout, or replacement use without the seller first receiving boot. The QI must be involved before closing.

### What if I sell the note to a third party?

The QI can sell it to a note buyer and send the cash to the replacement. Note buyers commonly pay a discount below face value, reducing proceeds, but the sale can avoid boot.

### Can I just accept the boot on the note?

Yes. Treat it as partial boot, pay tax on it—possibly spread under the installment method—and defer the reinvested cash. This trades tax for a simpler structure.

### Can I avoid the problem by not carrying a note?

Yes. An all-cash sale, with the buyer using a lender, avoids note boot and supports a clean fully deferred exchange, although it gives up seller-financing benefits.

### How do installment-sale rules interact with this?

A seller note is an installment sale and can spread gain recognition over payments. That timing rule operates alongside the 1031 analysis that treats the note as potential boot. The CPA models the interaction.

### Is seller financing on the replacement property a problem?

Generally no. This is buy-side financing, a debt the exchanger takes on. It can help replace relinquished debt, support debt matching, and close more quickly than conventional finance.

### When should I plan for seller financing in an exchange?

Before closing. Decide with the QI and CPA whether to exchange the note, accept partial boot, or avoid the carryback. The note should be QI-payable from the outset, not received and later contributed.

### Can seller financing and full deferral coexist?

Yes, with deliberate structure: note payable to the QI, then sale or buyout so its value funds the replacement. It requires early QI and CPA planning.

### Who do I need to structure a seller-financed exchange?

An experienced QI for the note mechanics and a CPA for boot and installment-sale treatment. Both need to be involved before closing.

### Does the note have to be payable to the QI from the start?

For the cleanest treatment, yes. Having the seller receive it and later try to add it is problematic. Making it exchange property from the beginning protects against boot.

### What if the buyer only needs short-term financing?

A short seller note can be paid off quickly with payoff cash entering the exchange, or the buyer can use bridge financing so the seller receives an effective all-cash sale. Any note still needs QI and CPA planning.

## Glossary

- **Seller Financing:** A seller accepting payment over time and carrying a note for the deferred amount.
- **Seller Carryback:** Another name for seller financing, in which a note is carried back from the buyer.
- **Promissory Note:** A written promise to pay over time; a financial instrument, not real property.
- **Installment Sale:** A sale with payments over time that spreads gain recognition and commonly takes the form of a seller note.
- **Boot:** Non-like-kind value received in an exchange; a seller note can be boot.
- **Non-Like-Kind Property:** Property other than like-kind real estate, including a note received as boot.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that can hold a note inside the exchange to keep its value from becoming boot.
- **Note Buyer:** A third party buying a note for cash, often at a discount.
- **Note Buyout:** The seller’s purchase of the note from the QI with outside funds, putting cash into the exchange.
- **Partial Exchange:** Accepting the note as taxable boot while deferring the rest of the gain.
- **Debt Matching:** Replacing relinquished debt; buy-side seller financing can help satisfy it.
- **Deed of Trust / Mortgage:** The security instrument securing a seller-carried note.
- **Installment Method:** The method spreading gain recognition over payments, affecting note-tax timing.
- **Replacement Consideration:** Using a note as payment for replacement property, keeping value in the exchange.
- **All-Cash Sale:** A sale without seller financing, avoiding the note-boot complication.
- **Relinquished Property:** The sold property; carrying a note on it creates the boot issue.
