# From DST to REIT: How a 721 Roll-Up Actually Works

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

UPREIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 7 min read

I have been thinking about how a DST’s exit changes the underwriting an investor ought to do at entry. Some DSTs are not designed to be held forever. They are structured to end inside a REIT.

The sponsor packages property in a Delaware Statutory Trust, sells beneficial interests to 1031 investors, holds the property for a period, then has its affiliated REIT acquire it at full cycle. Rather than receiving cash and triggering tax, DST investors can contribute their interests to the REIT operating partnership in exchange for operating-partnership units. That is a 721 roll-up, or UPREIT transaction.

The mechanics preserve deferral, but they do not make the destination irrelevant. The asset an investor begins with is the DST property. The asset they are ultimately underwriting is the REIT: its portfolio, leverage, distributions, redemption terms, and management. This is the practical explanation of how the roll-up works and why it changes DST selection.

## What a 721 roll-up is

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) holds one property or a small group of properties and sells fractional beneficial interests to investors completing 1031 exchanges. The DST offers passive ownership: the sponsor manages the real estate, and the interest is treated as like-kind real estate for 1031 purposes.

The 721 roll-up is the DST’s possible back end. In a structure designed for this exit, the sponsor also manages a REIT, commonly a non-traded REIT, which sits above a series of DST investments. When the DST reaches full cycle, the sponsor’s REIT acquires the DST property. Instead of buying it for cash and ending the DST investors’ deferral, the REIT lets them contribute their DST interests to its operating partnership for OP units.

Section 721 generally permits a taxpayer to contribute property to a partnership for a partnership interest without recognizing gain. The investor contributes the DST interest and receives the partnership interest—OP units. The deferred 1031 gain carries forward rather than becoming current tax at the roll-up.

The larger structure is an UPREIT, short for umbrella partnership REIT. The REIT generally owns units in the operating partnership, and that partnership directly owns the real estate. The 721 contribution works because the investor contributes into the same partnership below the REIT. For the full entry-to-units sequence, see [the 1031-to-721 exchange overview](1031-to-721-exchange.html).

## The sequence from exchange to units

The roll-up is usually the final move in a process that begins years earlier.

| Stage | What happens | Tax effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1031 into the DST | The investor exchanges relinquished property for DST beneficial interests through a qualified intermediary. | Gain is deferred under Section 1031. |
| Hold to full cycle | The DST holds the property, typically five to ten years, and distributes income. | Distributions are taxed as earned; deferred gain remains deferred. |
| 721 contribution | The sponsor’s REIT acquires the property, and the investor contributes the DST interest to the operating partnership for OP units. | No gain is generally recognized under Section 721; deferral continues. |
| Hold OP units | The investor owns a partnership interest and receives distributions reported on a K-1. | Distributions are taxed; deferred gain remains inside the units. |
| Convert to REIT shares | The investor elects to exchange OP units for REIT common shares, generally one-for-one or at a stated ratio. | Conversion is generally taxable; deferred gain is recognized on the converted units. |
| Sell shares | The investor sells REIT shares, in part or in full, for cash. | Capital gain or loss is measured from basis in the shares. |

This sequence is illustrative only. Timing, conversion ratios, and tax treatment depend on the offering documents and the investor’s own facts.

Two points are easy to miss. A roll-up is not instant liquidity. It moves the investor from one private, illiquid investment to another, although the REIT has a broader pool of properties underneath it. And the sponsor determines when the DST reaches full cycle and presents the 721 opportunity. The investor’s later decision—when to convert units and sell shares—is where the investor gains more control over timing.

## Continued deferral, and why the roll-up is not a sale

Section 721 is the reason the transaction exists. When property is contributed to a partnership for an interest in that partnership, gain or loss is generally not recognized. In this context, the DST interest is the contributed property and the OP units are the partnership interest received.

First-order thinking sees the REIT acquire the building and assumes the investor was cashed out. Second-order thinking looks at what the investor received. In a properly structured roll-up, the investor did not receive cash. They contributed the DST interest and took units. The prior gain does not disappear, but it does not become taxable at this point either; it carries forward embedded in the OP units.

Deferral lasts while the units are held. When the investor converts OP units into REIT shares, conversion is generally taxable and the deferred gain is realized on the converted units. The roll-up buys more deferral time, not an automatic permanent exemption. A possible intervening event is death: a step-up in basis can erase the deferred gain for heirs under current rules.

## OP units in plain terms

An OP unit is ownership in the REIT’s operating partnership. It is not a REIT share, and the difference matters for tax reporting and liquidity.

OP units are partnership interests and generally generate a Schedule K-1. REIT shares are securities; they generally generate a 1099 and, in a non-traded REIT, may be eligible for the sponsor’s redemption program. [OP units explained](op-units-explained.html) covers the reporting distinction in more detail.

Economically, units usually track REIT shares closely. They often receive the same distribution rate per unit that shares receive per share and are generally convertible to REIT shares at a stated ratio, frequently one-for-one. That potential conversion is the path to liquidity and the tax line: holding units continues deferral; converting them generally brings deferred gain into taxable income.

The investor controls the timing of conversion. Units can be converted and shares sold in slices over years, which can allow gains to be managed across tax years and brackets rather than recognized all at once. That staged option is different from a cash sale of the DST property at full cycle. It does not remove the tax; it can give the investor a dial for its timing.

## Why the roll-up should affect DST selection

If a DST is designed to end in a 721 roll-up, the original building is not the final investment. It is a way station. The destination REIT is where the investor’s income, downside, and liquidity will ultimately reside.

That changes the order of diligence. The DST property still matters, but so does the REIT’s portfolio mix, property-level and entity-level leverage, distribution history, distribution coverage by cash flow, possible return-of-capital funding, and redemption-program terms. Review the [DST sponsor diligence checklist](dst-sponsor-due-diligence.html) and the [REIT guide](reits-guide.html) with the eventual exit in mind.

The private placement memorandum should be read for the exit as well as the entry. A strong in-place tenant at the DST matters less if the asset eventually goes into a REIT with heavy leverage and a backed-up redemption queue. Conversely, a merely adequate DST property that goes into a large, well-capitalized REIT with a deep portfolio and functioning redemption program may offer a better long-term position. [Review the PPM](how-to-review-a-ppm.html) for this destination analysis.

## The path to liquidity

A 721 roll-up does not turn a private position into cash. The investor holds OP units, may later convert them to REIT shares, and can then sell shares. In a non-traded REIT, selling generally occurs through the sponsor’s redemption program, where the REIT buys back shares at an announced price on a periodic schedule.

Redemption terms are both the liquidity feature and the liquidity constraint. Programs commonly cap redemptions per quarter and per year, often by a percentage of total shares or net asset value. They may impose a holding period before redemption, charge an early-redemption discount, and allow the board to limit or suspend redemptions. In stressed markets, a surge of redemption requests can hit program gates or cause suspensions.

Liquidity is therefore real but conditional. An investor may sell gradually, but should not assume all shares can be sold on any particular day or at full value. The offset is timing control: the investor can convert and redeem in pieces as cash is needed and spread taxable gain over multiple years.

## The one-way door

The key trade-off comes after the 721 contribution. OP units and REIT shares are not like-kind real property, so they cannot be used in another 1031 exchange. The investor has lost the ability to exchange out of the position into new direct real estate.

Inside a DST at full cycle, the investor may generally have the option to complete another 1031 exchange into another DST or like-kind property, continuing deferral within real estate. Accepting the 721 contribution gives that flexibility up. The investor’s future choices generally become holding units, converting and selling in taxable transactions, or holding until death for a possible step-up in basis.

This is a trade-off rather than a free upgrade. Diversification, scale, staged liquidity, and estate planning may justify it for the appropriate investor. For an investor who wants continued 1031 optionality, the one-way aspect can be too costly. See the full discussion of [721 exchange downsides](721-exchange-downsides.html).

## Estate planning and step-up in basis

At death, heirs generally receive a stepped-up basis equal to the fair market value of the units or shares. Under current rules, the original gain deferred from the first 1031, through the DST, and through the roll-up can be erased for income-tax purposes. Heirs may then sell at the stepped-up basis with little or no capital-gains tax on the prior appreciation.

That is why the one-way nature may look different to an investor who expects to hold for life. The investor receives income and deferral during life, and heirs may receive a clean basis. OP units also divide more readily among heirs than a single building: multiple heirs can each inherit portions of a unit position without selling, partitioning, or disputing ownership of one property. See [721 exchange estate planning](721-exchange-estate-planning.html). Step-up rules can change, so the investor needs current advice.

## Is the roll-up mandatory or optional?

The answer depends on the offering and is usually in the DST private placement memorandum. Many 721-structured DSTs give investors a choice at full cycle: accept cash, recognize the deferred gain, and pay tax, or accept the 721 contribution into the REIT and continue deferral. Some offerings are built around the roll-up as the expected primary exit.

An investor should not learn after investing whether a contribution is effectively forced or whether a cash alternative exists. The PPM should be read for whether the 721 is optional or mandatory, the cash alternative and its terms, sponsor control over timing, the conversion ratio from OP units to REIT shares, and inherited redemption terms. An unclear exit description is itself a diligence concern.

## The risks inherited in the REIT

A roll-up diversifies one property into a REIT portfolio, but it also inherits the REIT’s risks.

- **Entity-level leverage.** Destination-REIT debt across the portfolio can affect the investor even though the investor never signed for it. High leverage or maturities in a difficult financing market can pressure the position.
- **Distribution sustainability.** A headline distribution rate needs to be measured against operating cash flow. Distributions supported partly by return of capital or new investor money may not be durable.
- **Redemption-queue risk.** Liquidity depends on a funded and open redemption program. If redemption requests are heavy, gates can be hit or the program can be suspended.

These are not automatic reasons to reject a 721-structured DST. They are reasons to underwrite the destination REIT before the investor accepts the roll-up. Its balance sheet, distribution discipline, and redemption capacity determine how the position behaves through the cycle. [DST full cycle](dst-full-cycle.html) explains where the roll-up occurs in a trust’s life.

## Who a 721 roll-up can fit

The structure may fit an investor who wants continued deferral, values portfolio diversification over a single building, accepts the loss of future 1031 optionality, and either plans to hold units for life or wants staged liquidity they can manage across tax years. It can be particularly relevant to an older investor who sees potential step-up in basis as part of the estate plan.

It may fit poorly for an investor who requires predictable near-term liquidity, wants to maintain fully 1031-eligible real estate, or is unwilling to underwrite the destination REIT. A conventional DST at full cycle may allow another 1031, and direct real estate can preserve options the roll-up closes.

The appropriate approach is to match the structure to the investor’s timeline and estate plan, study the REIT where the assets will land, and read the PPM for the exit terms. The broader [721 exchange guide](721-exchange-guide.html) provides the underlying rules.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 721 roll-up?

It is a DST exit in which a sponsor’s affiliated REIT acquires the DST property and investors contribute their DST interests to the REIT operating partnership in exchange for OP units. It is named for Section 721, which generally allows the contribution without recognizing gain.

### Do I pay tax when a DST rolls up into a REIT?

Generally not at the roll-up. Contributing a DST interest for OP units under Section 721 generally continues the deferral, so the prior 1031 gain remains embedded in the units. Tax generally comes later if the investor converts OP units to REIT shares or otherwise makes a taxable disposition.

### What is the difference between an OP unit and a REIT share?

An OP unit is a partnership interest in the operating partnership and is reported on a K-1. A REIT share is a security in the REIT and is reported on a 1099. OP units are generally convertible to shares at a stated ratio, and conversion is generally taxable.

### Can I 1031 exchange out after a 721 roll-up?

No. OP units and REIT shares are not like-kind real property, so they cannot be used in a 1031 exchange. The roll-up is generally a one-way door away from exchange optionality.

### How do I eventually get liquidity?

The investor converts OP units to REIT shares and sells the shares. In a non-traded REIT, selling usually relies on a redemption program. Programs may have quarterly and annual caps, holding periods, early-redemption discounts, and suspension rights, so liquidity is conditional rather than equivalent to a public-stock sale.

### Is a 721 roll-up mandatory?

It depends on the offering. Many structures give the investor a choice between cash, which generally triggers tax, and a 721 contribution that continues deferral. Some are designed around a roll-up as the primary exit. Read the PPM for the choice, cash terms, timing control, and conversion and redemption terms.

### What happens to deferred gain at death?

Under current rules, heirs generally receive a stepped-up basis to fair market value at death, which can erase prior deferred gain for income-tax purposes. This is why the one-way feature may fit an investor who plans to hold for life. Step-up rules can change, so advisers should confirm the current law and facts.

### Why should the roll-up affect which DST I buy?

Because the investor’s long-term outcome is determined by the destination REIT, not merely the original DST building. The investor should review the REIT’s portfolio, leverage, distribution coverage and history, and redemption program before purchasing a DST designed to roll up.

## Glossary

**721 Roll-Up:** A DST exit where a REIT acquires the property and investors contribute DST interests for OP units under Section 721.

**Operating Partnership (OP):** The partnership beneath an UPREIT that owns the real estate and receives 721 contributions.

**OP Units:** Operating-partnership ownership units, reported on a K-1 and generally convertible to REIT shares.

**Full-Cycle Sale:** The end of a DST’s life, typically five to ten years after formation, when the property is sold or contributed.

**Destination REIT:** The sponsor’s affiliated REIT that receives the DST property and determines the investor’s long-term income, risk, and liquidity.

**Continued Deferral:** Carrying prior deferred gain through a 721 contribution while OP units are held.

**Conversion:** A generally taxable exchange of OP units for REIT shares that realizes deferred gain on converted units.

**Step-Up in Basis:** A potential reset of basis to fair market value at death that can erase deferred gain for heirs under current rules.

**Redemption Program:** A non-traded REIT’s periodic share-repurchase program, typically subject to limits and suspension.
