# 721 Exchange for Retiring Real Estate Investors


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep hearing the same retirement wish from property owners: “I want the income, but I do not want another call about a roof, a tenant, or a turnover.” It is a reasonable goal. It also involves more than replacing one stream of rent with another.

A 721 exchange can help a retiring investor move active real estate into passive, diversified REIT ownership. OP-unit distributions can support retirement income, while the exchange can defer gain, reduce concentration, provide a liquidity path, and support estate planning. Many retiring owners find that combination worth examining.

The first-order view is passivity and distributions. The second-order view is whether the investor can afford the one-way shift from direct property, how unit liquidity and conversion tax fit actual spending needs, whether the REIT is traded or non-traded, and how remaining units fit heirs and other retirement assets. This is educational information, not tax, legal, or investment advice.

## Why Retiring Investors Use 721 Exchanges

The strategy can align with several retirement goals. First, the owner can stop active property management. Second, [OP units](op-units-explained.html) can provide distributions from a diversified REIT portfolio. Third, diversification can reduce the risk of having most wealth in one property or market at a time when a major loss may be harder to recover from.

The other two goals are deferral and estate planning. Retiring owners often have low-basis property with substantial embedded gain; a qualifying 721 can defer that gain. A step-up in basis at death and units divisible among beneficiaries can make the estate question easier. The benefits have to be evaluated against liquidity, risk, suitability, and actual retirement needs. See the [721 exchange guide](721-exchange-guide.html) for general mechanics.

## Converting Real Estate to Retirement Income

Direct rental income can require management. A 721 exchange changes property ownership into OP units that can pay distributions from the REIT portfolio. That can replace active rental income with a passive real-estate income stream.

Because a REIT portfolio can hold many properties, markets, and tenants, distributions may be more diversified than the income from one property. They are not guaranteed. They depend on the REIT's portfolio income and distribution policy and can be reduced. Retirement planning should use a margin of safety and other income sources rather than treating distributions as a promised paycheck.

## Passivity in Retirement

Managing property involves tenants, maintenance, turnover, and continued attention. The burden can become less attractive or harder to handle with age. In a REIT structure, the REIT manages the portfolio while the investor receives distributions. That can end the work of being a landlord without requiring the owner to sell first and take a current taxable gain.

Passivity can be valuable, but it comes with less direct control. An investor who wants to continue managing, improving, or actively acquiring property may find a 721 exchange premature. The move into passive REIT ownership is generally one-way, so readiness matters.

## Diversification and Risk Reduction

Retirement changes the cost of concentration. An investor may no longer have employment income to recover from a major loss and may rely on accumulated wealth for the rest of life. Moving from one or a few properties into a portfolio with many properties, markets, and tenants can reduce the impact of any one problem.

Diversification is not the same as a guarantee of capital preservation. REIT units can still face property, market, debt, distribution, and liquidity risk. The value is a reduction in one-property dependence, not an assurance that no loss can occur. Retirement allocations should be sized in light of the investor's broader portfolio.

## Deferral and Estate Planning

A sale can trigger a large tax, potentially a third or more of the gain in some situations, depending on capital gain, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state taxes. A qualifying 721 contribution defers gain, preserving the pre-tax amount in the REIT investment. The deferred tax can become current if units are converted or redeemed in a taxable disposition.

For estate planning, units held until death may receive a step-up in basis that can eliminate deferred gain. Their divisibility can also ease transfers among multiple heirs. The [tax benefits of a 721](insights.html), [step-up in basis at death](insights.html), and [transfer and liquidity for multiple heirs](insights.html) should be considered with the family's estate attorney, not in isolation.

## Managing Liquidity in Retirement

Distributions can cover regular income needs. Larger needs, such as healthcare or other major expenses, may require access to principal. OP units can provide a path by conversion to shares and then a cash sale, while remaining units can continue to be held. A gradual conversion can spread taxable gain, although every conversion has tax consequences.

Liquidity depends on the destination. After conversion, a traded REIT may offer more reliable public-market liquidity. A non-traded REIT can have more limited liquidity, so the investor should plan accordingly. A retirement plan needs to balance current distributions, cash reserves, potential conversion tax, and the desire to keep some units toward a step-up.

### Key Takeaways

- A 721 can move an active owner toward passive retirement income through REIT distributions.
- Diversification can reduce one-property concentration risk, an important retirement consideration, but cannot eliminate investment risk.
- A qualifying contribution can defer low-basis gain and support estate planning through a step-up and divisible units.
- Retirement liquidity requires planning: distributions for routine needs and potentially tax-managed conversions for larger needs, with liquidity varying by REIT type.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why are retiring investors good candidates for 721 exchanges?

The strategy can address several retirement goals at once: ending active management, receiving passive distributions, diversifying concentration risk, deferring low-basis gain, and planning an estate. The fit is strongest when the investor is ready for passive ownership and the liquidity, risk, and estate terms match their situation.

### How does a 721 exchange provide retirement income?

It converts active real estate into OP units that can pay distributions from a REIT portfolio. This can replace rental income that required management with passive income from diversified real estate. The distributions may be relatively more stable than one property's income, but they are not guaranteed.

### Why is passivity important in retirement?

Property management takes work: tenants, maintenance, turnover, and decision-making. It can become harder with age. A 721 transition places management with the REIT and allows the investor to receive distributions instead of operating the property directly.

### How does diversification help in retirement?

Retirees may have less ability to rebuild wealth after a severe property loss. A diversified REIT portfolio spreads exposure across properties, markets, and tenants, so one problem can have less effect than it would with a concentrated holding. It reduces concentration risk, not all investment risk.

### Does the 721 exchange help with estate planning in retirement?

It can. A retiring investor can hold units for income during life and pass them to heirs. A step-up at death can erase deferred gain for income-tax purposes, while divisible units can simplify transfers among multiple beneficiaries. Estate tax and partnership terms remain separate considerations.

### Can I access cash for retirement needs from OP units?

Yes, potentially through distributions for regular income and conversion of units to shares for larger needs. A gradual conversion may help manage tax while the owner holds remaining units. Liquidity after conversion is generally more reliable for a traded REIT than a non-traded REIT, so the details need planning.

### Is the 721 exchange better than selling for retiring investors?

For owners who want to stay invested for income and estate planning, a 721 can be more advantageous because a taxable sale can trigger a large tax—sometimes a third or more of the gain—and permanently reduce the amount invested. A qualifying 721 defers the tax and may preserve more pre-tax wealth. A sale can still fit an owner who wants to fully cash out and accepts the tax. The choice is situation-dependent.

### How do retiring investors reach a REIT?

Often through a DST-then-721 path. The investor completes a 1031 exchange into a DST for passive, tax-deferred real-estate income, then a later 721 exit can lead to a diversified, more liquid REIT. A direct contribution is possible only when a REIT wants the specific property. The DST bridge itself can be a retirement-income outcome even if the later exit does not occur.

### What if I need to keep working with my real estate longer?

If the owner still wants to manage property or actively invest, a 721 can be premature because it is generally a one-way move into passive REIT ownership. Direct ownership or a 1031 into a DST for partial passivity may fit until the owner is ready to stop active work.

### Is the distribution income reliable enough for retirement?

Portfolio distributions can be relatively stable because they are spread across many properties, but they are not guaranteed. They can be reduced based on portfolio income and policy. They can be one meaningful income source alongside other income and reserves, not a substitute for a complete retirement plan.

### Can I do a partial 721 exchange for retirement?

Potentially. An investor may transition some property for passive retirement income and diversification while retaining other direct real estate to remain partly active. Whether a partial approach is feasible depends on the property, REIT, and goals. It can ease a retirement transition without requiring all property to move at once.

### How does the 721 fit with my other retirement accounts?

OP units are direct real-estate investments held outside retirement accounts. They can complement retirement accounts and other investments as part of taxable holdings, alongside Social Security and other income sources. A financial adviser and CPA should coordinate the units with the broader retirement portfolio.

### What if I want to leave my real estate to heirs but enjoy income now?

That is one potential fit for a 721: hold OP units for passive distributions during life and pass the units to heirs at death. A step-up can erase deferred gain for income-tax purposes and the units can divide cleanly. The arrangement should be evaluated against estate-tax exposure, unit terms, liquidity needs, and the family's actual plan.

## Glossary

- **Retiring Investor:** A real-estate owner moving toward retirement and passive ownership.
- **Retirement Income:** Passive distributions from OP units in retirement.
- **Passivity:** Hands-off REIT ownership that frees an owner from management.
- **Distributions:** The passive income stream from the REIT portfolio.
- **Diversification:** Spreading risk across a REIT portfolio.
- **Risk Reduction:** Lowering one-property concentration risk.
- **Deferral:** Preserving pre-tax wealth by deferring gain.
- **Estate Planning:** Passing wealth to heirs, a common retirement focus.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** A death-time basis reset that can serve the estate plan.
- **Divisible Units:** OP units that can be divided among heirs.
- **DST-then-721 Path:** A route to REIT ownership through a DST.
- **Liquidity Path:** Converting units to address retirement cash needs.
- **Stable Income:** The relative stability of diversified distributions, not a guarantee.
- **Concentration Risk:** Dependence on one property or limited holdings.
- **Tired Landlord:** A retiring owner ready to stop managing property.
- **Retirement Transition:** The shift from active property ownership to a passive role.
