# Interval Funds vs. Non-Traded REITs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I have been thinking about how often investors use “illiquid alternatives” as though it were a complete description. It is not. Two investments can both limit liquidity and still behave very differently when an investor needs capital, receives a tax form, or learns what the portfolio actually owns.

Interval funds and non-traded REITs are often mentioned together because each can offer income-producing investments outside public exchanges. They are genuinely different vehicles. An interval fund is a registered closed-end fund under the Investment Company Act of 1940—the “’40 Act”—and makes scheduled periodic repurchase offers, usually quarterly, at net asset value (NAV). A non-traded REIT is a real-estate-focused REIT subject to the 90% distribution rule; its redemption program is discretionary and its board can gate or suspend it.

First-order thinking sees two illiquid funds and looks for the higher current distribution. Second-order thinking separates structure, holdings, liquidity mechanics, fees, tax reporting, and the role each vehicle plays in a whole portfolio. Both are illiquid and generally suit longer-term investors. Verify current rules with an advisor, and remember that past performance does not guarantee future results.

## What an Interval Fund Is

An interval fund is a registered closed-end fund organized under the ’40 Act. It does not trade on an exchange. Instead, liquidity comes through periodic repurchase offers: at regular, scheduled intervals—most commonly quarterly—the fund offers to buy back a stated portion of investor shares at NAV. Between those windows, an investor generally cannot redeem. That makes an interval fund a longer-term investment despite its built-in, limited liquidity mechanism.

Because it is a registered fund rather than a single-asset vehicle, an interval fund can hold a diversified, multi-asset portfolio of illiquid or alternative investments. Depending on its mandate, that can include real estate, private credit, private equity, infrastructure, and other strategies that would not fit easily in a daily-liquid mutual fund or ETF.

The structure gives a manager a more stable capital base for holding illiquid assets without forced selling, while giving ordinary investors access to professionally managed, institutional-style alternatives through one fund. An interval fund is therefore a ’40 Act fund wrapper with scheduled, partial liquidity—not an exchange-traded investment or a promise of ready cash.

## Periodic Repurchase Mechanics

The defining feature is the repurchase schedule. A fund commits in advance to make offers at set intervals. Quarterly is common, but some funds use monthly, semiannual, or annual schedules. Each offer covers a stated percentage of outstanding shares, typically 5% to 25%—a range from 5 to 25 percent—with 5% common for quarterly funds. During the offer window, an investor may submit a redemption request, and the fund repurchases shares at the NAV set for that offer, up to the available amount.

The distinction is important: an interval fund’s offer is scheduled and mandated by its policies, rather than left to the manager’s day-to-day discretion. But the size of the offer is limited. If requested redemptions exceed the offered percentage, the fund prorates requests and fulfills only part of each one. The investor has recurring, predictable windows, but may not be able to redeem an entire position in one window.

> The interval fund’s signature is the calendar: a commitment to buy back a stated slice of shares at regular intervals, not an assurance that every investor can exit in full at once.

## How It Differs From a Non-Traded REIT

The structural difference comes first. An interval fund is a registered investment company, a closed-end fund under the ’40 Act, with the disclosure and investor protections that framework brings. A non-traded REIT is a real-estate company. To qualify as a REIT it must satisfy REIT rules, including holding mostly real estate and distributing at least 90% of taxable income.

The liquidity structure differs too. Interval-fund repurchase offers are scheduled and mandated. A non-traded REIT’s redemption program is discretionary: its board may cap, reduce, gate, or suspend redemptions. Both vehicles can limit or prorate redemptions, but the interval fund gives a more predictable cadence in form. It does not create unlimited liquidity or guarantee an outcome in stressed conditions.

One vehicle is a fund. The other is a real-estate company. That single distinction carries into holdings, tax reporting, and how the liquidity promise should be understood.

## Liquidity & Holdings Compared

Neither vehicle provides on-demand exit at a market price like an exchange-traded security. Both are fundamentally illiquid, both cap redemptions, and both can prorate when demand is heavy. The interval fund is obligated to make periodic offers, while a non-traded REIT board has greater latitude to gate or suspend a redemption program. The interval fund’s timing is therefore somewhat more reliable in form, though still limited in size.

Holdings are the other major difference. A non-traded REIT is a focused real-estate vehicle and must satisfy REIT asset and income tests. An interval fund can hold a diversified, multi-strategy portfolio: real estate alongside private credit, private equity, infrastructure, structured credit, and other alternatives. Its specific mix depends entirely on its mandate.

The decision is not about which label sounds more flexible. It is about whether an investor wants concentrated real-estate exposure or a broader alternatives allocation, and whether the liquidity mechanism fits the capital being committed.

## Key Takeaways

- An interval fund is a registered ’40 Act closed-end fund that makes scheduled, mandated periodic repurchases, often quarterly, for 5% to 25% of shares at NAV.
- A non-traded REIT is a real-estate-focused REIT, subject to the 90% distribution rule, with a discretionary redemption program that its board can gate or suspend.
- Interval funds can hold diversified, multi-asset portfolios including real estate, private credit, and more; non-traded REITs hold real estate.
- Both are illiquid and longer-term. Interval funds generally use Form 1099 and fund taxation; non-traded REITs generally use Form 1099-DIV and REIT-dividend taxation, including Section 199A treatment for qualifying dividends.

## Tax & Reporting Differences

The two vehicles are taxed under different regimes. A non-traded REIT passes most income through as REIT dividends reported on Form 1099-DIV. Most ordinary REIT dividends are taxed as ordinary income because the REIT does not pay corporate tax. A 20% deduction under Section 199A can apply to qualified REIT dividends, and distributions can also include return of capital or capital-gain components.

An interval fund is generally taxed as a registered investment company. Its distributions are reported on Form 1099 and take their character from the income the fund earns and passes through. That can include ordinary income, including interest from private credit, qualified dividends, short- and long-term capital gains, and, for some funds, return of capital. The mix can differ substantially from one fund to another because it reflects the fund’s holdings and strategy.

If an interval fund invests in REITs, some distributions may carry REIT-dividend character eligible for the Section 199A deduction. In general, however, an interval fund follows fund taxation rather than direct REIT-dividend taxation. The tax distinction matters, and it is a question for a tax advisor, not a shortcut based on a product label.

> Even the tax forms diverge: the non-traded REIT uses a 1099-DIV and REIT-dividend treatment, while interval-fund distributions on a 1099 depend on what the fund owns.

## Which Suits Your Goals

An interval fund can fit an investor seeking diversified, multi-strategy alternatives in one registered fund, who values a scheduled—even if limited—liquidity cadence and is comfortable with fund-style taxation. Its breadth can include real estate, private credit, private equity, and other strategies.

A non-traded REIT can fit an investor who specifically wants real-estate exposure and REIT-dividend tax characteristics, including Section 199A treatment on qualifying dividends, and who accepts a discretionary redemption program. It is purpose-built for focused real-estate exposure rather than a broad alternatives blend.

Both choices require comfort with illiquidity and a long time horizon. Match the structure to the objective: diversified alternatives with scheduled partial repurchases and fund taxation, or focused real estate with REIT taxation and discretionary redemptions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is an interval fund?

An interval fund is a registered closed-end fund organized under the Investment Company Act of 1940, or ’40 Act. It does not trade on an exchange. It offers periodic repurchases at NAV—usually quarterly—for a stated portion of shares. Between windows, investors generally cannot redeem. The fund can hold illiquid alternatives such as real estate, private credit, private equity, and infrastructure, offering professionally managed, institutional-style exposure with scheduled but limited liquidity.

### How do interval fund repurchases work?

The fund schedules and mandates offers in advance. They are commonly quarterly, although some use monthly, semiannual, or annual schedules. Each offer commonly covers 5% to 25% of shares, with 5% common for a quarterly fund. Investors submit requests during the window and receive NAV-based repurchases up to the offer amount. If requests exceed the cap, they are prorated. Review the specific repurchase policy before investing.

### What is the difference between an interval fund and a non-traded REIT?

The differences are structure, holdings, liquidity, and tax. An interval fund is a registered ’40 Act fund and can hold a multi-asset portfolio; a non-traded REIT is a real-estate company subject to REIT rules including the 90% distribution requirement. Interval funds make scheduled, mandated repurchases, commonly quarterly and often for 5% to 25% of shares. A non-traded REIT has a discretionary, gateable redemption program. Interval funds generally use Form 1099 and fund taxation; non-traded REITs generally use Form 1099-DIV and REIT-dividend treatment, including Section 199A on qualifying dividends. Both are illiquid.

### Are interval funds and non-traded REITs both illiquid?

Yes. Neither trades on an exchange or permits an on-demand exit at a market price. Both cap buybacks and can prorate redemptions. Interval-fund offers are scheduled and mandated, so the timing is more predictable; non-traded REIT boards can cap, reduce, gate, or suspend redemptions. Neither should be treated as a source of ready capital, especially in stressed markets.

### What can an interval fund invest in?

An interval fund can invest in a broad range of illiquid and alternative assets because it offers liquidity periodically rather than daily. Holdings can include real estate, private credit and private loans, private equity, infrastructure, structured credit, and other less-liquid strategies. The fund may blend these into a multi-asset portfolio, but its specific holdings depend on its mandate. A non-traded REIT, by contrast, must hold mostly real estate to meet REIT rules.

### How are interval funds taxed?

An interval fund is generally taxed as a registered investment company. Its distributions are reported on Form 1099 and take their character from underlying income: ordinary income, qualified dividends, short- or long-term gains, and sometimes return of capital. A fund that owns REITs may pass through some Section 199A-eligible REIT-dividend character, but an interval fund generally follows fund taxation rather than direct REIT-dividend taxation. Confirm fund-specific treatment with a tax advisor.

### Which has more predictable liquidity, an interval fund or a non-traded REIT?

An interval fund generally has more predictable liquidity in timing because it is committed to periodic, scheduled repurchase offers, often quarterly. A non-traded REIT’s program is discretionary and may be capped, reduced, gated, or suspended. More predictable does not mean unlimited or guaranteed: both cap redemptions, both can prorate during heavy demand, and interval offers remain limited in size.

### Can an interval fund invest in real estate?

Yes. Many interval funds invest in real estate or real-estate debt as a primary focus or as part of a diversified portfolio. They can also combine real estate with private credit, private equity, infrastructure, and other strategies. A real-estate-focused interval fund and a non-traded REIT can overlap in exposure, but they remain different in structure, liquidity mechanics, and tax: fund taxation and scheduled repurchases versus REIT taxation and a discretionary redemption program.

### Do interval funds have fees?

Yes. An interval fund has an annual expense ratio for management and operations, often higher than plain index mutual funds or ETFs because it holds actively managed, illiquid alternatives. Some funds also have performance or incentive fees, sales charges or distribution and servicing fees for some share classes, and layered fees from underlying funds or strategies. Those costs can materially affect long-term net returns. Review the full fee schedule and prospectus, not only the headline expense ratio.

### Which is better for real estate exposure, an interval fund or a non-traded REIT?

Neither is universally better. A non-traded REIT gives focused real-estate exposure, REIT-dividend tax treatment, and potential Section 199A treatment for qualifying dividends, with a discretionary redemption program. A real-estate interval fund offers a fund wrapper with scheduled periodic repurchases and fund taxation, and may include other strategies for a broader mix. Compare the specific holdings, liquidity terms, fees, tax treatment, and suitability rather than treating the vehicles as interchangeable.

### Are interval funds and non-traded REITs suitable for all investors?

No. Both are illiquid, longer-term investments and are generally offered to accredited or otherwise suitable investors through a broker-dealer after a suitability review. That review considers financial circumstances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. They are not generally appropriate for capital needed soon or for emergency funds. Fees apply, NAV and distributions can fall, and underlying illiquid holdings can lose value.

### Can I hold both an interval fund and a non-traded REIT?

Yes, for a suitable long-term investor. A non-traded REIT provides focused real-estate exposure and REIT-dividend treatment, while an interval fund can add broader multi-strategy alternatives with scheduled liquidity. Holding both can diversify structures and assets, but both are illiquid. The combined allocation must be sized carefully relative to overall liquidity needs, fees, and risk tolerance, and each requires suitability review.

### Why are interval funds becoming more popular?

They offer registered, accessible entry to alternatives that were often limited to institutions or very wealthy investors. Private credit, private real estate, and private equity are inherently illiquid and do not fit well in daily-liquid funds. Periodic repurchases give managers a stable capital base while providing investors partial, scheduled liquidity. Professional management, multi-asset diversification, and the ’40 Act’s disclosure and investor protections have supported demand, as has investor demand for income and diversification beyond public stocks and bonds. Popularity is not an investment thesis; the vehicles remain illiquid, fee-bearing, and appropriate only for suitable long-term investors.

## Glossary

- **Interval Fund:** A registered closed-end fund offering scheduled periodic repurchases.
- **Closed-End Fund:** A ’40 Act fund with a fixed share count that is not exchange-redeemable.
- **’40 Act:** The Investment Company Act of 1940 governing registered funds.
- **Repurchase Offer:** An interval fund’s scheduled, mandated buyback of shares at NAV.
- **Repurchase Interval:** The set schedule for offers, often quarterly.
- **Proration:** Partial fulfillment when redemption requests exceed an offer.
- **Non-Traded REIT:** An SEC-registered, real-estate-focused REIT that is not exchange-listed.
- **Discretionary Redemption:** A non-traded REIT board program that can be gated or suspended.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The REIT requirement to pay out most taxable income.
- **Net Asset Value (NAV):** The per-share value used to price repurchases for both vehicles.
- **Multi-Asset Portfolio:** An interval fund’s blend of real estate, credit, and more.
- **Private Credit:** Private loans an interval fund may hold for income.
- **Form 1099-DIV:** The form reporting a non-traded REIT’s dividends.
- **Fund Taxation:** An interval fund’s tax treatment based on underlying income.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.
- **Suitability Review:** An assessment of whether either illiquid vehicle fits an investor.

## Which should you choose?

An interval fund fits when an investor wants diversified exposure—often including credit, equity, and alternatives—with a scheduled repurchase window for liquidity.

A non-traded REIT fits when an investor specifically wants real-estate exposure and accepts that a redemption program may be limited or gated when many investors seek to exit. Match the liquidity mechanism and the holdings to the actual goal, and read each prospectus’s repurchase terms carefully.
