# Hotel & Lodging REITs Explained

I have been thinking about a simple contrast that gets missed when investors group every REIT together: an office tenant may sign for years, while a hotel guest may stay one night. The asset may be real estate in both cases. The cash-flow behavior is not remotely the same.

Hotel and lodging REITs own hotels whose income resets nightly. They do not generally collect long-term rent to smooth that reset. Their revenue is highly sensitive to travel and the economy, and high operating leverage amplifies the resulting swings in profit.

First-order thinking sees a full hotel, an attractive dividend, or a strong RevPAR number and assumes the trend will continue. Second-order thinking asks whether rate gains are holding, whether demand is broad, how much new supply is coming, how fixed costs behave if occupancy weakens, and what is already reflected in the price. Lodging is the most cyclical and volatile of the major REIT sectors precisely because those answers can change quickly.

## What Lodging REITs Own

Lodging REITs own hotel real estate, but they generally do not operate the hotels directly. A typical REIT uses a hotel brand through a franchise or management agreement, or hires a third-party manager. That operator handles staffing, reservations, pricing, and guest services.

The REIT receives the economic results of hotel operations after management fees, rather than a fixed contractual rent payment. That is the core distinction. The investor is exposed to actual travel demand, room rates, occupancy, and operating costs.

Portfolios can span economy and select-service hotels, full-service and upscale properties, luxury hotels, and resorts. Each segment has a different demand and cost profile. Luxury and resort travel, for example, may behave differently through a cycle than economy or select-service demand. Geographic and market mix matters as well.

The important point is that lodging REIT income is operating income, not lease income. The absence of long leases defines much of the sector's risk profile.

## Why Hotel Income Is Cyclical

In most REIT sectors, a tenant signs a multi-year lease that locks in rent and provides some income smoothing through a downturn. A hotel is the opposite. Each room is effectively re-leased each night, and both the rate and occupancy can change daily.

That nightly reset means lodging revenue responds almost immediately to economic conditions. In strong periods for business and leisure travel, occupancy and room rates can rise together. In a recession or travel disruption, both can fall quickly. There is no contractual cushion to delay the impact.

Hotels also carry substantial fixed and semi-fixed costs: staffing, building maintenance, utilities, and management. When revenue rises, more of each additional dollar may reach profit after those costs are covered. When revenue falls, the costs remain and profit can decline faster than revenue. That is operating leverage.

> Hotels do not sign long leases; they sell stays. Income resets every evening, which is why lodging lives so close to the economic cycle.

## RevPAR and Operating Metrics

RevPAR is the primary top-line measure for lodging. It is revenue per available room, calculated as ADR times occupancy. It captures both how much a hotel charges for occupied rooms and how full the hotel is across all available rooms.

ADR measures pricing power. Occupancy measures demand. A hotel may show high occupancy with low rates, filling rooms cheaply, or lower occupancy with premium rates. Two hotels can reach a similar RevPAR by different paths. Tracking ADR and occupancy separately helps show whether performance is coming from price, demand, or both.

Investors also watch total revenue, including food, beverage, and other income, plus operating margins and hotel EBITDA. Because operating leverage is high, margin and EBITDA changes can be much larger than a shift in RevPAR alone might first suggest. ## Key Takeaways

- Lodging REITs own hotels run by brands or managers and receive operating results rather than fixed rent.
- Hotel income resets nightly, has no long leases, and carries high operating leverage. That makes lodging highly cyclical and volatile.
- RevPAR—ADR times occupancy—is the defining metric, combining pricing power and demand.
- Lodging is a higher-risk, higher-return REIT sector that calls for cycle awareness and appropriate position sizing.

## Sector Risks

The first risk is economic and demand sensitivity. A pullback in business or leisure travel can reduce occupancy, rates, and revenue immediately. Operating leverage then amplifies the hit to profit.

Travel-disruption risk is closely related. Events that sharply curtail travel can cause rapid and severe revenue declines. The sector has experienced this in major shocks, and its nightly-reset income offers no long lease to cushion the effect.

Supply matters, too. Hotels can be developed relatively quickly in attractive markets. New rooms compete for the same demand and can pressure occupancy, ADR, and RevPAR even while overall travel looks healthy. The local development pipeline is therefore part of any lodging analysis. Markets where supply is harder to add can have more durable pricing power, but supply conditions vary and should be checked rather than assumed.

Lodging also has labor and operating-cost risk. High fixed costs can squeeze margins when wages or other expenses rise. Hotels require ongoing renovation and refurbishment to remain competitive, adding capital intensity. Interest rates and leverage bring the standard REIT risks on top of all this.

> A hotel can be built quickly, emptied by a travel shock, and squeezed by costs. Lodging stacks more cyclical pressure points than most REIT sectors.

## Investing Through the Cycle

Lodging is better approached as a cycle-aware allocation than as a stable-income holding. Hotel REITs tend to do better in expansions marked by rising RevPAR and weaker in recessions or disruptions. Timing and the economic backdrop matter more here than in lease-driven sectors.

The same cyclical behavior that creates sharp drawdowns can support strong recoveries when travel returns. That is the higher-return side of the higher-risk profile. It does not make the sector defensive.

A measured approach includes modest position sizing, attention to portfolio quality and well-located markets, and ongoing review of supply and the economy. Lodging should not be relied on for the kind of [steady cash flow income REITs target](income-reits-explained-investing-for-cash-flow.html); distributions can be cut sharply in a downturn. Investors need realistic, non-promissory expectations and enough risk capacity to remain patient through the cycle.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a hotel or lodging REIT?

A hotel or lodging REIT is a Real Estate Investment Trust that owns hotels and earns income from their operations. It generally uses a hotel brand through a franchise or management agreement, or a third-party manager, to run the property. The REIT receives operating results after management fees instead of long-term fixed rent. Like all REITs, it must distribute at least 90% of taxable income. Portfolios range from economy and select-service hotels to full-service, upscale, luxury, and resort properties. Because results depend on travel demand, rates, occupancy, and costs, lodging is the most cyclical and volatile major REIT sector.

### Why is hotel income so cyclical?

Most REIT tenants sign multi-year leases. Hotel guests book one night or a few nights, so rooms are re-leased and repriced continuously. Rates and occupancy can change daily with demand, leaving no contractual income cushion. In strong periods, business and leisure travel can lift both occupancy and room rates. In recessions or travel disruptions, they can drop sharply. Large fixed costs create operating leverage, so profit falls faster than revenue in a downturn and rises faster when revenue recovers. Those mechanics make hotel income highly cyclical.

### What is RevPAR?

RevPAR is revenue per available room, and it is the core lodging metric. It equals ADR multiplied by occupancy and is calculated across all available rooms, not only occupied rooms. That combines pricing and demand in one figure. A hotel can grow RevPAR by charging more, filling more rooms, or both. ADR measures the price guests pay; occupancy measures how many rooms are filled. RevPAR is the primary measure investors and operators use for top-line room performance and for comparing hotels and periods.

### What is the difference between ADR and occupancy?

ADR, or average daily rate, is the average price charged per occupied room per night. It measures pricing power. Occupancy is the percentage of available rooms that are filled. It measures demand. RevPAR combines them by multiplying ADR by occupancy. The distinction matters: one hotel may fill rooms cheaply with high occupancy and low ADR, while another may command premium rates with lower occupancy. Both may report a similar RevPAR, but the drivers and implications differ. Tracking them separately shows whether performance is coming from pricing strength or additional demand.

### Are hotel REITs riskier than other REITs?

Generally, yes. Lodging REITs are commonly considered the highest-risk and most volatile major REIT sector because income resets nightly, responds immediately to the economy, and has no long-term lease cushion. High operating leverage magnifies gains and losses. New supply, labor and operating costs, renovation needs, interest rates, and leverage add risk. Stable lease-driven sectors such as net-lease retail or medical office tend to have less direct exposure to nightly travel demand. Lodging may have higher return potential in strong markets, but this is a general sector characteristic, not a prediction; current conditions should be verified.

### Why don't hotel REITs have long leases?

The hotel business sells rooms night by night instead of leasing space for years. Guests book a short stay, and rooms are repriced and re-booked based on current demand. There is no multi-year office or retail tenant paying a set rent. A hotel brand or third-party manager may operate the property, but the REIT still receives the hotel's operating results from nightly room sales rather than contractual rent. That structural absence of long leases is why lodging revenue resets continuously and reacts so quickly to economic conditions.

### What is operating leverage in hotels?

Operating leverage describes how a high share of fixed and semi-fixed costs makes profit move more than revenue. Staffing, building maintenance, utilities, and management costs do not fall much when occupancy declines. When RevPAR rises, more incremental revenue can flow to profit after those costs are covered. When revenue falls, the costs remain and margins can compress or disappear. This is why hotel EBITDA and cash available for distributions can be so volatile across the travel and economic cycle: profits magnify revenue movement in both directions.

### How does new supply affect hotel REITs?

New supply adds rooms competing for the same travel demand. Hotels can be developed relatively quickly in attractive markets, often after a strong period for rates and occupancy. If supply grows faster than demand, occupancy and ADR can decline, putting pressure on RevPAR even when travel remains healthy. Investors review the development pipeline in a REIT's key markets because a wave of new rooms can weaken pricing power and returns. Supply-constrained markets can support stronger RevPAR, but conditions differ by market and change over time, so projected supply should be verified.

### Are hotel REIT dividends reliable?

They are generally less reliable than dividends in more stable REIT sectors. Lodging REITs must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, but their taxable income and cash flow can swing sharply with travel and economic conditions. Distributions may be strong in expansions and cut sharply or suspended in recessions or travel disruptions. High operating leverage increases this variability. Lodging is not a dependable stable-income substitute; an investor should expect dividends to follow the cycle. This is not a prediction. Distributions vary, past performance does not guarantee future results, and current conditions should be verified.

### How do I invest in lodging through the cycle?

Approach lodging with its cyclicality in mind, not as a steady-income position. Hotel REIT performance often strengthens during expansions with rising RevPAR and weakens during recessions or disruptions. A prudent approach can include modest sizing, a preference for quality portfolios and well-located, supply-constrained markets, and careful attention to the supply pipeline and broader economy. Do not rely on lodging for stable income because distributions can be cut sharply. The sector can support recovery upside as travel returns, but it is a tactical, higher-risk allocation rather than a defensive one. This is a general approach, not personalized advice.

### What hotel segments do lodging REITs own?

Lodging REITs can own economy, select-service, full-service, upscale, luxury, and resort hotels. Economy and select-service hotels tend to offer fewer amenities at lower rates, often serving budget-conscious and roadside travelers with relatively lower operating costs. Full-service and upscale hotels offer restaurants, meeting space, and more services, usually at higher rates and with greater business and group-travel exposure. Luxury and resort properties serve high-end leisure and destination travel. Some lodging REITs focus on a particular segment, while others hold diversified portfolios spanning several. Segment and geographic mix affect demand, cost structure, and cycle sensitivity.

### How do travel disruptions affect lodging REITs?

They can affect lodging more suddenly and severely than many other real estate sectors because hotel income depends on travel and resets nightly. Economic shocks, health crises, and other interruptions can reduce occupancy and revenue almost immediately, with no long leases to cushion the impact. High operating leverage then magnifies the profit decline, and distributions can be cut or suspended. The sector has experienced rapid, severe revenue declines during major travel shocks and recoveries as travel resumed. The timing and severity of future disruptions are unpredictable, so this is a general risk characteristic rather than a forecast.

### Are lodging REITs good for income investors?

Lodging REITs are generally not well suited as a primary source of stable income. They must distribute at least 90% of taxable income, but that income can be very cyclical. Dividends may be strong in good times and reduced or suspended in recessions and travel disruptions. Investors seeking steadier cash flow often look to more defensive, lease-driven sectors such as net-lease retail, medical office, or certain residential property. Lodging offers a higher-risk, higher-return profile for investors who can accept variable distributions and drawdowns. No return is promised, and the allocation should fit goals and risk tolerance.

## Glossary

**Lodging REIT:** A REIT that owns hotels and earns their operating results.

**RevPAR:** Revenue per available room—ADR multiplied by occupancy.

**Average Daily Rate (ADR):** The average price charged per occupied room per night.

**Occupancy:** The percentage of available hotel rooms that are filled.

**Operating Leverage:** Fixed costs that amplify profit swings as revenue moves.

**Hotel EBITDA:** A measure of a hotel's operating profitability.

**Management Agreement:** A contract under which a manager operates the hotel.

**Franchise Agreement:** A contract to operate a hotel under a brand.

**Select-Service Hotel:** A hotel offering limited amenities at moderate rates.

**Full-Service Hotel:** A hotel offering extensive amenities and services.

**Cyclicality:** The tendency of income to swing with the economic cycle.

**New Supply:** Newly built hotel rooms that compete for demand.

**Travel-Disruption Risk:** Risk of sudden revenue loss when travel is curtailed.

**Capital Intensity:** The ongoing renovation and refurbishment hotels require.

**Business vs. Leisure Travel:** The two main sources of hotel demand.

**Resort:** A destination hotel catering to leisure and vacation travel.
