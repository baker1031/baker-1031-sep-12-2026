I keep seeing DST conversations start with the convenience of a passive exchange and stop there. The structure earns its place only when the property, sponsor, debt, fees, and liquidity fit the investor who must live with it.

First-order thinking sees passive ownership and a way to meet a 1031 timeline. Second-order thinking considers the 5–10-year illiquid hold, sponsor execution, fee load, debt, diversification, and the right replacement role.

<section class="about-section" id="overview">
<div class="kicker">Overview</div>
<h2 class="optima">A fractional interest the IRS treats as like-kind real property.</h2>
<p>A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) is a legal entity that holds title to one or more real estate assets on behalf of many investors. Each investor owns a beneficial interest in the trust rather than a deeded piece of the property, and the trust — through a professional sponsor — handles financing, management, and eventual sale. In 2004, IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86 confirmed that a properly structured DST interest is treated as like-kind real property, making it eligible as 1031 exchange replacement property.</p>
<p>For investors selling appreciated real estate, that ruling is the whole point: a DST can absorb exchange proceeds — including the debt that must be replaced — without the investor signing on a new loan or managing a building. Minimums are typically $25,000 to $100,000, so a single exchange can be diversified across several DSTs, property types, and sponsors.</p>
</section>
<hr class="divider">
<section class="about-section" id="how">
<div class="kicker">How It Works</div>
<h2 class="optima">From sponsor acquisition to full cycle</h2>
<div class="steps">
<div class="step"><div class="n">01</div><div><h3>Sponsor Acquires the Asset</h3><p>A DST sponsor buys an institutional property (or portfolio), arranges any non-recourse financing, and places it into the trust before investors come in.</p></div></div>
<div class="step"><div class="n">02</div><div><h3>You Purchase Beneficial Interests</h3><p>Through a broker-dealer, accredited investors buy interests via private placement memorandum, often using 1031 exchange proceeds within the 45/180-day windows.</p></div></div>
<div class="step"><div class="n">03</div><div><h3>The Sponsor Manages, You Receive Distributions</h3><p>All operations — leasing, capital projects, lender relations — are handled by the sponsor and its asset manager. Investors receive their pro-rata share of cash flow, typically monthly.</p></div></div>
<div class="step"><div class="n">04</div><div><h3>Full-Cycle Sale or 721 Roll-Up</h3><p>When the trust sells, investors can 1031 again into a new DST, exchange into a REIT via a 721 UPREIT, or cash out and pay deferred tax.</p></div></div>
</div>
</section>

<section class="about-section" id="benefits">
<div class="kicker">Benefits</div>
<h2 class="optima">Why exchangers choose DSTs</h2>
<div class="pillars" style="grid-template-columns:repeat(2,1fr);">
<div class="pillar"><h3>Truly Passive</h3><p>No tenants, toilets, or trash — the sponsor runs everything. DSTs are popular with investors exiting active management.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>Debt Comes Pre-Packaged</h3><p>Non-recourse debt is arranged at the trust level, satisfying the 1031 requirement to replace debt without a personal loan application.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>Diversification</h3><p>Lower minimums let one exchange spread across sectors, geographies, and multiple sponsors instead of a single replacement building.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>Backup-Strategy Fit</h3><p>Because a DST can close quickly, it is widely used as an identified backup so an exchange doesn't fail at the 45-day mark.</p></div>
</div>
</section>

<section class="about-section" id="risks">
<div class="kicker">Considerations &amp; Risks</div>
<h2 class="optima">What to weigh before investing</h2>
<div class="pillars" style="grid-template-columns:repeat(2,1fr);">
<div class="pillar"><h3>Illiquidity</h3><p>DSTs are long-term, illiquid holdings — typically 5&ndash;10 years — with no public secondary market. Plan to hold to the sponsor's full cycle.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>No Investor Control</h3><p>The 'seven deadly sins' rules that protect 1031 status also prohibit investors from making management decisions; you are a passive owner.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>Fees and Load</h3><p>Syndicated DSTs carry sponsor, broker-dealer, and ongoing fees that reduce net return; compare load and projected distributions across offerings.</p></div>
<div class="pillar"><h3>Market and Sponsor Risk</h3><p>Returns depend on the sponsor's execution and on real estate fundamentals. Diversify across sponsors and read each PPM's risk factors.</p></div>
</div>
</section>

<section class="about-section" id="sfaq">
<div class="kicker">Common Questions</div>
<h2 class="optima">Frequently asked questions</h2>
<div style="margin-top:12px;"><details class="faq-item"><summary>What is a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)?</summary><div class="answer">A DST is a legal entity that holds title to institutional real estate and lets multiple investors own fractional beneficial interests. The IRS treats a properly structured DST interest as like-kind real property, so it can serve as 1031 replacement property — giving investors passive, professionally managed ownership.</div></details><details class="faq-item"><summary>Can I use a DST for a 1031 exchange?</summary><div class="answer">Yes. IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86 treats a properly structured DST interest as a direct interest in real estate, making it eligible replacement property in a 1031 exchange.</div></details><details class="faq-item"><summary>What is the typical minimum investment in a DST?</summary><div class="answer">Minimums commonly range from about $25,000 to $100,000, and because a DST can accept precise amounts, it's often used to place an exact slice of exchange proceeds.</div></details><details class="faq-item"><summary>How long is a DST held?</summary><div class="answer">Most programs target a five-to-ten-year hold, though timing depends on the market and the sponsor's plan. Investors do not control when the sale happens.</div></details><details class="faq-item"><summary>What happens when a DST sells?</summary><div class="answer">The sponsor sells and returns capital. You can then cash out and pay the deferred tax, complete another 1031 exchange, or — where offered — a 721 exchange into a REIT to keep deferring.</div></details><details class="faq-item"><summary>Are DSTs risky?</summary><div class="answer">Yes — DSTs are speculative, illiquid securities that can lose value or principal through market declines, leverage, tenant problems, or sponsor missteps. Read each PPM's risk factors and diversify across sponsors.</div></details></div>
</section>

<section class="about-section" id="sglossary">
<div class="kicker">Glossary</div>
<h2 class="optima">Key DST terms</h2>
<dl class="glossary-list" style="margin-top:8px;"><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Beneficial interest</dt><dd>An investor's ownership share in a DST; treated by the IRS as a direct interest in real property for 1031 purposes.</dd></div><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Rev. Rul. 2004-86</dt><dd>The 2004 IRS ruling confirming that a properly structured DST interest qualifies as like-kind replacement property.</dd></div><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Full cycle</dt><dd>A DST program that has been acquired, held, and sold — so its realized investor return can be measured.</dd></div><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Non-recourse debt</dt><dd>Loan secured only by the property; the lender cannot pursue investors personally, satisfying the 1031 debt-replacement rule.</dd></div><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Sponsor</dt><dd>The firm that acquires the asset, structures the trust, and manages it on investors' behalf.</dd></div><div class="glossary-term"><dt>Seven deadly sins</dt><dd>IRS restrictions on a DST trustee (e.g., no new capital, no renegotiating leases) that preserve 1031 eligibility and make the investment passive.</dd></div></dl>
