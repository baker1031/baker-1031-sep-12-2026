# DST Returns and Fees: What You Actually Keep

[Back to All Insights](insights.html) · [Home](baker1031.html)

*Delaware Statutory Trust · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 18 min read*

I keep noticing how quickly a projected distribution can become the whole conversation. It is the visible number, so it gets the attention. But a DST’s headline distribution is not the whole story. Once you understand the two sources of return and the fee layers between them and your pocket, you can read an offering honestly.

Two questions decide whether a DST is a good investment: what will it actually return, and what will it cost to get there? Marketing can make both look simpler than they are. Return comes from two separate places that do not necessarily move together, distributions are targets rather than promises, and fees arrive in layers that can compound quietly. This is a framework for examining the economics of any DST critically; none of these figures are predictions, only the anatomy of how the money works.

First-order thinking treats the displayed distribution rate as the return. Second-order thinking separates current income from the capital result at sale, nets every fee, asks whether a payout is supported by property cash flow, and tests the assumptions underneath it.

## Key Takeaways

- DST return has two components: ongoing distributions and the capital result when the property is sold.
- Distributions are typically paid monthly, but they are targets rather than guarantees and can be reduced or suspended.
- Watch for distributions that exceed cash flow. That can be a return of your own capital, not yield.
- Fees generally come in three layers—upfront load, ongoing fees, and a disposition fee—so evaluate a DST net of all of them.

For the larger framework, see [our complete guide to Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html).

## The Two Components of Return

Total DST return has two distinct streams, and blending them together is a common analytical error. The first is **current income**: your share of the property’s net cash flow, paid as distributions during the hold. The second is the **capital result at sale**: your share of the gain or loss when the sponsor sells the property at [full-cycle](dst-full-cycle.html).

Those streams can move independently. A DST may pay steady distributions for years yet return less than your invested capital if property value stalls or leverage works against it. A modest distribution can also accompany a strong sale. A headline yield describes the first stream and says nothing about the second. Assess both over the investment’s full life, not by the first year’s check.

## Distributions: Targets, Not Guarantees

Most DSTs seek to make regular distributions, often monthly, and the projected distribution rate is usually the most prominent figure in a presentation. It is a **target supported by assumptions**, not a coupon you are owed. It depends on the property leasing up, maintaining occupancy, and covering debt service. If any of those falls short, the distribution can be reduced or suspended.

The rate also varies widely with property type and leverage. A stabilized net-lease property and a value-add apartment transaction have very different income profiles. The useful question is what must go right for the projected distribution to hold, and what happens if it does not.

## Return of Capital: The Yield Illusion

One trap can flatter a weak deal: a distribution that **exceeds the property’s actual cash flow**. If a sponsor funds part of a payout from reserves, loan proceeds, or your own subscription, part of the stated “yield” is a **return of your own capital**—your money coming back labeled as income.

That can make the distribution rate look appealing in the short run. Over time, it erodes the capital meant to work for you and can conceal an underperforming asset. A transparent sponsor should show the cash flow supporting the distribution. If a payout appears too good for the property’s economics, ask exactly where the cash is coming from.

## The Fee Layers Explained

DST fees generally fall into three layers:

| Layer | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Upfront load | Selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, organization and offering costs, and acquisition fees, paid when you invest. |
| Ongoing fees | Asset- and property-management fees charged over the hold period. |
| Disposition fee | A fee paid to the sponsor when the property is sold. |

*Fee levels vary by sponsor and offering; the PPM has the specifics.*

Fees are not inherently bad. Institutional real estate, financing, and professional management cost money to assemble. The issue is that fee layers are easy to underestimate when attention stays on the headline distribution alone.

## What the Load Means for Your Money Working

The upfront load deserves attention because it is charged at the start. Part of the investment is used for costs before it earns a return; less than the full investment is “in the ground.” That is not unique to DSTs—most packaged real-estate products work this way—and it can be fair value for what an investor receives.

Still, the underlying real estate must perform somewhat better just to return your full capital. Two offerings with identical projected distributions can produce materially different net results when their loads differ. Ask what fraction of each invested dollar is actually deployed into the property.

## Leverage and Its Effect on Return

Leverage is the other large lever on return, and it cuts in both directions. Borrowing lets a DST control more property per dollar of equity, which can magnify income and appreciation, but losses as well. A debt-free DST gives up that amplification for safety: lower potential return, no refinancing risk, and no chance that a maturing loan forces a bad sale.

Neither approach is automatically better. The appropriate leverage level depends on your risk tolerance and whether your exchange requires debt replacement. What matters is understanding how much of a projected return comes from the real estate and how much comes from borrowing.

## Evaluating Return Net of Fees

Evaluate every DST on a **net** basis: distributions after ongoing fees, total return after the upfront load and disposition fee, and ideally after tax, since pass-through depreciation can shelter some income. A DST with a somewhat lower headline distribution, a leaner fee load, and more conservative, supportable assumptions can leave you better off than a flashier alternative.

Ask the sponsor or your advisor to walk through a net-of-fees projection. Then pressure-test it: what is the return if distributions come in below target or the sale price is flat?

## How the Income Is Taxed

Return is not fully “what you keep” until tax is considered. Because a DST is treated as direct real-estate ownership, your share of depreciation passes through and can shelter part of a distribution from current income tax. A DST distribution and a fully taxable bond coupon with the same headline rate are therefore not equivalent in your pocket.

The sheltered portion is not free. Depreciation reduces basis, and the benefit is generally recaptured when the property sells, or deferred again if you exchange. At [full-cycle](dst-full-cycle.html), deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture come due unless you continue deferring. Compare offerings—and DSTs with alternatives—on an after-tax, net-of-fees basis rather than headline yield. This is general information, not tax advice; your rate, state, and circumstances determine the result, so model it with your CPA.

## Stress-Testing the Projection

Every projection rests on assumptions. Before relying on a headline figure, ask what happens if occupancy runs several points below plan, a key tenant does not renew, or expenses rise faster than rents. For a leveraged DST, ask the harder question: what happens if the loan matures into a higher-rate or weaker market and must be refinanced, or if the property must sell below the underwritten price?

The aim is not certainty; no real-estate offering can provide it. The aim is resilience. An offering whose distribution survives a reasonable downside, and whose debt does not mature at the worst moment, is sturdier than one that works only if everything goes right. If a sponsor cannot or will not explain the downside, treat that as a finding. Before investing, know what would need to go wrong for the investment to disappoint and how likely that is.

## Comparing Offerings Fairly

No two DSTs are structured alike, so a fair comparison requires normalization. Put offerings side by side using the same terms: projected distribution net of ongoing fees, total load as a share of investment, leverage and loan terms, and the credibility of the business plan behind the numbers.

Resist the largest headline yield. It is often the offering assuming the most risk or returning the most capital. The objective is not the highest figure but the best risk-adjusted, net-of-cost outcome from a sponsor you trust. That approach is slower, and more reliable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What return do DSTs pay?

It varies widely by offering and is never guaranteed. Return comes from distributions plus any gain at sale; evaluate each offering’s targets net of every fee instead of relying on a single headline number.

### Are DST distributions guaranteed?

No. Distributions are targets supported by property cash flow and can be reduced or suspended if the property underperforms. Watch for payouts that exceed cash flow, which can be a return of capital.

### What fees do DSTs charge?

Usually three layers apply: an upfront load—selling, dealer-manager, organization, offering, and acquisition fees—ongoing management fees, and a disposition fee at sale. They vary by sponsor and are disclosed in the PPM.

### How do fees affect my DST return?

The upfront load means part of capital is not immediately invested. Ongoing and disposition fees reduce distributions and sale proceeds. Assess a DST net of all fees.

### What is return of capital in a DST?

It is a distribution returning part of your own investment rather than income the property earned. It can inflate apparent yield while reducing invested capital, so confirm that distributions are supported by cash flow.

## Glossary

**Distribution:** The periodic payout of a DST’s net cash flow to investors.

**Return of Capital:** A distribution that returns part of your own investment rather than income earned.

**Load:** The total upfront fees and commissions in a DST offering.

**Disposition Fee:** A fee paid to the sponsor when the DST property is sold.

**Debt-Free DST:** A DST using no leverage, removing financing risk at the cost of leverage-driven return.
