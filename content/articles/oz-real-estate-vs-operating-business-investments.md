# OZ Real Estate vs. Operating Business Investments

I keep seeing Opportunity Zone discussions start with a building. That is understandable: apartments, industrial projects, and mixed-use development account for much of what investors encounter. But a qualified opportunity fund, or QOF, has another route. It can invest in an operating business in the zone, known as a QOZB.

The first-order view is that both routes seek the same Opportunity Zone benefit. The second-order view is that the underlying risks, return drivers, qualification rules, and sponsor skills can be very different. One owns and improves property. The other owns a business whose value comes from customers, products, cash flow, and execution.

This is educational information, not investment, tax, or legal advice. Opportunity Zone rules are time-sensitive and evolving, so confirm current rules with your tax advisor.

## Two kinds of QOF investments

The [Opportunity Zone](opportunity-zones-guide.html) statute allows a QOF to deploy capital in two broad ways. It can hold qualified opportunity zone business property directly—most often real estate that it develops or substantially improves within the zone. Or it can own an equity interest in a qualified opportunity zone business, an operating company that conducts substantially all of its activity in the zone. Either route can satisfy the QOF’s **90%** asset test.

The economic substance is not interchangeable. A real-estate QOF owns land and buildings, creates value through construction and lease-up, and has tangible collateral underneath it. A QOZB fund owns company equity. Its value depends on what that enterprise can sell, how it serves customers, and whether its cash flow can grow. That is closer to private equity or venture exposure located in a zone.

Before an investor studies a sponsor’s projected return, the more useful question is simple: which of those two things does this fund actually own? The answer affects the compliance framework and the risk that is being priced.

## Real estate development QOFs

Real estate development QOFs are the more common form. When a fund acquires existing property in a zone, it generally needs either original-use property—new construction qualifies inherently—or substantial improvement. The familiar practical test is roughly doubling the building’s basis, excluding land, within **30** months. That requirement helps explain why funds often pursue ground-up construction or major renovation rather than buy and hold stabilized, already-leased buildings.

Typical strategies include multifamily or apartment development, industrial and logistics facilities, self-storage, and mixed-use projects. The fund raises capital, develops the property, leases it up, and ideally holds the QOF investment for **10** years or more, when the tax-free exclusion can apply to appreciation.

The asset backing matters, but it does not erase risk. Land and buildings can provide collateral and a measure of downside value. Construction cost overruns, delays, lease-up failures, execution problems, and the ordinary real-estate market can still impair value. The substantial-improvement rule has steered many opportunity funds toward building or heavy renovation, not stabilized acquisitions.

## Operating business (QOZB) investments

The other path is a QOF equity investment in a qualified opportunity zone business. A QOZB is an operating company that conducts substantially all of its activity in the zone. It must meet property and income tests: a substantial portion of its tangible property must be qualified opportunity zone business property, and a large share of its gross income must come from the active conduct of business in the zone.

The statute also excludes certain “sin businesses”: golf courses, country clubs, massage parlors, hot tub or suntan facilities, gambling facilities, and liquor stores. The list can involve facts and nuance, particularly around ancillary activity, so a tax advisor should confirm the application to an actual business.

A QOZB can use a written-plan working-capital safe harbor of roughly **31** months to deploy capital over time. That flexibility can be useful, but it does not make the business asset-backed. The investor has enterprise exposure: a successful company may have considerable upside, while a failure can produce a greater loss of principal than a property-backed project. Products, competition, market position, and execution do the work here.

## Risk and return differences

For real estate, return can come from development value creation, rental income, and long-term appreciation. The tangible property is a support, while the development process adds construction and lease-up risk on top of market risk.

For an operating business, return comes from enterprise growth rather than property appreciation. The potential upside can be higher, but the investment has no real-property cushion and its risk is concentrated in the company’s execution, market, customers, and competitive position.

Neither path has an inherent return advantage, and neither has a promised result. Both routes can qualify for the **10**-year exclusion only if the QOF and investment meet the program’s requirements and the investment actually appreciates. A project that does not appreciate, or a business that fails, leaves little or nothing for the exclusion to shelter. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Key takeaways

- A QOF can hold developed or substantially improved real estate directly, or equity in a QOZB operating business.
- Real estate is the more common, asset-backed route; its substantial-improvement rule often means original use or roughly doubled building basis within **30** months.
- QOZB exposure is enterprise-backed, must satisfy property and income tests, cannot be an excluded sin business, and carries full operating-business risk.
- The right comparison is risk versus return, not a promise of a tax result. Verify current rules with a tax advisor.

## Which suits your goals

Investors who want tangible, asset-backed exposure may prefer real-estate development QOFs. They have more market choices and can offer returns from development, income, and appreciation. The trade is accepting construction and lease-up risk. Collateral can offer some downside cushion; it is not a guarantee against loss.

An investor who accepts venture-like risk and the possibility of a larger loss may consider QOZB exposure for its potential enterprise upside. These funds are rarer, so diligence should focus on the business itself, not merely the real estate around it. Some investors use both routes, often treating operating-business exposure as a smaller, higher-risk sleeve and diversifying across projects, sponsors, and markets where suitable.

The decision is not real estate versus a different version of real estate. It is asset-backed, development-driven risk versus enterprise-driven business risk. Match that choice to the portfolio, liquidity needs, and loss tolerance rather than to the most appealing story.

## Reading a fund’s strategy

Read the offering documents and private placement memorandum. A real-estate development QOF should identify target property types—multifamily, industrial, self-storage, or mixed-use—its development plan, and its method for meeting substantial-improvement or original-use requirements. A business-oriented fund should identify the company or companies it backs, how their QOZB property and income tests are met, and how the working-capital safe harbor applies.

Hybrid structures deserve special care. Some funds combine real estate and operating-business elements. A real-estate project may sit inside a QOZB operating subsidiary, which can blur the line while remaining a legitimate structure. The investor still needs to understand the actual mix and each part’s qualification path.

Sponsor skill is a second-order issue. Delivering a project on budget and leasing it up is a different discipline from scaling an operating company. A strong developer is not automatically equipped to run a venture-style business, and an experienced business builder may not have construction expertise. Hybrid funds need credible capability in both.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the two kinds of Opportunity Zone investments?

A QOF can directly own qualified opportunity zone business property, most commonly developed or substantially improved real estate, or it can own equity in a QOZB operating company. Both can satisfy the **90%** asset test. The real-estate route owns land and buildings and is asset-backed; the business route owns company equity and is enterprise-backed. Identify the fund’s route and verify current rules with a tax advisor before investing.

### Why are most OZ funds real estate development vehicles?

The substantial-improvement requirement makes development common. Existing property generally must be original use or substantially improved—roughly doubling building basis excluding land within **30** months. Coupled with the appreciation-focused **10**-year exclusion, this favors ground-up construction or major renovation of multifamily, industrial, self-storage, and mixed-use property over a stabilized acquisition.

### What is a qualified opportunity zone business (QOZB)?

It is an operating company that conducts substantially all its activity in the zone and can receive QOF equity. It must satisfy tangible-property and active-income tests, avoid excluded sin businesses, and may use a roughly **31**-month written-plan working-capital safe harbor. It is a business investment, not a building investment, so confirm current tests with a tax advisor.

### How do real estate and operating-business OZ investments differ in risk?

Real estate has land and buildings as collateral but faces construction, lease-up, and market risk; returns can come from development value creation, income, and appreciation. A QOZB has business, market, competition, and execution risk without real-property backing. It can offer more upside and a larger possible principal loss. Both require appreciation for the **10**-year exclusion to have value.

### Which path offers higher returns?

Neither path guarantees or inherently delivers higher returns. A successful QOZB can have venture-like upside and a large downside. A real-estate QOF has tangible support but real development risk. Choose the risk profile that fits the actual fund and investor, not a return promise.

### Can a QOF invest in both real estate and operating businesses?

Yes. A QOF can mix qualified zone property and QOZB equity if it meets the **90%** test. It may also hold real estate through a QOZB subsidiary. Read the documents to see the true mix, each component’s qualification method, and whether the sponsor has both development and operating expertise.

### What are the excluded “sin businesses” for a QOZB?

They include golf courses, country clubs, massage parlors, hot tub facilities, suntan facilities, racetracks or other gambling facilities, and stores principally selling alcoholic beverages for off-premises consumption. A fund should confirm that a backed company is not an excluded business; the application can be fact-specific.

### Does the substantial-improvement rule apply to operating businesses?

It applies most prominently to acquired tangible property, especially real estate. Existing real estate generally needs original use or roughly doubled building basis excluding land within **30** months. QOZBs focus more on active-conduct, property, income, and working-capital requirements, although real property owned by a business can still raise substantial-improvement questions.

### Which path is more common in the OZ market?

Real-estate development is more common because of the substantial-improvement rule and the appreciation-focused exclusion. Pure operating-business QOZB investments are rarer and more complex, sometimes embedded in a real-estate QOZB structure. Investors seeking enterprise exposure may have fewer choices and should diligence them carefully.

### How do I tell which kind of investment a fund makes?

Use the offering documents and PPM. Look for property type, development plan, and original-use or substantial-improvement discussion in a real-estate fund. Look for backed companies, property and income tests, and working-capital use in a QOZB fund. Ask about hybrid structures and compare the sponsor’s track record with the work it is actually undertaking.

### Is real estate OZ investing safer than operating-business investing?

Hard assets may provide more downside value than a company with no hard assets, but “safer” is relative. A development project can lose money through construction, lease-up, or market trouble. A QOZB can have higher upside but full enterprise risk and a larger potential loss. Neither is risk-free, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Do both paths qualify for the 10-year tax-free exclusion?

Both can qualify when program requirements are met. If a QOF interest is held at least **10** years and the elective basis step-up to fair market value is made at sale, the new investment’s appreciation can be tax-free whether it came from real estate or a business. The investment must appreciate, and the rules remain time-sensitive; confirm current requirements with a tax advisor.

### Should the sponsor’s expertise differ between the two paths?

Yes. Real-estate funds need evidence of construction delivery, budgeting, and lease-up. Operating-business funds need operating and growth-equity capability in the relevant industry. A hybrid needs both. A mismatch between sponsor experience and strategy is a material risk.

### Can I diversify across both kinds of OZ investments?

Yes. A blended fund or separate fund allocations can spread return drivers between property appreciation and enterprise growth and reduce concentration in a single project, sponsor, or strategy. Because business exposure is rarer and venture-like, it should be sized with its higher risk in mind and assessed for suitability.

## Glossary

- **QOF:** Qualified Opportunity Fund, the vehicle for Opportunity Zone capital.
- **QOZB:** Qualified Opportunity Zone Business, an operating company in a zone.
- **QOZ Business Property:** Tangible property, often real estate, used in a zone.
- **Real Estate Development QOF:** A QOF that builds or improves property in a zone.
- **Operating-Business Investment:** QOF equity in a zone operating company.
- **90% Asset Test:** The share of QOF assets that must be qualified.
- **Substantial Improvement:** Roughly doubling building basis within **30** months.
- **Original-Use Property:** New construction that qualifies inherently.
- **Working-Capital Safe Harbor:** A roughly **31**-month written-plan period to deploy QOZB capital.
- **Property Test:** A QOZB requirement for tangible property in the zone.
- **Income Test:** A QOZB requirement for income from active zone business.
- **Sin Business:** An excluded business such as golf, gambling, or a liquor store.
- **Asset-Backed:** Backed by tangible real property.
- **Enterprise-Backed:** Backed by the value of an operating company.
- **10-Year Exclusion:** Tax-free appreciation after a **10**-year QOF hold.
- **Form 8996:** The IRS form a QOF files to self-certify.

## OZ real estate vs. operating-business investments at a glance

| Factor | OZ real estate | Operating business (QOZB) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it funds | Development or improvement of property | An active business in the zone |
| Return driver | Property appreciation and rent | Business growth and profits |
| Risk profile | Real-estate and development risk | Operating and execution risk |
| Tangibility | Physical property | The enterprise’s prospects |
| Return potential | Steadier, real-estate-like | Potentially higher, more variable |
| Best for | Investors wanting real-estate exposure | Investors comfortable with business risk |

## Which should you choose?

Real-estate development may fit an investor seeking the tangibility and relatively steadier profile of property, with appreciation and rent as return drivers. Operating-business QOF exposure can have more upside but also the execution and operating risk of the enterprise. Read what each fund actually holds, then decide how much business risk you are willing to accept.
