# 1031 Exchange vs. Selling and Investing in Stocks

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Category:** 1031 Exchange · **Research:** Baker 1031 Research · **Updated:** June 2026 · **Reading time:** 16 min read

This week, a familiar question came up while reviewing the consequences of a taxable property sale: “Why not just sell, pay the tax, and own stocks instead?” It is a reasonable question. The answer is not an asset-class slogan. It is a comparison between the permanent cost of leaving real estate and the value of diversification, liquidity, income, and a different return profile.

An investor who sells appreciated property to buy equities faces the tax bill first. An investor who continues through a 1031 exchange keeps the full pre-tax base in real estate. Stocks can reduce a portfolio’s exposure to property-specific risks and can be sold quickly, including in pieces. Those are real benefits. This guide considers the cost of selling, tax drag against continued deferral, risk and diversification, income, and the cases where diversifying out of real estate is sensible.

## The cost of selling to buy stocks

The first item to price is the four-layer tax. A taxable sale of appreciated investment real estate can combine federal capital gains of up to 20%, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) of up to 25%, the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. Together, the bill often exceeds one-third of the gain.

That tax is not merely a timing issue. Once paid, it is capital that cannot earn another return for the investor. A large gain can leave the stock portfolio starting with roughly a third less capital than the investor would have retained in deferred real estate. The tax is, in plain terms, the price of switching asset classes.

By contrast, a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) keeps the capital that would otherwise go to tax working in replacement real estate. The relevant comparison is therefore not simply “real estate return versus stock return.” It is full pre-tax real-estate capital versus an after-tax equity base. That asymmetry belongs at the beginning of the analysis.

## Tax drag vs. continued deferral

Tax drag is the reduction in the capital available to compound after a taxable sale. Sell property, pay tax, and the stock portfolio compounds from the after-tax remainder. Continue to defer, and the real-estate investment compounds from the full pre-tax amount.

If the two paths earn similar returns, the full-base path produces more wealth over time. Compounding magnifies the initial difference. That is the mathematical appeal of continued deferral.

But returns are not identical across these two asset classes, and neither are the risks or the uses of the capital. First-order thinking says that avoiding tax is always the win. Second-order thinking asks whether the diversification, liquidity, and different return characteristics of stocks are worth the permanent reduction in capital. Tax drag is a real cost. It is not the whole decision.

> Selling to buy stocks compounds the after-tax base. Continued deferral keeps the full pre-tax base compounding in real estate.

## Risk & diversification trade-offs

The strongest argument for selling into stocks is diversification. An investor with most of their wealth in real estate remains exposed to property markets, interest rates, local economics, and illiquidity. Equities are a different asset class with different drivers. Adding them can reduce the risk of having too much riding on one area of the portfolio.

Stocks also offer liquidity that property does not. They can generally be sold quickly and in part. That flexibility can matter for emergencies, rebalancing, or simply retaining options. Direct real estate and DSTs are relatively illiquid.

Real estate is not a single homogeneous holding, however. An investor can spread exposure across property types and geographies, or use DSTs, while remaining in real estate and deferring tax. That option does not make stocks redundant; it means the investor should compare stock-specific diversification and liquidity with the tax cost of exiting and with tax-deferred diversification inside real estate.

## Income differences

Income needs often clarify the choice. Direct property and DSTs usually provide regular rent or distributions. Depreciation can shelter part of that income, creating a steadier, tax-advantaged stream.

Stocks provide dividends, which are generally lower-yielding than real-estate income, and they provide capital appreciation or proceeds when an investor sells shares. For someone who wants current, depreciation-sheltered income, income-producing real estate may fit more naturally. For someone focused on growth and total return, stocks’ dividend-plus-appreciation profile may be more appealing.

Neither profile is universally better. The question is whether the investor needs regular income now or prefers growth and the flexibility to turn shares into cash.

## When diversifying out makes sense

There are cases where paying the tax to diversify is prudent. The clearest is dangerous concentration: if nearly all net worth is in real estate, reducing that concentration can be worth the cost of sale. The same can be true when the investor needs liquidity that property cannot provide, wants to simplify, wants to retire from real estate altogether, or has a real change in strategy.

In each case, the tax is the price of exiting. Some investors should pay it.

For many others, an exchange into several or different properties, or into DSTs, can provide substantial diversification without a taxable sale. For investors committed to long-term real-estate wealth building, continued deferral and the possible step-up in basis at death can be more wealth-efficient than selling to buy stocks. It depends on the investor’s concentration, liquidity needs, objectives, and willingness to remain in real estate.

### Key Takeaways

- Selling real estate to buy stocks triggers the four-layer tax and permanently reduces the capital available to invest.
- Continued 1031 deferral avoids that tax drag and keeps the full base compounding in real estate; stocks contribute diversification and liquidity.
- Real estate can provide regular income partly sheltered by depreciation, while stocks offer dividends, growth, and total return.
- High concentration, a real liquidity need, or a genuine desire to exit may justify paying tax. In other cases, tax-deferred diversification inside real estate may do much of the job.

## Balanced approaches

This choice does not have to be all or nothing. One approach is a partial exchange or partial sale: exchange most of the real estate and defer tax on that portion, while selling a portion and paying tax to add some stock exposure. That keeps most capital in tax-deferred real estate while adding equities.

Another approach is to diversify the real-estate allocation through exchanges into different properties or DSTs and build stock holdings gradually from other income or savings. The investor gets both types of exposure without triggering a large sale tax to fund the stocks.

A third approach is simply to keep a long-term, tax-deferred real-estate allocation, including its possible step-up advantage, alongside a separate stock portfolio funded independently. A balance between the two is often more useful than treating the question as a stark sell-all-or-stay-all choice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I sell my real estate to invest in stocks?

It depends on the investor’s goals and facts. Selling triggers the four-layer tax, often more than one-third of the gain, and permanently reduces capital. Deferral retains the full base in real estate. Stocks add diversification and liquidity, but an investor can often diversify inside real estate tax-deferred, and balanced approaches can provide some of both.

### What does it cost to sell real estate to buy stocks?

The potential stack is federal capital gains of up to 20%, depreciation recapture up to 25%, the 3.8% NIIT, and state tax. It can exceed one-third of gain. Because it is paid before the stock purchase, it leaves a smaller investment base; that cost is central to the decision.

### What is tax drag in this comparison?

It is the loss of compounding capital caused by paying tax to sell. The stock portfolio compounds from the after-tax base. Continued deferral keeps the full pre-tax base in real estate, so compounding widens the difference over time before stock-specific benefits are considered.

### Do stocks offer better diversification than real estate?

Stocks add a different asset class with different drivers and offer liquidity that real estate lacks. But investors can diversify in real estate by property type, geography, and DSTs without a taxable sale. The question is how much the investor values stocks’ particular diversification and liquidity compared with those tax-deferred choices.

### How do the income profiles differ?

Real estate can provide regular rent or distributions that are often partly sheltered by depreciation. Stocks provide dividends, generally lower-yielding than real-estate income, plus capital appreciation. The former can suit income-focused investors; the latter can suit growth-focused investors seeking total return.

### When does diversifying out of real estate make sense?

It can make sense when concentration in real estate is dangerous, liquidity is needed, or the investor wants to simplify, retire from real estate, or change strategy. In those cases, diversification or liquidity may justify the tax. Otherwise, diversifying inside real estate while deferred is often the better fit.

### Can I diversify without selling my real estate?

Yes. A 1031 exchange can move into multiple or different properties, including DSTs across property types and markets, without immediate tax. An investor can also build a separate stock portfolio from other income or savings rather than selling the existing real estate to fund it.

### Is continued deferral always better than selling?

No. Pure compounding math and a potential step-up in basis favor deferral, but stocks’ diversification, liquidity, and growth can justify the tax cost for an investor who is highly concentrated, needs cash, or wants out of real estate. The answer is individual.

### What’s a balanced approach?

It is a way to capture elements of both paths: a partial sale and exchange, tax-deferred diversification in real estate while building stocks separately, or a deferred real-estate allocation alongside an independently funded stock portfolio.

### Does the step-up at death factor into this?

Yes. If deferred real estate is held until death, a step-up in basis can erase accumulated deferred gain for heirs. A taxable sale to buy stocks pays that tax now, so it gives up that particular advantage. For long-term wealth building and transfer, the deferral-plus-step-up combination can be significant.

### How do I decide between the two?

Work with a CPA to quantify the tax cost of selling. Then compare tax drag and continued deferral with the desired diversification, liquidity, income, concentration level, and commitment to real estate. Consider balanced approaches. There is no universal answer.

### Can I keep real estate and still own stocks?

Yes. Many investors keep tax-deferred real estate through exchanges while maintaining a separate stock portfolio from income or savings. That retains real estate’s deferral and possible step-up benefits while adding stock exposure without selling property to fund it.

### How do DSTs compare to stocks for passive investors?

DSTs provide passive real estate with no management, regular income that is often partly sheltered by depreciation, and possible 1031 eligibility. They are illiquid and remain tied to real estate. Stocks provide liquidity, broad diversification, and growth but do not share the same depreciation shelter or steady real-estate income profile. A passive investor is weighing those differences, not choosing a universal winner.

### Won’t stocks outperform real estate over the long run?

It depends on the period, the investments, and the method of comparison. Both asset classes have produced strong long-run returns, and neither consistently dominates. Real estate has leverage, income, depreciation, deferral, and step-up effects on after-tax results; stocks offer liquidity and broad growth. Compare after-tax, leverage-adjusted results, not a simple pre-tax return claim.

### Is real estate’s illiquidity a real disadvantage?

It can be. Real estate, including DSTs, cannot usually be sold quickly or in part, which limits rebalancing, emergency liquidity, and opportunistic moves. Illiquidity can also discourage reactive selling and support long-term compounding. The investor must decide whether the liquidity benefit of stocks is worth the tax cost of obtaining it.

## Glossary

- **Tax Drag:** The reduction in compounding capital caused by paying tax to sell real estate for stocks.
- **Continued Deferral:** Remaining in real estate through 1031 exchanges and avoiding the tax drag of sale.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax—the potential cost of selling real estate.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across asset classes, such as real estate and stocks, to reduce concentration risk.
- **Liquidity:** Ease of sale. Stocks offer it; real estate, including DSTs, is relatively illiquid.
- **Compounding:** Returns earning further returns; it benefits from the larger pre-tax base retained through deferral.
- **Concentration Risk:** Exposure created by being heavily invested in one asset class.
- **Depreciation Shelter:** The real-estate-income tax advantage from depreciation, which stock dividends do not share.
- **Dividends:** Stock income that is generally lower-yielding than real-estate income.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The reset at death that can erase deferred real-estate gain; a taxable sale for stocks gives up that benefit.
- **Balanced Approach:** Holding deferred real estate and stocks through partial diversification or separate funding.
- **Partial Exchange:** Deferring most real estate while selling some of it for stock diversification.
- **Total Return:** Income plus appreciation; often the frame for stocks, contrasted with real estate’s income focus.
- **After-Tax Base:** The reduced capital remaining after tax is paid on a sale and then invested in stocks.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A vehicle for diversifying within real estate tax-deferred as an alternative to selling for stocks.
- **Asset Class:** An investment category, such as real estate or stocks, with distinct characteristics.

## 1031 exchange vs. selling for stocks at a glance

| Factor | 1031 Exchange | Sell & Buy Stocks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax at sale | Deferred under §1031 | Capital-gains tax due now |
| Capital invested | Full pre-tax proceeds | After-tax remainder |
| Asset class | Real estate continues | Equities — broad diversification |
| Income | Rent, sheltered by depreciation | Dividends and capital gains |
| Liquidity | Illiquid; finite holds | Highly liquid, daily pricing |
| Diversification | Property-by-property; build your own | Instant via index funds |
| Best when | You want continued real-estate exposure | You want to diversify out of real estate |

## Which should you choose?

Continue a 1031 exchange when ongoing real-estate exposure and full pre-tax compounding without an immediate tax bill are the priorities. Selling into stocks can make sense when diversification and liquidity outweigh the tax drag—but compare after-tax results, rather than headline returns. A partial exchange can diversify some equity while deferring the rest.
