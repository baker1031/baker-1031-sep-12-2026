# DSTs and the 1031 Same-Taxpayer Rule

I keep seeing exchange work get reduced to a property search and a deadline calendar. Those matter. But one small question can decide whether the exchange survives at all: who, exactly, is the taxpayer?

Baker 1031 Research updated this educational guide in June 2026. The 1031 same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer that sold the relinquished property to acquire the replacement. For a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST), the beneficial interest must be titled to match the taxpayer—and taxpayer identification number—that sold the old property. The name on paperwork may not tell the whole story. Tax treatment does. A single-member LLC or revocable living trust can be disregarded for tax purposes and treated as the same taxpayer as its owner. Spouses and community-property rules can add another layer.

First-order thinking is “make the subscription paperwork look tidy.” Second-order thinking is “will the IRS view the buyer as the same taxpayer that realized the gain, and has the attorney, CPA, and qualified intermediary confirmed it before the signature?” A real mismatch can disqualify the exchange and trigger the tax that was meant to be deferred. ## The same-taxpayer requirement

The rule is foundational to a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html): the same taxpayer that sold the relinquished property must take title to the replacement property. The person or entity that realized the gain is the one that must reinvest the proceeds and hold the replacement if that gain is to be deferred. You cannot sell as one taxpayer and have a different taxpayer acquire the replacement. In practical terms, the taxpayer identification number on the sale must match the taxpayer identification number on the acquisition.

With a DST, that requirement governs how the beneficial interest is titled. If you sold the relinquished property as an individual, the DST interest generally needs to be held by you as that individual. If an entity sold, the interest generally needs to be held by that entity or a structure that is treated as the same taxpayer. Titling is not a clerical detail. A different buyer can disqualify the exchange and trigger the deferred tax.

## Holding title through entities

Owners frequently hold real estate through LLCs, partnerships, trusts, or corporations. The question is not merely which name appears on title; it is whether the entity is the taxpayer or whether tax law disregards it and treats its owner as the taxpayer.

If a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership held and sold the relinquished property, the partnership is the taxpayer. The partnership generally needs to acquire the DST interest. This can be awkward when members want different outcomes—some want cash, others want to exchange, or each wants a different replacement. A partnership cannot simply divide into individual exchanges at closing.

A “drop and swap” is one technique sometimes used to address that issue: before the sale, partnership property is distributed to members as tenants-in-common so that each person may be an individual taxpayer able to exchange separately. It is technical, has its own tax risks, and must be planned well in advance with counsel. It should never be improvised at closing.

Before you title a DST interest, the central question is not whose name is on the paperwork. It is who the IRS considers the taxpayer, because that is who must acquire the replacement.

## Disregarded entities and trusts

A disregarded entity exists legally but is ignored for federal income-tax purposes. Its income and activity are reported directly by its owner. For a 1031 exchange, it is treated as the same taxpayer as that owner.

Common examples are a single-member LLC that has not elected corporate taxation and a revocable living trust, which is generally a grantor trust. Because tax law looks through those structures to the owner, holding a DST interest through one can preserve taxpayer continuity.

That can be useful in practice. If you sold a property in your individual name, you can generally acquire the DST through your single-member LLC or revocable living trust without breaking the rule, because each is treated as you. If a disregarded single-member LLC sold the property, you can often acquire the DST individually or through another disregarded entity you own. The structure must actually be disregarded. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership is not; it is its own taxpayer.

## Spousal and community-property issues

Married couples need to match the DST title to how they sold the relinquished property. If the couple sold jointly, they generally need to acquire the DST interest jointly so the same taxpayers acquire the replacement. If only one spouse was on the original title, that spouse generally needs to hold the DST interest unless a recognized same-taxpayer structure applies.

Community-property states, including California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Washington, and several others, can introduce state-specific considerations. Property acquired during marriage is generally owned by the marital community, and spouses may use community-property title, sometimes with rights of survivorship. Community-property treatment may interact favorably with a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) when one spouse dies: in some circumstances, both halves can receive a full step-up in basis rather than only the deceased spouse’s half. That can matter for a 1031-deferred DST interest with a low carryover basis.

The same-taxpayer rule still requires consistency between how the property was sold and how the DST is acquired. State law, survivorship, and estate-planning goals make this a question for an attorney and CPA familiar with the couple’s circumstances, not a one-size-fits-all answer.

### Key takeaways

- The same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer that sold the relinquished property to acquire the DST replacement.
- For entity-owned property, identify the actual taxpayer. A partnership that sold generally must acquire; a disregarded entity follows its owner.
- A single-member LLC and a revocable living trust are generally treated as the same taxpayer as their owner, which can preserve the exchange.
- Married couples and community-property states add nuance. Match the DST to how the property was sold and confirm the structure with the attorney and CPA.

## Titling the DST correctly

The subscription agreement is where this becomes concrete. The owner of record of the DST beneficial interest must be the same taxpayer, by taxpayer identification number, that sold the relinquished property. That may be you individually, a single-member LLC, a revocable living trust, or another valid same-taxpayer structure.

Confirm the original property’s title and the taxpayer before you sign. Your [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) knows how the relinquished property was held and how the exchange is documented. Your attorney advises on legal title and entity structure. Your CPA confirms tax treatment and taxpayer identification. Those three should be aligned before the sponsor’s subscription process asks how the interest will be held.

Correcting a title after subscription can be difficult or impossible without putting the exchange at risk. This is the practical culmination of the rule: the owner of record in the subscription agreement must match the taxpayer that sold the old property.

Titling is the step where a small error can undo an entire exchange. Confirm the taxpayer and match it before you sign.

## Common titling mistakes to avoid

The recurring mistake is a capacity mismatch. An owner sells individually, then subscribes through a newly formed LLC that is not disregarded, or jointly with a spouse who was not on the original title, without a recognized same-taxpayer basis. Either change can create a different buyer.

Another mistake is assuming every LLC works. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership is its own taxpayer; putting a DST interest in a new partnership does not satisfy the rule the way a disregarded single-member LLC may.

Other risks include forming an entity too late or improperly, allowing the QI, attorney, and CPA each to assume someone else confirmed the title, overlooking spousal or community-property details, and treating a partnership drop and swap as a last-minute fix. The common thread is acting before confirming the actual taxpayer. Verify the taxpayer first, plan the DST title, and align the advisors before subscribing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the same-taxpayer rule in a 1031 exchange?

The same-taxpayer rule requires the taxpayer that sold the relinquished property to acquire the replacement property. The taxpayer that realized the gain must reinvest and hold the replacement, and the taxpayer identification number on the sale must match the acquisition. For a DST, the beneficial interest must be held by the same taxpayer—individually, through a valid single-member LLC, a revocable living trust, or another structure treated as the same taxpayer. A wrong title can disqualify the exchange; confirm it with your QI and attorney.

### Can I hold my DST interest through an LLC?

It depends on the LLC’s tax status. A single-member LLC that has not elected corporate taxation is generally a disregarded entity, so it is treated as the same taxpayer as its owner. It can generally hold the DST if that owner sold the relinquished property. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership is a separate taxpayer; it generally must be both seller and acquirer. Confirm the classification and title with the attorney and CPA before subscribing.

### What is a disregarded entity?

It is an entity that exists legally but is ignored for federal income-tax purposes, with income and activity reported by the owner. A single-member LLC that has not elected corporate treatment and a revocable living trust are common examples. The tax law looks through them to the owner, so a person may generally sell individually and acquire through a single-member LLC, or do the reverse, without breaking taxpayer continuity. This flexibility applies only to a genuine disregarded structure; a multi-member partnership LLC is not disregarded. Confirm status with your CPA.

### Can a revocable living trust hold a DST interest in a 1031 exchange?

Generally, yes. A revocable living trust is generally a grantor trust, so its grantor is treated as the owner of its assets for federal income-tax purposes. If you sold individually or through that revocable trust, the trust can generally hold the DST without violating the rule. Revocable trusts are often used for estate planning, probate avoidance, and asset management. Irrevocable trusts can be separate taxpayers, so the trust type and terms should be confirmed with the attorney and CPA.

### How do married couples title a DST interest in a 1031 exchange?

They generally match the title to how they sold. A couple that sold jointly generally acquires jointly. If only one spouse was on the relinquished-property title, that spouse generally holds the DST unless a recognized same-taxpayer structure applies. California, Texas, and Arizona are among community-property states where marital property, survivorship, and the step-up in basis can add nuance. The state and estate-plan details matter, so the attorney and CPA should confirm the title before subscription.

### What are community-property issues in a DST 1031 exchange?

In community-property states, property acquired during marriage is generally owned equally by the marital community. California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Washington, and several other states follow this approach. A couple may hold property as community property, sometimes with rights of survivorship. When one spouse dies, community property can receive a full step-up in basis on both halves, not only the deceased spouse’s half, which may be valuable for a low-basis, 1031-deferred DST interest. The DST still must be titled consistently with the property that was sold. Use advisors familiar with the state’s rules.

### What happens if I title my DST interest incorrectly?

If a genuinely different taxpayer buys the DST without a recognized same-taxpayer basis, the 1031 exchange can be disqualified. That can trigger the capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture that were meant to be deferred. This is substantive, not a technicality. Because the subscription and exchange documents have already been completed, unwinding or correcting the error afterward can be difficult or impossible and can create further problems. Confirm the seller’s title and taxpayer in advance, then match the DST title with your QI, attorney, and CPA.

### Can a partnership do a 1031 exchange into a DST?

Yes. The partnership itself is the taxpayer, so it must be both the seller of the relinquished property and acquirer of the DST interest. This is straightforward when all partners want to exchange into the same replacement. If some partners want cash or different replacements, the partnership cannot simply split at closing. A drop and swap—distribution of property to partners as tenants-in-common before sale—can permit individual exchanges, but it carries tax risks and requires advance counsel, CPA, and QI planning.

### Where do I specify how I’ll hold title to my DST interest?

The subscription agreement, the purchase contract for the DST, records the owner of the beneficial interest. The sponsor asks how title will be held, and the selection must match the sale taxpayer by taxpayer identification number. It may be individual title, a single-member LLC, a revocable living trust, or another true same-taxpayer structure. Confirm it with the QI, attorney, and CPA before you sign, because correcting it afterward can jeopardize the exchange.

### Does the same-taxpayer rule apply to all 1031 exchanges?

Yes. It applies to DST interests and directly owned replacement property alike. The taxpayer that sells and realizes the gain must acquire and hold the replacement; there is no general exception allowing one taxpayer to sell and another to buy. Entity, trust, spousal, and community-property facts affect how the rule is applied, but taxpayer continuity is universal. Confirm the fact-specific result with your QI, attorney, and CPA.

### Can I add a co-owner to my DST interest who was not on the original property?

Generally, no. Adding a child, business partner, or spouse who was not a taxpayer on the relinquished property can change who acquires the replacement and violate continuity. There are narrow, recognized same-taxpayer situations, including some community-property and disregarded-entity structures, but a genuinely different taxpayer is generally problematic. A change may need to be addressed after the exchange with its own tax considerations, or planned before the exchange with specific advice. Do not add a co-owner at subscription without attorney, CPA, and QI confirmation.

### How do I make sure my DST titling is correct?

Before signing the subscription agreement, confirm exactly how the relinquished property was titled and who the taxpayer was. Then title the DST consistently. The QI confirms exchange documentation, the attorney advises on title and entities, and the CPA confirms taxpayer identification and tax treatment. All three should agree before subscription. Use the confirmed individual, single-member LLC, revocable living trust, or other qualifying structure; corrections after subscription may be hard or impossible.

### What are the most common DST titling mistakes?

The classic mistake is selling in one capacity and acquiring in another—individually on sale, then through a new non-disregarded LLC or with a spouse who was not on title. Others are assuming every LLC qualifies, forming an entity too late or incorrectly, poor coordination between the QI, attorney, and CPA, and overlooking spousal or community-property rules. Partnership drop-and-swap situations are especially error-prone because they need advance planning. Confirm the taxpayer first; it is the best protection against a costly title error.

### Is the same-taxpayer rule a tax or legal question?

It is both. The CPA evaluates taxpayer identity, entity classification, and taxpayer identification treatment. The attorney handles ownership structure, LLC or trust title, spousal and community-property rules, and documentation. The QI ensures the exchange documents reflect the same taxpayer. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice. A disqualified exchange and resulting tax can be serious, so these technical questions deserve professional attention before a DST subscription.

## Glossary

- **Same-Taxpayer Rule:** The 1031 requirement that the seller of the old property acquire the replacement.
- **Taxpayer Identification Number:** The number that must match between the sale and the acquisition.
- **Relinquished Property:** The property you sell in a 1031 exchange.
- **Replacement Property:** The property, such as a DST, you acquire in a 1031 exchange.
- **Disregarded Entity:** An entity ignored for tax and treated as the same taxpayer as its owner.
- **Single-Member LLC:** An LLC with one owner, generally disregarded for tax purposes.
- **Revocable Living Trust:** A grantor trust treated as the same taxpayer as its grantor.
- **Grantor Trust:** A trust whose assets are treated as owned by the grantor for tax.
- **Partnership:** An entity that is its own taxpayer for 1031 purposes.
- **Drop and Swap:** Distributing partnership property so partners can exchange individually.
- **Community Property:** Marital property owned equally by spouses in certain states.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Reset of basis to market value at death, erasing deferred gain.
- **Title:** The legal ownership of the DST beneficial interest.
- **Subscription Agreement:** The contract where you specify how you hold your DST interest.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds and documents the 1031 exchange.
- **Beneficial Interest:** An undivided fractional interest in the DST, treated as like-kind real property.
