# DSTs for Dividing Property in a Divorce

I have been thinking about the point at which a real estate holding stops being only an investment decision and becomes a problem of fair division. A building can be valuable, income-producing, and still be hard to divide. You cannot hand one former spouse the north half of a fourplex and the other the south half of a small commercial building.

First-order thinking is to sell the property, divide the cash, and move on. The second-order question is what a sale does to accumulated capital-gains tax, depreciation, ongoing income, timing, and the ability of each person to choose an independent path. A buyout can solve some of that but demands cash or financing. Continued co-ownership can leave two people who are separating responsible for tenants, repairs, and financing decisions together.

A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) can offer a structural alternative. A properly planned 1031 exchange can convert jointly owned investment real estate into fractional beneficial interests that can be divided cleanly. Each spouse may hold a defined share of the same DSTs or, if the structure permits, separate DSTs. This is educational information, not tax or legal advice. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice; confirm current rules and your facts with family-law counsel and a CPA.

## The Property-Division Challenge

The central difficulty is that a building is a single, undivided asset. When two spouses own a rental fourplex, apartment building, retail center, or small commercial property together, there is no physical way to give each person half while preserving the property as a functioning asset.

The ordinary alternatives all have trade-offs. One spouse can buy out the other, but that requires available cash or financing and leaves one party holding and managing the whole property. The parties can sell the property and divide proceeds, but a sale will generally trigger capital-gains tax accumulated through appreciation and depreciation. That can shrink the amount available to divide and end an income stream on which either spouse may have relied. Continuing to co-own after divorce is also often unattractive, since it requires ongoing joint decisions about tenants, repairs, and financing.

The problem is not merely emotional. It is structural: an indivisible asset is being divided between two people whose objectives, liquidity needs, and willingness to manage property may now differ.

## How Fractional Interests Help

A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) holds income-producing real estate in which investors own undivided fractional beneficial interests. Those interests are measured by percentage or dollar amount, not by a physical portion of a building. That makes them divisible in a way a directly owned building is not.

In a possible structure, the couple exchanges the jointly owned property into one or more DSTs under Section 1031. The resulting interests can then be allocated so each spouse has an identified share. The spouses may each hold fractional interests in the same DSTs, or each may direct a portion into separate DSTs owned individually. Counsel and a CPA must determine the appropriate structure and sequence.

The result can be two defined interests in passive, income-producing real estate rather than one shared stake in an indivisible building. The underlying purpose is straightforward: turn economic value that cannot be physically divided into interests that can be divided.

> You cannot split a building in half, but fractional DST interests can divide its economic value. That is why a 1031 into DSTs can help unlock an otherwise stuck property division.

## 1031 and Same-Taxpayer Issues

Two rules need to work together: the same-taxpayer rule under Section 1031 and the spousal-transfer rule under Section 1041.

For a 1031 exchange, the taxpayer who sells the relinquished property generally must be the taxpayer who acquires the replacement property. That continuity is what carries the tax deferral through the exchange. In a divorce, a jointly owned property has parties who are separating, so the sequence of ownership, sale, transfer, and replacement acquisition matters. A mismatch can jeopardize deferral.

Section 1041 generally treats transfers of property between spouses—or between former spouses incident to divorce—as tax-free. No gain or loss is recognized on the transfer itself, and the recipient takes the transferor's carried-over basis. This can allow a couple to divide an interest without recognizing tax merely on the division.

Section 1041 governs the transfer between spouses; Section 1031 governs deferral when property is exchanged for like-kind replacement real estate. Coordinating them can permit division and exchange while preserving deferred gain, but the exact sequence and taxpayer treatment are technical. Family-law counsel and a CPA should design them rather than improvising after a sale is underway.

## Coordinating With Counsel

This kind of division is a coordinated, multi-professional process. Family-law counsel handles the divorce settlement: the marital estate, each spouse's entitlement, and the language in the decree or marital settlement agreement. That language matters. It can set up the intended exchange and allocation, or it can create a conflict with the exchange requirements.

A CPA or tax advisor confirms how Sections 1041 and 1031 apply to the specific facts, models deferred gain and carried-over basis, and evaluates whether a proposed path preserves deferral. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) holds proceeds and manages the 1031 mechanics so the seller does not receive the funds directly. The DST sponsor and broker-dealer manage offering access, suitability, and subscription.

Each professional has a distinct role. A poorly drafted settlement provision, a missed exchange timeline, or a same-taxpayer error can undo the desired tax treatment or division. Baker 1031 handles the DST side in coordination with the legal and tax professionals; the attorney and CPA lead the legal and tax decisions.

> A clean property division is not a do-it-yourself project. Settlement language, exchange timing, and same-taxpayer treatment have to line up, which is why counsel and a CPA should lead the structure.

## Key Takeaways

- A jointly owned building is indivisible. A buyout, a taxable sale, and continued co-ownership all have meaningful drawbacks.
- A 1031 exchange into DSTs can convert the property into fractional interests that divide cleanly, allowing each spouse to own a passive, income-producing share.
- Section 1041 generally makes qualifying spouse-to-spouse transfers tax-free, and coordination with the Section 1031 same-taxpayer rule can preserve deferral.
- The process requires family-law counsel, a CPA, a qualified intermediary, and the broker-dealer. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

## Planning the Split

Timing matters because the divorce and the exchange run on different clocks. In a 1031 exchange, replacement property must be identified within **45 days** after sale of the relinquished property and acquired within **180 days**. Those dates do not pause while divorce negotiations continue.

The questions should be answered before the sale where possible. Who is the taxpayer when the property is sold? Does each party's proposed path satisfy the same-taxpayer rule? Will an interest be divided before or after the exchange, and how does Section 1041 apply? Will each spouse hold the same DSTs or separate DSTs? Does each spouse actually want deferral, or does one prefer cash and the tax consequence that comes with it?

DSTs can be useful operationally because their lower minimums allow a precise allocation of each person's share, and a DST can often close in days rather than the weeks often needed for direct-property due diligence, financing, and closing. Those features do not change the deadlines. The settlement, the relinquished-property sale or transfer, and DST subscriptions still need to be sequenced carefully. For more detail, see the [45-day identification rules](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

The most common failure is allowing the **45-day** identification and **180-day** closing periods to run out while divorce negotiations drag on. Once the relinquished property sells, those clocks begin. If a contentious settlement remains unresolved, the parties may lose the exchange and face a taxable sale instead of the deferred division they intended.

Another risk is a same-taxpayer mismatch: the person treated as selling a share is not the person treated as acquiring the corresponding replacement interest. Settlement language can also create problems when it is drafted without coordinating with the tax and exchange team. And a spouse should not assume that taking cash from a property sale is tax-free; a cash sale generally triggers gain attributable to that spouse's share.

Suitability must be identified early. DST interests are illiquid securities offered to [accredited investors](accredited-investor-requirements.html) after a suitability review. A spouse who needs liquidity soon or does not qualify as accredited may not be a fit for a DST. That may call for cash or another replacement approach, not an effort to force the structure to fit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is dividing a jointly owned property in a divorce so difficult?

A building is a single, undivided whole. It cannot be split evenly like cash. A buyout requires cash or financing and leaves one person holding the entire property. A sale generally triggers capital-gains tax built up through appreciation and depreciation, reduces the amount to divide, and ends rental income. Continued co-ownership keeps two separating people making property decisions together. A DST exchange can offer an alternative by converting the property into fractional interests that can be divided.

### How does a DST help divide property in a divorce?

A DST holds income-producing real estate and issues undivided fractional beneficial interests measured by percentage or dollar amount rather than a physical piece of a building. In a potential 1031 structure, the jointly owned property is exchanged into one or more DSTs, and the interests are allocated so each spouse holds a defined share. The spouses may hold shares in the same DSTs or separate DSTs owned individually. That converts the indivisible into divisible interests in passive, income-producing real estate. Attorneys and CPAs should coordinate the structure.

### What is the same-taxpayer rule and why does it matter in a divorce?

The Section 1031 same-taxpayer rule generally requires that the taxpayer who sells the relinquished property also acquire the replacement property. In a divorce, the structure must make sure each person treated as selling a share is the person acquiring the corresponding DST interest. Otherwise, deferral can be lost. This interacts with Section 1041, which generally permits tax-free spouse-to-spouse transfers incident to divorce with carried-over basis. A CPA and attorney should determine who transfers and exchanges, and when. Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

### Are transfers between spouses in a divorce taxable?

Generally, Section 1041 treats transfers between spouses or former spouses incident to divorce as tax-free. No gain or loss is recognized on the transfer, and the recipient takes the transferor's carried-over basis rather than a stepped-up basis. Section 1041 applies to the transfer between spouses; Section 1031 applies when a share is exchanged for replacement real estate such as a DST. The rules can work together, but their facts and timing should be confirmed with a CPA and attorney.

### Can each spouse exchange into separate DSTs?

Yes, depending on the structure. Each spouse may direct a portion of the exchange into separate DSTs owned individually, avoiding ongoing co-investment. Alternatively, the spouses may each hold fractional interests in the same DSTs, which may be simpler in some structures but keeps them as co-investors. Lower minimums and multiple available DSTs can make it practical to size separate interests precisely. Counsel and a CPA must ensure that the sequence preserves same-taxpayer treatment and deferral.

### What deadlines apply to a 1031 exchange in a divorce?

The same strict deadlines apply: identify replacement property within **45 days** of selling the relinquished property and close within **180 days**. Divorce negotiations do not stop those clocks. DSTs can often close in days rather than the weeks needed for a direct purchase, and their lower minimums can help size allocations, but the deadlines remain firm. Engage the qualified intermediary, CPA, and attorney early. The **3-property**, **200%**, or **95%** identification rules also apply.

### What happens if one spouse wants cash instead of a DST?

The structure may accommodate different choices. One spouse may use a 1031 exchange into a DST to defer gain and retain income-producing real estate while the other takes cash for a home purchase or liquidity. The spouse taking cash should understand that a cash sale, rather than a properly structured exchange, generally triggers tax on that spouse's share of deferred gain. A CPA should model the after-tax outcomes, and counsel should coordinate sequence and same-taxpayer treatment.

### Who needs to be involved in a DST-based divorce property division?

Family-law counsel documents the decree or marital settlement agreement. A CPA or tax advisor analyzes Sections 1041 and 1031, deferred gain, and carried-over basis. A qualified intermediary (QI) holds proceeds and facilitates the exchange. The DST sponsor and broker-dealer handle suitability and subscription. A drafting mistake, a missed deadline, or a same-taxpayer error can defeat the intended result. Baker 1031 handles the DST side and coordinates with the attorney and CPA, who lead legal and tax decisions.

### Is the income from a DST guaranteed for each spouse?

No. DST distributions are projections, not guarantees. Sponsors may project a target distribution, but actual income depends on occupancy, rents, expenses, and market conditions and may be reduced. The income is passive, but it is not a fixed amount like alimony. Diversifying across sponsors, property sectors, and markets can help reduce the effect of one underperforming property, but it cannot guarantee income. Interests are also illiquid, generally for a five-to-seven-year hold. Each spouse should size and diversify an allocation with advisors.

### Do both spouses need to be accredited investors?

Generally, yes. DST interests are Regulation D securities offered to accredited investors, and each spouse who will hold an interest generally must meet income or net-worth thresholds and complete a broker-dealer suitability review. A spouse's financial position after the settlement may change the result. If a spouse will not be accredited or needs liquidity, cash or another replacement strategy may be more appropriate. Assess those issues early, rather than at the end of the exchange window.

### Can we still do this if the property has a mortgage?

Yes. A mortgaged property can be exchanged into DSTs. For full deferral, an investor generally replaces relinquished-property debt or contributes additional cash to make up the difference. Many DSTs include non-recourse financing, which can provide replacement debt without either former spouse personally qualifying for or signing a new loan. The financing is non-recourse to the investors. A CPA and the sponsor or broker-dealer should model the amount of required replacement debt and appropriate DSTs, because debt replacement affects deferral.

### What are the main risks of using DSTs in a divorce settlement?

There are transaction risks and investment risks. Transaction risks include missed **45-day** and **180-day** deadlines, a same-taxpayer mismatch, and settlement drafting inconsistent with the exchange. Investment risks include illiquidity, fees, non-guaranteed distributions that may be reduced or suspended, possible loss of real-estate value, concentration, and sponsor risk. DSTs are accredited-only securities, so a person who needs liquidity or cannot qualify may not be a fit. Coordination and diversification can mitigate risks; they do not eliminate them. Discuss the structure with an attorney, CPA, and broker-dealer before proceeding.

### Is a DST division better than just selling the property?

It depends on the goals of each spouse. A straight sale to divide cash may trigger accumulated capital-gains tax and end rental income. A 1031 exchange into DSTs can defer that tax, preserve more value for division, turn an indivisible building into divisible interests, and provide passive income. If held until death, a later step-up in basis may benefit heirs by resetting basis to fair market value. The trade-off is illiquidity and the accredited-investor requirement. A spouse needing cash now may be better served by a sale, even with tax. A hybrid outcome—one spouse deferring into a DST and the other taking cash—is often possible. A CPA should compare after-tax results.

### How long does the DST side of a divorce property division take?

The DST subscription and closing can often move in a matter of days because the property is already acquired and the trust structure is in place. A direct acquisition can take weeks of due diligence, financing, and closing. The longer part is often the divorce: negotiations, settlement documentation, and professional coordination. Start DST and exchange planning before the relinquished property sells so that **45-day** and **180-day** clocks do not create a last-minute problem.

## Glossary

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding income-producing real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.

**Fractional Beneficial Interest:** An undivided share of a DST's real estate, measured by percentage or dollar amount.

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Same-Taxpayer Rule:** The 1031 requirement that the seller and buyer of replacement property be the same taxpayer.

**Section 1041:** The rule making transfers between spouses incident to divorce tax-free.

**Carried-Over Basis:** The transferor's basis that follows property in a tax-free spousal transfer.

**Marital Settlement Agreement:** The document dividing the marital estate, including property.

**Relinquished Property:** The jointly owned property sold to start a 1031 exchange.

**Replacement Property:** The like-kind real estate, such as a DST, acquired in the exchange.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds proceeds and facilitates a 1031 exchange.

**45-Day Identification Period:** The window to identify replacement property after a sale.

**180-Day Closing Period:** The window to close on replacement property in an exchange.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** DST financing not personally guaranteed by the investors.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.

**Suitability Review:** The broker-dealer's assessment of whether a DST fits the investor.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The basis reset to fair market value at death that can erase deferred gain.
