# The CPA's 2026 Guide to Opportunity Zone Funds

For Tax Advisors · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 21 min read

What a CPA needs to advise on Qualified Opportunity Funds — the deferral and exclusion mechanics, the forms, the timing traps, and the OBBBA changes reshaping the program.

I keep seeing the Opportunity Zone conversation narrow to a tax benefit before anyone asks how a client will fund the inclusion event or hold an illiquid position for a decade. The election lives on the return, but the investment decision lives much longer than that.

First-order thinking is that a QOF simply postpones a gain and makes future appreciation tax-free. Second-order thinking asks whether the gain qualifies, when the 180-day clock starts, whether the fund can meet its tests, how state rules apply, and whether the real estate works even after the tax benefit is stripped away.

<p class="lead">Opportunity Zones sit squarely in the CPA's lane: the benefits are claimed by election on the return, the timing rules are unforgiving, and the program was materially rewritten by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act. This guide is a practitioner's reference for §1400Z-2 — how deferral and the ten-year exclusion work, the forms and basis mechanics you'll handle, the 180-day and §1231 timing rules clients get wrong, and how OBBBA reshapes the program for 2027 and beyond. It pairs with our client-facing <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">Opportunity Zones guide</a>.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways for Advisors</div>
<ul>
<li>Under §1400Z-2, a client may elect to defer eligible capital gain invested in a QOF within 180 days; the deferred gain is recognized at the earlier of disposition or the program's inclusion date (12/31/2026 for the original regime).</li>
<li>Hold ten years and the client may elect a basis step-up to FMV under §1400Z-2(c), eliminating tax on the QOF's appreciation; the original gain is still recognized at inclusion.</li>
<li>The investor files Form 8949 (deferral election) and Form 8997 (annual); the fund self-certifies on Form 8996 and meets the 90% asset test.</li>
<li>OBBBA (2025) made the program permanent with a new 2027 map, rolling five-year deferral for post-2026 investments, and enhanced rural benefits; watch state nonconformity.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 id="works">How the OZ incentive works (advisor's refresher)</h2>
<p>Section 1400Z-2 lets a taxpayer defer eligible capital gain by investing an equal amount in a <strong>Qualified Opportunity Fund (QOF)</strong> within <strong>180 days</strong> of the gain. The fund deploys capital into Opportunity Zone property, typically through a <a href="qof-vs-qozb.html">two-tier QOF/QOZB structure</a>. Three benefits flow from a timely election: deferral of the original gain, a historical basis step-up (now largely expired for new investments under the original regime), and — the marquee benefit — <strong>exclusion of post-investment appreciation</strong> after a ten-year hold. Your role is to confirm the gain is eligible, the 180-day clock, and the election mechanics on the return.</p>
<h2 id="defer">Deferral and the inclusion event</h2>
<p>The deferred gain is not forgiven; it is <strong>recognized at the earlier of</strong> the date the QOF interest is sold/exchanged or the statutory <strong>inclusion date</strong> — <strong>December 31, 2026</strong> under the original regime, reported on the 2026 return filed in 2027. Plan client liquidity for that recognition, since the QOF itself is illiquid. If the QOF investment's value has declined, the amount included may be limited to the lesser of the deferred gain or the FMV at inclusion. Under <strong>OBBBA</strong>, investments made in 2027 and later use a <strong>rolling five-year deferral</strong> rather than the fixed 2026 date — a key change to track for newer clients.</p>
<h2 id="tenyear">The ten-year exclusion election</h2>
<p>The most valuable benefit is the §1400Z-2(c) election: if the client holds the QOF interest for at least <strong>ten years</strong>, they may elect to step up basis in the QOF investment to <strong>fair market value</strong> on the date of sale, eliminating tax on the QOF's appreciation. Mechanically, the deferred-gain investment starts with a <strong>$0 basis</strong>, which is increased by the recognized deferred gain at inclusion; the ten-year election then resets basis to FMV at disposition so the post-investment appreciation escapes tax. Note the distinction clients blur: the original deferred gain is still taxed at inclusion; only the <em>new</em> appreciation is excluded. Detailed in our <a href="opportunity-zone-10-year-rule.html">10-year-rule memo</a>.</p>
<h2 id="forms">Forms and reporting</h2>
<p>Three forms structure OZ reporting. The investor elects deferral on <strong>Form 8949</strong> in the year of the gain (reporting the gain, then an offsetting deferral entry with the proper code), and files <strong>Form 8997</strong> (Initial and Annual Statement of QOF Investments) every year the interest is held, reporting holdings and any dispositions. The <strong>fund</strong> files <strong>Form 8996</strong> to self-certify and report the <strong>90% asset test</strong>. At the ten-year exit, a second Form 8949 entry effects the FMV step-up election. Maintain a multi-year workpaper tracking the deferred amount, basis, and inclusion; OZ is a long engagement, not a single-year event.</p>
<h2 id="timing">180-day and §1231 timing rules</h2>
<p>Timing is where eligible gains are lost. The general rule is <strong>180 days</strong> from the date the gain would be recognized. Special rules matter: for gains from a <strong>pass-through entity</strong>, the partner/shareholder may use the entity's year-end (or the date of the entity's sale, or the return due date) as the start of the 180 days — useful flexibility. For <strong>§1231 gains</strong>, the final regulations start the 180-day period on the <strong>date of the sale</strong> (earlier guidance had used year-end netting). Only <strong>capital gain</strong> is eligible, so ordinary income and depreciation recapture generally are not. Confirm the character and the correct 180-day start before assuming a gain can be rolled in.</p>
<h2 id="qof">QOF and QOZB compliance tests</h2>
<p>While fund-level compliance is the sponsor's job, you should understand it to vet an offering. A <strong>QOF</strong> must hold ≥90% of assets in qualified OZ property (the 90% asset test, measured semiannually, reported on Form 8996), with penalties for shortfalls. Most funds invest through a <strong>QOZB</strong>, which must meet the 70% tangible-property test, the 50%-of-gross-income active-conduct test, and the &lt;5% nonqualified-financial-property limit, and which can use the <strong>31-month working-capital safe harbor</strong> to deploy development capital. The <a href="qof-vs-qozb.html">two-tier structure</a> is standard; failures at either level jeopardize the client's benefits, so confirm the sponsor monitors them.</p>
<h2 id="obbba">OBBBA 2.0: what changed</h2>
<p>The 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act made OZ <strong>permanent</strong> and revised it. Key advisor-relevant changes: a new, <strong>tighter zone map effective January 1, 2027</strong> (governors nominate beginning mid-2026); a <strong>rolling five-year deferral</strong> for investments made in 2027 and later; and enhanced <strong>rural QOF</strong> benefits — a 30% basis step-up (vs. 10%) and a halved substantial-improvement test. For clients deciding between investing now (original regime, 2026 inclusion) and waiting for 2027 (new map, rolling deferral), the analysis turns on gain timing and the transition rules, which Treasury continues to clarify. Track guidance closely; our <a href="opportunity-zones-2-0.html">OZ 2.0 memo</a> summarizes the changes.</p>
<h2 id="state">State conformity and other considerations</h2>
<p>Not all states conform to §1400Z-2. Some <strong>decoupled</strong> entirely (so the deferral/exclusion isn't recognized for state purposes), and others conform partially or with their own zone maps — meaning a client could owe state tax on a gain that's federally deferred, or fail to get the state exclusion at year ten. Check the client's state of residence and the project's state. Also weigh the interaction with other strategies: a client might prefer a <a href="opportunity-zones-vs-1031.html">1031</a> for a real-estate gain (indefinite deferral, real estate only) versus an OZ fund for a non-real-estate gain or for the ten-year exclusion.</p>
<h2 id="checklist">Client due-diligence checklist</h2>
<ul class="bul">
<li><strong>Confirm eligible capital gain</strong> and the correct 180-day start (note pass-through and §1231 rules).</li>
<li><strong>Make the Form 8949 deferral election</strong> in the year of the gain; calendar Form 8997 annually.</li>
<li><strong>Reserve liquidity</strong> for the 2026 (or rolling) inclusion-event tax.</li>
<li><strong>Vet QOF/QOZB compliance</strong> and the sponsor; the underlying real estate is often development-stage.</li>
<li><strong>Model the ten-year FMV election</strong> and the hold requirement.</li>
<li><strong>Check state conformity</strong> in residence and project states.</li>
<li><strong>Evaluate OZ 1.0 vs 2.0 timing</strong> for clients investing near the 2027 transition.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="clients">What clients should know</h2>
<p>Clients should understand the structure delivers <strong>deferral now and potential elimination of growth later</strong>, but only with a genuine ten-year commitment to an illiquid, often development-stage investment; that the original gain comes due at the inclusion event regardless; and that the program's rules are shifting under OBBBA. Emphasize underwriting the real estate independent of the tax benefit — a poor project with great tax treatment is still a poor project. For non-real-estate gains (a business or stock sale), the OZ fund is frequently the only deferral tool available, which is often the deciding factor.</p>
