# 721 Exchange Case Study: From Rental Portfolio to REIT Units

I keep returning to the same question when an owner has held rental real estate for decades: is the asset still serving the owner, or has the owner begun serving the asset? That question is especially sharp when a portfolio has appreciated, the tax bill from a sale is large, and managing tenants and repairs has become work the owner no longer wants.

Baker 1031 Research updated this 721 Exchange case study in June 2026. It is a 16 min read. This is an illustrative composite for education—not a real client, a depiction of a specific person, or a promise, projection, or guarantee of an outcome. Actual results depend on individual circumstances, the specific REIT, market conditions, tax law, and other factors. They will vary.

First-order thinking sees a 721 exchange as a way to defer a sale tax. Second-order thinking asks what happens to concentration, management burden, liquidity, estate division, control, and the risks of the REIT or DST being selected. Deferral is important, but it is only one part of whether an irreversible transition makes sense.

## The situation

Consider an illustrative owner, a composite rather than a real person. She is in her early 70s and has accumulated several rental properties—apartments and small commercial buildings—over decades. They have appreciated substantially and carry low basis after long ownership and depreciation. A taxable sale could create a large [capital gains tax](capital-gains-tax-on-real-estate.html), including the four-layer stack and meaningful depreciation recapture.

The portfolio has become a burden. Tenants, maintenance, and daily operating work are harder to carry as she ages. Most of her net worth is concentrated in this particular real estate. She is also thinking about her three children: one portfolio of properties can be difficult to divide cleanly among several heirs.

The case therefore reflects a common cluster of facts: appreciated, low-basis, management-intensive, concentrated rentals; management fatigue; concentration risk; and estate-planning concerns. A [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html) can be considered for this kind of situation, but this composite remains an educational illustration rather than a recommendation for a specific owner.

## The goals

The owner’s goals follow from the problem. She wants passivity instead of landlording and wants passive income rather than ongoing management. She wants diversification so that most of her wealth is not tied to a few individual buildings. She wants an estate plan that can pass wealth tax-efficiently and divide it cleanly among three children.

She also wants to defer the large tax a sale could create—potentially a third or more of a substantial gain—so more pre-tax wealth remains invested. Finally, she wants liquidity flexibility. Direct property is illiquid; over time she may need capital for healthcare, gifts, or another purpose.

Put simply, the targets are passivity, diversification, estate planning, tax deferral, and liquidity flexibility. Those are the benefits this illustrative 721 exchange is designed to address. They are not universal benefits. An owner who primarily wants to retain direct control or continued 1031 flexibility may reach a different conclusion.

## The strategy

In the illustration, the owner moves the rental portfolio into REIT ownership through a 721 exchange. A direct contribution to a REIT may be possible if a REIT wants the specific properties. More often, the practical path is the DST bridge: first a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) into DST interests structured for a later 721 exit, and then a transition into REIT [OP units](op-units-explained.html).

With a securities-licensed advisor, CPA, and attorney, she would assess suitability, select the REIT or DST by quality, diversification, and structure, and carry out the contribution. The contribution can be direct property for OP units or a DST-then-721 path. Tax protection and documentation belong with the appropriate professionals.

The strategy is tailored to the case’s stated goals: diversified, passive REIT ownership, tax deferral, and potential estate benefits. It is not a standardized template and does not guarantee a result. A real transaction must be built around the owner’s property, tax position, objectives, and available REIT or DST options.

## The illustrative outcome

In the hypothetical outcome, the owner no longer actively manages rental property. She holds OP units in a diversified REIT and receives passive distributions rather than dealing with tenants and maintenance. That addresses the passivity objective.

The concentrated portfolio becomes a stake in the REIT’s broader holdings, reducing the risk attached to a handful of particular properties. The gain that a sale could have triggered is deferred under Section 721, allowing her pre-tax wealth to remain in the REIT. The OP units may also offer liquidity flexibility because they can be converted to shares over time, subject to the applicable terms and tax consequences, if cash is needed.

The estate result is also part of the illustration. OP units can be divided among her three children more readily than a physical portfolio. If the units are held until death, a step-up in basis can erase deferred gain for heirs under current law. These are hypothetical potential outcomes, not promised distributions, liquidity, tax results, or estate results.

## The illustrative tax result

Had the owner sold the rentals, the four-layer tax on the low-basis gain could have taken a large immediate amount—potentially a third or more of gain—and reduced the capital available to reinvest. In this illustration, Section 721 defers that tax, so pre-tax wealth goes into the REIT instead of only the after-tax remainder.

That distinction compounds over time. The deferred tax remains invested rather than being paid at the sale, and distributions are earned on the full value in the illustration. Deferral is not tax elimination during life; it is the postponement of a liability, subject to the terms of the transaction and tax law.

For estate planning, the illustration adds a further possibility: if the owner holds OP units until death, the step-up in basis can eliminate deferred gain for her heirs. The actual result depends on her facts, current law, and CPA analysis. The case is meant to show the potential value of deferral and a possible step-up, not to state a tax conclusion for any reader.

## Key takeaways

- This is an illustrative composite, not a client result or promise; actual outcomes vary.
- The hypothetical aging owner had appreciated, concentrated, management-intensive property and sought passivity, diversification, estate planning, deferral, and liquidity.
- The strategy was a 721 exchange, potentially through a DST bridge, into diversified REIT OP units on a tax-deferred basis.
- The hypothetical result addressed those goals, with tax deferral and a potential step-up contributing to wealth and estate planning.

## Lessons from the case

The first lesson is that one strategy can address more than one goal. A 721 exchange can combine passivity, diversification, estate planning, deferral, and some liquidity flexibility. That is why it may suit an owner with this exact group of priorities.

The second is practical. A direct REIT contribution often is not feasible because a REIT may not want the owner’s specific properties. The DST bridge—the 1031-then-721 path—often creates the path to REIT ownership. The third is that an advisor, CPA, and attorney are not optional decoration around a complicated transaction. They are necessary to assess suitability, tax treatment, documentation, and the route selected.

Finally, strategy must fit the goals. It works in this illustration because the owner wants less control, broader ownership, and a divisible estate. It may not fit someone who wants to keep actively controlling and exchanging direct real estate. Each owner’s facts differ.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is this case study based on a real client?

No. It is an illustrative composite created for educational purposes, not a specific client or that client’s result. It combines common circumstances to show how a 721 exchange may be applied. It is not a promise, projection, guarantee, or representation of typical results.

### Will I get the same results as the case study?

Not necessarily. Actual results depend on the reader’s properties, gain, basis, goals, selected REIT, market conditions, tax law, and other facts. The example presents a potential application, not a predicted or typical result.

### Why use the DST bridge in the case study?

A REIT often will not directly accept a particular set of individual properties. The 1031-then-721 path can let an owner exchange direct property into DSTs structured for a 721 exit and later transition to REIT OP units. That is frequently the practical route to tax-deferred REIT ownership from direct property.

### What goals does the 721 exchange address in the case?

The illustration addresses passivity through distributions, diversification through a REIT portfolio, estate planning through divisible units and a possible step-up, tax deferral through Section 721, and liquidity flexibility through potentially convertible units. Its ability to address several goals at once is the point of the example.

### How much tax did the owner defer in the case?

No exact figure is stated. Conceptually, she deferred the four-layer tax on a substantial low-basis gain, potentially a third or more of gain. The actual amount would depend on gain, basis, depreciation, state, and other circumstances and should be calculated by the CPA.

### Could the step-up eliminate the deferred tax in the case?

In the illustration, it can if she holds the OP units until death and a step-up is available under current law. Her heirs could inherit with the deferred gain eliminated. That outcome depends on holding period, current law, and the actual facts; it is not guaranteed.

### What lessons does the case offer?

It shows the ability to address passivity, diversification, estate planning, deferral, and liquidity together; the practical role of the DST bridge; the need for an advisor, CPA, and attorney; and the importance of matching the strategy to the owner’s goals. Every situation differs.

### Does the 721 exchange suit owners with multiple heirs?

It can. OP units can divide cleanly among several heirs, unlike a single portfolio that may be difficult to partition. A possible step-up can also support tax-efficient inheritance. The estate result remains dependent on the family’s facts and current law.

### Is the 721 exchange right for me if my situation is similar?

Possibly, if the reader has appreciated, concentrated, management-intensive property and values passivity, diversification, estate planning, deferral, and flexibility. The case is not a template. An advisor, CPA, and attorney should determine whether it fits the reader’s actual goals and circumstances.

### What if my goals are different from the case?

The 721 may not fit. An owner who wants control and continued direct-property 1031 exchange options may prefer a 1031 strategy or another approach. The appropriate strategy follows the real objectives rather than the example.

### Does Baker 1031 guarantee results like the case?

No. Baker 1031 does not guarantee results. The case is illustrative and educational. Actual outcomes depend on the owner, REIT, market conditions, tax law, and other variables. The goal is sound guidance and a tailored approach, not a promised result.

### What if the illustrative owner had wanted to keep some property?

She could use a partial transition: contribute some property through the 721 strategy for passivity, diversification, and estate benefits while retaining other property for direct ownership, control, and 1031 flexibility. That can balance mixed goals, but it must be tailored to the owner’s facts.

### How would the owner’s children benefit in the case?

The three children could receive divisible OP units, each able to manage their own units, rather than a hard-to-divide portfolio. If their mother held the units until death and a step-up applied, they could inherit at a stepped-up value and potentially convert or sell without income tax on the prior appreciation.

### Is this case typical of 721 exchange outcomes?

No. It is not offered as typical or average. It is a composite designed to demonstrate potential application. Results can be better or worse and the strategy does not suit every owner.
