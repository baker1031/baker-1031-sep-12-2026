# 1031 Exchange Across State Lines

I keep seeing investors treat a state line as though it changes the federal exchange rule. It does not. What changes is the number of tax systems, professional relationships, and local assumptions attached to the decision.

An owner may want to move real estate toward a new home or retirement location, leave a market they no longer favor, pursue an opportunity elsewhere, or spread exposure across more than one state. A properly structured 1031 exchange can allow that move without current federal recognition of gain. The hard part is not the map. It is keeping the state-level consequences and the replacement decision in view at the same time.

First-order thinking is, “I can move the property to a lower-tax state and leave the old tax behind.” Second-order thinking is, “Which state still tracks the gain, what filings survive the move, what does the replacement actually cost to own, and am I buying an asset I would want without the exchange pressure?” The federal rule is broad. The planning should be, too.

## You can exchange across states

There is no federal restriction on moving from one U.S. state to another in a 1031 exchange. The [like-kind standard](like-kind-property-what-qualifies-1031.html) generally treats U.S. real property held for investment as like-kind to other U.S. real property held for investment, regardless of where either property sits. A California investor can exchange into Texas property; a New York owner can exchange into Florida real estate. The federal deferral works on the same terms as an in-state exchange if the exchange otherwise qualifies.

That flexibility can let an investor leave a market, consolidate holdings closer to home, or diversify across markets without current tax recognition. It does not, however, cross the U.S./foreign divide. U.S. real property is like-kind only to other U.S. real property. Foreign property is like-kind only to other foreign property. You cannot exchange one for the other and qualify.

So the federal point is simple: within the United States, you can [exchange across state lines](1031-exchange.html). The work begins with the state tax rules, not the federal geography.

## Multistate tax filing issues

An exchange can involve three states at once:

- The **source state**, where the relinquished property is located, generally has a claim on gain from property inside its borders.
- The **residence state** generally taxes its residents on income from all sources.
- The **replacement state** governs the replacement property’s future income and eventual sale.

In a fully deferred exchange, gain is deferred federally and, in states that conform, at the state level as well. That does not necessarily eliminate current filing obligations. A source state may require reporting of the exchange, particularly where it has a clawback rule. The residence-state return still reflects the taxpayer’s overall facts. Later, as the replacement produces income or is sold, the relevant state rules come back into the picture.

The key interaction is usually the source state’s tax and the residence state’s tax. Many states tax residents on out-of-state income but allow a credit for tax paid to the source state so the same income is not taxed twice. The mechanics differ by state. A multistate CPA is the person who maps those mechanics and makes sure the required filings are made.

Federal deferral is uniform. A cross-state exchange is not. It touches the source, residence, and replacement states, each with its own tax and filing considerations.

## Clawback and reporting states

The most important state-level issue is the clawback provision, most prominently associated with California. When an owner exchanges property in a clawback state for an out-of-state replacement, the state may defer the gain at the exchange but keep track of it. If the owner later sells the out-of-state replacement in a taxable sale rather than completing another exchange, the state can tax the previously deferred in-state gain.

California requires annual reporting of deferred California-source gain on **FTB Form 3840** while the out-of-state replacement is held, and it taxes the gain when that replacement is sold. Massachusetts, Montana, and Oregon have also adopted clawback or special reporting provisions for out-of-state replacement property, although their details vary. See the guide to [state-specific 1031 rules and the California clawback](state-specific-1031-rules-california-clawback.html).

The idea is plain: the state defers, but does not forgive. Moving a replacement to a no-income-tax state does not erase the source state’s claim on the original gain. Missing an annual filing can trigger the deferred gain, and the later tax must be planned for unless another exchange continues the deferral. Ask your CPA whether the source state has a clawback rule before the relinquished property is listed.

## Sourcing an out-of-state replacement

The tax work is only one part of the problem. In a home market, an investor may know neighborhoods, pricing, local operators, and what is changing. In an unfamiliar state, that knowledge has to be built or borrowed. The [45-day identification period](45-day-identification-period.html) does not leave much time to build it after a sale closes.

Three approaches can help:

- A local broker or advisor can bring target-market knowledge and access to opportunities.
- A national advisor with multistate reach can help source and evaluate replacements outside the investor’s home market.
- A [Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)](dst-guide.html) can offer professionally managed, diversified real estate, sometimes across multiple states, without requiring the investor to source one direct property alone.

DSTs can reduce the practical sourcing burden. The sponsor assembles and manages the portfolio, while the investor gains exposure to real estate in markets that would be difficult to evaluate quickly on an individual basis. That does not eliminate the investor’s work. The investor still must read the sponsor’s private placement memorandum (PPM) and conduct independent diligence.

DSTs are illiquid securities. They carry fees, sponsor risk, concentration risk, and possible loss of principal, and they are sold only to verified accredited investors. They can be one replacement option, not a substitute for deciding whether the underlying real estate and the security’s terms make sense.

## Coordinate the professionals before the sale

One [qualified intermediary (QI)](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) can handle the federal exchange mechanics nationwide. You do not need a QI in each state. The parts that benefit from state-specific experience are conveyancing law, local market sourcing, multistate filing, and clawback compliance.

The CPA has a particularly important role in a cross-state transaction. A CPA familiar with multistate taxation can map the source-state, residence-state, and replacement-state consequences; identify clawback and reporting obligations; handle required filings; and prepare for the eventual state tax. Some obligations arrive years after the exchange. That is why the multistate view must be part of the initial decision, not a cleanup exercise after closing.

Replacement expertise rounds out the group. Whether it comes from a local broker, a national advisor, or a DST option, an investor needs a credible way to source and vet the target asset. Before the relinquished-property sale closes, line up the QI, the multistate-savvy CPA, and the support needed to evaluate replacement property.

## Why investors move real estate across states

Relocation is a common reason. An owner who lives in one state while holding property in another may prefer investments closer to home or to a future retirement location for management convenience or estate-planning reasons. A 1031 exchange can reposition that real estate without triggering current gain.

Market preferences matter, too. An investor may want to leave a softening market, enter a stronger one, or follow a specific opportunity. Some look to no-income-tax states such as [Texas](1031-exchange-texas.html) or [Florida](1031-exchange-florida.html) because of the replacement property’s future income. But the original source state may still have a clawback claim on the relinquished gain.

Geographic diversification is another reason. Replacements can be spread among more than one state, and a DST can itself hold a portfolio across markets. That may reduce exposure to a single market, but it also adds a different set of underwriting, liquidity, and sponsor questions.

The exchange should start with the investor’s real objective: relocation, market repositioning, diversification, or tax positioning. Once that is clear, the tax rules can be organized around the decision instead of driving it.

## Key takeaways

- U.S. investment real property can be exchanged across state lines under the federal like-kind rule. Foreign property remains separate.
- A cross-state exchange can bring the source, residence, and replacement states into the tax picture, with filing obligations and credits that vary by state.
- California and other clawback/reporting states may track deferred gain and collect it on a later taxable sale of an out-of-state replacement.
- Local professionals, a national advisor, or a DST can help source replacements, but each option calls for real diligence.
- Put the QI, multistate CPA, and replacement-sourcing team in place before the sale closes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I do a 1031 exchange across state lines?

Yes. Federal rules generally allow a U.S. investment property in one state to be exchanged for U.S. investment property in another state, with gain deferred just as in an in-state exchange. Foreign property is a separate category and is not like-kind to U.S. property.

### Does exchanging across states affect the federal deferral?

No. Federal qualification is the same whether the two properties are in the same state or different states. Cross-state complexity comes from state tax filing, clawback, and replacement sourcing.

### What multistate tax issues arise?

The source state can tax gain from the relinquished property, the residence state generally taxes income from all sources, and the replacement state applies to future income and sale of the new property. Credits may reduce double taxation, but filings and mechanics vary.

### What is a clawback state?

A clawback state, most notably California and also Massachusetts, Montana, Oregon, and others, may tax gain that was deferred when the owner later sells an out-of-state replacement without another exchange. California’s annual reporting mechanism is FTB Form 3840. The state defers the gain but continues to track it.

### Does moving to a no-income-tax state avoid the clawback?

No. The destination state may not impose its own income tax, but that does not remove the source clawback state’s claim on previously deferred gain.

### How do I find replacement property in another state?

An investor can use a local broker or advisor, a national advisor with multistate reach, or a DST that provides professionally managed real-estate exposure across markets. Each choice calls for investigation of the property or offering.

### Why are DSTs popular for cross-state exchanges?

They can reduce the challenge of sourcing and diligencing a direct property in an unfamiliar market during the 45-day identification window. They offer professional management and possible geographic diversification, but they are illiquid, fee-bearing securities with risks and are limited to accredited investors through a PPM.

### Do I have to file taxes in multiple states?

Possibly. A source state may require exchange reporting, a residence return reflects the taxpayer’s full situation and credits, and rules apply as the replacement is held and sold. A multistate CPA should determine the actual requirements.

### Why do investors exchange across state lines?

Common reasons include moving real estate closer to home or retirement, leaving a softening market, following opportunity, seeking future-income tax positioning, and spreading holdings across markets. The exchange allows the geographic move to be tax-deferred when the rules are met.

### What professionals do I need for a cross-state exchange?

Use a QI for federal mechanics, a CPA who understands multistate taxation, and local or national replacement-sourcing expertise, or evaluate a DST. The CPA is especially important because state rules vary and some obligations can appear years later.

### Can I exchange U.S. property for foreign property?

No. U.S. property is like-kind only to other U.S. property, and foreign real estate is like-kind only to foreign real estate. The federal flexibility runs across U.S. state lines, not between the two categories.

### Is a cross-state exchange much harder than an in-state one?

The federal exchange qualification is identical. The added work is state filing, clawback compliance, and unfamiliar-market sourcing. With coordinated guidance, it is manageable rather than prohibitive.

### Do I need a qualified intermediary in each state?

No. One QI handles the federal exchange nationwide. You may still want local attorneys or brokers and a CPA who can address the states involved.

### Will I owe tax in the replacement state right away?

Generally not at the exchange itself. Gain is deferred federally and in conforming states. The replacement state’s rules apply to the property’s future income and eventual sale, while the source and residence states have their own rules.

### Can I diversify across several states in one exchange?

Yes. Subject to the identification rules, an investor can acquire replacements in multiple states. A DST may also hold real estate across several markets, which can reduce single-market exposure while adding security-specific risks.

### Should I move my real estate to where I live through a 1031?

Many investors do for management convenience or estate-planning reasons. It is a valid use of a cross-state exchange, but the investor should account for source-state clawback, multistate taxes, and whether the replacement fits the new market.

## Glossary

- **Cross-State Exchange:** A 1031 exchange that relinquishes property in one state and acquires it in another; permitted federally.
- **Like-Kind:** The standard that treats U.S. investment real property as like-kind across state lines.
- **Source State:** The state where the relinquished property is located; it may tax gain and require reporting.
- **Residence State:** The taxpayer’s home state, generally taxing income from all sources with credits for source-state tax.
- **Replacement State:** The state where the replacement property is located, governing its future income and sale.
- **Clawback Provision:** A state rule that taxes previously deferred gain when an out-of-state replacement is later sold.
- **FTB Form 3840:** California’s annual reporting form for deferred gain on out-of-state 1031 replacements.
- **Double Taxation Credit:** A credit for tax paid to one state against another state’s tax on the same income.
- **Multistate Filing:** Tax filing obligations in more than one state arising from a cross-state exchange.
- **Foreign Property:** Non-U.S. real estate, like-kind only to other foreign property, not U.S. property.
- **Geographic Diversification:** Spreading real estate across several states to reduce single-market risk, often through DSTs.
- **No-Income-Tax State:** A state without income tax, such as Texas or Florida; a source clawback can still apply.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive multistate real-property interest that can ease cross-state replacement sourcing.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party handling uniform federal exchange mechanics.
- **Conveyancing Law:** State-specific law governing real-estate transfers.
- **Multistate Tax Coordination:** A CPA’s mapping of source, residence, and replacement-state tax consequences.
