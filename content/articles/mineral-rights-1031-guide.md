# Mineral Rights & Royalties for 1031 Exchanges

Definitive Guide · 2026 · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 28 min read

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Producing oil, gas, and mineral interests can be exchanged tax-deferred into real estate, including a passive DST, but only when they are the right type of interest. This guide identifies what qualifies, what does not, and how to work through an exchange.

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">I have been thinking about how quickly an owner can move from “I have minerals” to “I have a 1031 replacement.” The distance between those statements can be large. A label is not an eligibility opinion; the duration and legal character of the actual interest do the work.</p><p>First-order thinking focuses on the promise of deferral and a passive replacement. Second-order thinking asks whether the interest is perpetual, whether state law supports real-property treatment, whether the 45- and 180-day process can be met, and what risks are being exchanged rather than removed.</p>
<p>The whole game comes down to one question, answered two ways below — once in plain language, and once in an interactive tool that classifies your specific interest. Beginners can read straight through; owners who already know a working interest from an override can jump to the <a href="#qualify" style="color:var(--blue-d);border-bottom:1px solid var(--blue)">qualifier tool</a>.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">The short version</div>
<ul>
<li>Since the 2017 tax law, Section 1031 covers <strong>real property only</strong> — and a <strong>perpetual</strong> mineral or royalty interest counts as real property.</li>
<li><strong>Qualify:</strong> mineral fee, perpetual royalties, overriding royalties (ORRIs), working interests, and most net-profits interests. <strong>Don't:</strong> production payments and carved-out term interests.</li>
<li>Qualifying minerals are like-kind to almost any U.S. real estate — so you can exchange wells for an apartment DST, or real estate into minerals, and defer the tax either way.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<h2 id="what-is">01 · What Mineral Rights &amp; Royalties Are</h2>
<p>Land ownership can be split into the <strong>surface estate</strong> and the <strong>mineral estate</strong>. Owning the mineral estate means you control the oil, gas, and minerals beneath the ground — and you can lease them to an operator in exchange for an upfront bonus and an ongoing <strong>royalty</strong> (a share of production, free of drilling and operating costs). The operator holds the <strong>working interest</strong>: the right to drill and produce, along with the obligation to pay the costs. From these flow a family of interests — overriding royalties, net-profits interests, term assignments — each with a different legal character that determines its 1031 eligibility.</p>
<h2 id="real-property">02 · Can You 1031 Them? The Real-Property Test</h2>
<p>The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act narrowed Section 1031 to <strong>real property held for investment or business</strong> — machinery, equipment, and other personal property no longer qualify. The central question for minerals is therefore whether your specific interest is <em>real</em> property. Courts have answered with a durability test: an interest that endures for the <strong>life of the reserve or the underlying lease</strong> — a perpetual interest — is real property and can be exchanged like-kind for other real estate. The foundational case, <em>Commissioner v. Crichton</em> (1941), held that a mineral royalty interest swapped for a city lot was a valid like-kind exchange. Duration, not the label, is what matters.</p>
<div class="pullquote">
<p>A royalty that lasts as long as the well produces is real property. A royalty carved out for a fixed term or a fixed number of barrels is just income — and income isn't like-kind to land.</p>
<cite>Baker 1031 Research</cite>
</div>

<h2 id="perpetual">04 · The Perpetual-vs-Term Rule</h2>
<p>If you remember one principle, make it this. A <strong>perpetual</strong> interest — one that runs until the resource is exhausted or the lease ends — is treated as a fee-like real-property interest and is broadly like-kind to other real estate. A <strong>term or carved-out</strong> interest — a royalty limited to a set number of years or barrels, or a production payment that simply assigns a slice of future income — is treated as personal property or an income right, and is <em>not</em> like-kind to a fee. In <em>Commissioner v. P.G. Lake</em> (1958), a carved-out oil payment exchanged for a fee interest failed, even though state law called it real property. The lesson: the federal duration test can override the state-law label.</p>

<h2 id="state">08 · State-by-State Nuance</h2>
<p>Whether a mineral interest is real property is ultimately a question of <strong>state law</strong>, and the major producing states are generally favorable. Texas, Oklahoma, New Mexico, North Dakota, and most others treat severed mineral and royalty interests as real property — which is precisely why exchanges out of those states are common. But the details vary: some states characterize certain royalty or production interests differently, and an interest treated as real property in one state may be analyzed differently in another. Where your minerals sit, and where your replacement property sits, both matter. This is the single most important reason to involve a qualified intermediary and tax counsel familiar with oil-and-gas exchanges before you commit.</p>
<h2 id="taxes">09 · Taxes: Depletion, Recapture &amp; Inherited Minerals</h2>
<p>Mineral owners don't depreciate — they take <strong>depletion</strong>, either cost depletion (recovering basis as reserves are produced) or percentage depletion (a fixed percentage of gross income). That matters at exchange time because of <strong>recapture</strong>: on a sale, Section 1254 can recapture previously deducted intangible drilling costs and depletion as ordinary income. A properly structured 1031 defers that recapture along with the capital gain — but any boot (cash or non-like-kind property you receive) can trigger it. Two more points worth knowing: <strong>inherited minerals</strong> receive a stepped-up basis at death, which often reduces or eliminates the gain and the need to exchange at all; and <strong>valuation</strong> is genuinely hard — there's no MLS for minerals, so a defensible appraisal of producing reserves is essential to document the exchange.</p>
<h2 id="strategy">10 · From Active Wells to Passive Real Estate</h2>
<p>For many owners — especially those inheriting interests or tiring of commodity swings — the appeal of a mineral 1031 is the chance to convert volatile, depleting royalty income into <strong>stable, diversified real estate</strong>. Minerals are among the highest-yielding interests around (our sector benchmark puts producing mineral and royalty interests near a 9.6% average yield), but that income declines as wells deplete and swings with prices. Exchanging into a DST or net-lease portfolio trades some of that upside for predictability, professional management, and easier estate division. It doesn't have to be all-or-nothing — a popular play is to exchange part of a mineral position into a DST while keeping the rest. Use the selector to see which path fits you.</p>

<h2 id="risks">11 · Risks &amp; Due Diligence</h2>
<p>The tax deferral is only worth pursuing if the underlying assets are sound. On the minerals you're selling, get a credible reserve-based valuation and clean title — defects and unclear ownership are common and can derail a closing. On the replacement side, apply the same rigor you would to any acquisition: vet the sponsor, the leverage, and the income durability (our <a href="dst-guide.html" style="color:var(--blue-d);border-bottom:1px solid var(--blue)">DST guide</a> has a full sponsor-review tool for exactly this). And mind the classification risk that runs through this whole guide — an interest you assume is real property but that the IRS treats as a production payment turns a "tax-free" exchange into a fully taxable sale.</p>
<h2 id="faq">12 · Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq">
<details open=""><summary>Can I really 1031 mineral rights into an apartment building or DST?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Yes — if your mineral interest is a qualifying, perpetual real-property interest. Since real property is broadly like-kind to real property, qualifying minerals can be exchanged into fee real estate, a net-lease asset, or a Delaware Statutory Trust, deferring the capital-gains tax.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>What's the difference between a working interest and a royalty interest for 1031?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>A working interest is the operating interest — the right to drill and produce, with the cost obligations — and is a real-property interest. A royalty is a cost-free share of production; it qualifies when it's perpetual (lasts as long as the well produces), but a term royalty may not.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Why don't production payments qualify?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>A production payment is treated as an assignment of future income — economically a loan secured by production, not an ownership interest in the minerals. The Supreme Court's P.G. Lake decision treated a carved-out oil payment as income, so it isn't like-kind to real estate.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Do I have to exchange into more minerals?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>No. That's the flexibility of the real-property rule — qualifying minerals can go into almost any U.S. real estate, and real estate can be exchanged into minerals. Many owners diversify out of oil and gas entirely.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>I inherited my minerals — should I still do a 1031?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Maybe not. Inherited minerals get a stepped-up basis to fair-market value at death, which can eliminate most or all of the taxable gain — reducing the reason to exchange. Run the basis math before assuming a 1031 is needed.</p></div></details>
</div>
<h2 id="glossary">13 · Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>Mineral Estate</dt><dd>Ownership of the oil, gas, and minerals beneath a tract — separable from the surface estate.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Royalty Interest</dt><dd>A cost-free share of production. Real property for 1031 when perpetual.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Working Interest</dt><dd>The operating interest — the right to drill and produce, bearing the costs. A real-property interest.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Overriding Royalty (ORRI)</dt><dd>A royalty carved out of the working interest; real property when it lasts the life of the lease.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Net Profits Interest (NPI)</dt><dd>A share of net profits from production; generally qualifying but structure-sensitive.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Production Payment</dt><dd>A right to a set amount of production or proceeds — treated as income, not real property.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Perpetual Interest</dt><dd>An interest lasting until the reserve or lease ends — the test for real-property treatment.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Depletion</dt><dd>The mineral analog to depreciation; recovers basis (cost) or a percentage of income (percentage).</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Section 1254 Recapture</dt><dd>Recapture of deducted drilling costs and depletion as ordinary income on disposition.</dd></div>
</dl>
