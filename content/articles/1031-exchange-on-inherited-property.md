# 1031 Exchange on Inherited Property

I keep seeing the same instinct after an inherited-property sale is proposed: “It is real estate, so we should do a 1031.” The first question is usually more basic. After the step-up in basis, is there enough gain left to defer?

Inherited investment real estate generally receives a new basis at fair-market value on the prior owner’s date of death. That can erase the appreciation accumulated during the decedent’s ownership for income-tax purposes. A sale soon after inheritance may therefore produce very little tax. But time, post-inheritance depreciation, a need to reposition, and family ownership can change the answer.

## Inherited property and step-up in basis

When property is inherited, including investment real estate, its basis is generally [stepped up to fair-market value at the decedent’s date of death](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html), or at an alternate valuation date. The heir begins near current value rather than at the price the decedent paid. Appreciation during the decedent’s ownership is generally erased for income-tax purposes.

That changes the comparison sharply. The decedent may have owned the asset for decades, accumulated appreciation, and had a low basis reduced further by depreciation. A sale by that owner might have produced a large four-layer tax. The heir selling near the stepped-up value may have little built-in gain and little immediate tax. The step-up is one of the most valuable tax benefits associated with appreciated inherited property.

It also resets depreciation for the heir. If the heir keeps the property as a rental, depreciation begins from the higher stepped-up basis, often producing larger deductions than the decedent had. A [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) defers gain; where a near-term sale has little gain, there may be little for a 1031 to defer. Start with the step-up before deciding anything else.

## When a 1031 still helps

A 1031 can become useful as gain accumulates after inheritance. If the heir holds the property for years and it appreciates, or if post-inheritance depreciation lowers the stepped-up basis, a sale can generate taxable gain above that new basis. The longer the hold and the more appreciation or basis reduction, the more a 1031 can defer.

Repositioning can matter as much as tax. An heir may inherit a management-intensive rental, a property in a market they do not want exposure to, or a concentrated asset that does not fit their goals. An exchange can move that value into a better-fitting property or passive DSTs while keeping accumulated gain deferred and the full value invested rather than selling and rebuying after tax.

An older inheritance is different from a fresh one. The step-up may now be remote history, with appreciation since inheritance and depreciation lowering basis. That is much like any other long-held investment property. “Inherited property” is not one outcome: a 1031 can help when gain has accumulated through appreciation, depreciation, or time, or when repositioning makes sense.

> Just after inheritance, the step-up can leave little gain to defer. A 1031 becomes more useful when gain accumulates or when the heir needs a different kind of property.

## Holding-period considerations

For capital-gains purposes, inherited property is automatically treated as held long-term regardless of how long the heir actually owns it. Any gain on sale is therefore long-term capital gain at the favorable rates rather than short-term gain. This benefit is separate from the step-up.

For a 1031, though, the held-for-investment requirement still applies. An [inherited rental that the heir continues to rent](inherited-rental-property-tax-options.html) can be investment property and can be exchanged. A property the heir moves into as a personal residence is not held for investment and cannot be 1031-exchanged. The heir’s use controls eligibility.

Both the inherited relinquished property and the replacement need investment use and investment intent. There is [no special 1031 holding-period rule for inherited property beyond the general standard](1031-exchange-holding-period-how-long.html). Automatic long-term capital-gain treatment is helpful, but it does not substitute for the investment-use requirement on either side of the exchange.

## Exchanging into passive DSTs

For heirs who want the inherited value working but do not want to operate the real estate, [an exchange into passive DSTs](inherited-property-and-dst-1031-exchanges.html) can be an option. A [Delaware Statutory Trust](dst-guide.html) can convert a management-intensive rental or commercial property into passive, professionally managed, diversified real estate while deferring accumulated gain.

The trade-offs are real. DST interests are illiquid, there is no established secondary market, investors have no management or voting rights over the underlying property, distributions are not guaranteed and may be reduced or suspended, and principal can be lost.

Where a property has appreciated since inheritance and the heir wants to leave active management, a DST exchange can preserve the full value invested, defer gain, and replace hands-on ownership with passive exposure. Any distribution arrives without management responsibility, but it is not guaranteed. The value is diversified across institutional real estate rather than concentrated in the single inherited asset.

DSTs can also reduce the friction of multiple heirs owning one building. Each heir may hold an independent fractional interest and decide independently when to exit rather than operating the shared property together. That does not give any heir management or voting rights over the real estate, and the interest remains illiquid. DSTs are securities offered only to accredited investors pursuant to a private placement memorandum under Regulation D. Distributions are not guaranteed. Investors should read the PPM, including risk factors, before investing.

## Coordinating with the estate

The step-up depends on a defensible date-of-death valuation. It establishes the heir’s basis for a future sale or exchange. An understated or poorly documented value can create trouble later, so estate administration and the heir’s planning should align at the time of inheritance.

When several heirs inherit together, early coordination helps them decide whether to hold, sell, or exchange, and whether ownership should remain joint, become separate interests, or be repositioned through a DST. Some families use [a 1031 exchange into DSTs for trust and estate beneficiaries](dsts-for-trust-and-estate-beneficiaries.html) so each heir holds a passive, divisible interest rather than a friction-prone shared property.

Personal circumstances matter. An heir who needs cash may simply sell, often with little tax immediately after inheritance because of the step-up. An heir seeking passive income may prefer DSTs. An heir building a real estate portfolio may prefer other replacement property. The exchange discussion typically begins when post-inheritance appreciation exists or management is no longer wanted. Coordinate the estate, the CPA, and the other heirs where relevant.

### Key Takeaways

- Inherited investment property generally receives a date-of-death step-up in basis, often erasing the gain and making a fresh 1031 unnecessary.
- A 1031 can help when gain accumulates through post-inheritance appreciation, depreciation, or time, or when the heir wants to reposition.
- Inherited property receives automatic long-term gain treatment, but the held-for-investment rule still governs 1031 eligibility.
- Passive DSTs can fit heirs who do not want management and can give multiple heirs independent interests; coordinate with the estate and a CPA.

## An heir's decision framework

First, quantify the current gain. With a CPA, [compare likely sale value with the stepped-up basis](role-of-your-cpa-in-a-1031-exchange.html), adjusted for depreciation taken since inheritance. If the gain is small, as it often is soon after inheritance, the tax case for a 1031 is weak and a simple sale may be best. If gain is substantial because of appreciation, an older inheritance, or post-inheritance depreciation, a 1031 can defer meaningful tax.

Second, define the goal beyond tax. Is the heir seeking cash, continued investment, an exit from management, or repositioning? A passive DST exchange may make sense for an heir who wants passive income from a rental they do not want to manage, even when immediate tax is modest. The goal, not tax alone, drives the decision.

Third, weigh cost and family circumstances. A 1031 has fees and complexity that a straightforward sale avoids. First-order thinking treats any gain as a reason to exchange. Second-order thinking weighs the tax benefit against cost, management, liquidity, and the value of giving heirs a workable ownership structure. For a fresh inheritance, the step-up often makes a sale cleaner. When accumulated gain or repositioning is valuable, the exchange can earn its complexity. Run the numbers with a CPA before deciding.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I do a 1031 exchange on inherited property?

Yes, if it is investment real estate held for investment. A fresh step-up often means little gain to defer, making a 1031 unnecessary at first. It becomes more useful after appreciation, depreciation, time, or when repositioning is wanted.

### What is the step-up in basis on inherited property?

Basis generally resets to fair-market value at the decedent’s death, erasing appreciation during their ownership for income-tax purposes. A sale soon after inheritance near that value commonly produces little gain and little tax.

### Do I need a 1031 if I just inherited the property?

Often not. Compare current gain with stepped-up basis through a CPA. A 1031 is generally useful for accumulated gain or a repositioning goal, not merely because the property was inherited.

### When does a 1031 help with inherited property?

It can help after post-inheritance appreciation, a longer hold, or depreciation that lowers basis, and when a replacement property or DST better fits the heir’s goals. Older inheritances with substantial built-in gain are analyzed like other longtime ownership.

### Is inherited property treated as long-term for capital gains?

Yes. It is automatically treated as held long-term for capital-gains purposes, regardless of actual ownership time. This is separate from the step-up and avoids a short-hold short-term-gain issue.

### Does the held-for-investment rule apply to inherited property?

Yes. A continued rental can be exchanged, but a property converted to the heir’s personal residence is not investment property and cannot be 1031-exchanged. Use determines eligibility.

### Can I exchange inherited property into DSTs?

Yes. When there is gain to defer and passive ownership is desired, DSTs can replace active management with professionally managed diversified real estate. They are securities and require a suitability review.

### How do DSTs help when multiple heirs inherit together?

They can give each heir an independent, divisible interest instead of requiring all heirs to operate a shared property. Each can make later decisions on their own timeline, reducing joint-ownership friction.

### What if I inherited the property years ago?

The step-up may be old news. Appreciation and depreciation since inheritance may have created substantial gain, and a 1031 can defer it. A CPA can quantify the current gain.

### How do I value inherited property for the step-up?

Use fair-market value at the decedent’s date of death, or the alternate valuation date, generally supported by an appraisal. A defensible estate valuation establishes the heir’s basis for future sale or exchange.

### Should I sell or exchange inherited property?

It depends on gain and goals. Little gain plus a need for cash can favor a sale. Accumulated gain, passive income, repositioning, or retaining value invested can support a 1031, perhaps into DSTs. There is no one answer.

### What's the first step for an heir considering a sale or exchange?

Ask a CPA to compare likely sale value with stepped-up basis, adjusted for heir depreciation. Then clarify cash, passive-income, and repositioning goals; weigh costs against benefits and coordinate the estate and other heirs.

### Can I move into inherited investment property and still exchange it?

No. Moving in converts it to personal use, so it is no longer held for investment and cannot be 1031-exchanged. It might later qualify for the Section 121 residence exclusion, but moving in gives up the 1031 option.

### Do I owe tax on the appreciation during the decedent's ownership?

No. The step-up erases that appreciation for income-tax purposes. Tax arises on appreciation after inheritance above the stepped-up basis, which a 1031 can defer if substantial.

### What if several of us inherited the property and disagree?

A DST exchange can give each heir an independent divisible interest, or the property can be sold and proceeds divided, often with little tax soon after inheritance. Early coordination with family advisers helps resolve friction.

### Does inheriting depreciated rental property change things?

The step-up resets basis despite the decedent’s prior depreciation, and the heir begins depreciating anew from the higher basis. That generally means low near-term gain and larger deductions if the property is held. A CPA establishes the new schedule.

## Glossary

- **Step-Up in Basis:** Resetting inherited-property basis to fair-market value at the decedent’s death, often erasing gain.
- **Date-of-Death Value:** The fair-market value used to set an heir’s stepped-up basis.
- **Alternate Valuation Date:** An optional valuation date, generally six months after death, for estate assets.
- **Inherited Property:** Property received from a decedent, with a stepped-up basis and automatic long-term treatment.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Basis after adjustments such as depreciation; for an heir it begins at the stepped-up value.
- **Capital Gain:** The amount by which sale price exceeds adjusted basis; usually small on a near-term inherited-property sale.
- **Long-Term Treatment:** Automatic long-term treatment of inherited property for favorable capital-gains rates.
- **Held for Investment:** The 1031 requirement that inherited property be held for investment to be exchangeable.
- **1031 Exchange:** A transaction deferring gain on investment real property reinvested into like-kind real property.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive, diversified real-property interest that can give heirs independent interests.
- **Depreciation:** A rental-property deduction that restarts from stepped-up basis and lowers that basis over time.
- **Fractional Interest:** A divided share, such as a DST interest, that can be easier for multiple heirs to hold than one shared property.
- **Heir Coordination:** Aligning multiple heirs’ decisions about holding, selling, or exchanging inherited property.
- **Estate Administration:** Settling a decedent’s estate, including establishing date-of-death valuations.
- **Repositioning:** Exchanging inherited property into assets that better fit an heir’s goals.
- **Carryover Basis:** The relinquished property’s basis transferred to a replacement property in a 1031 when gain is deferred.
