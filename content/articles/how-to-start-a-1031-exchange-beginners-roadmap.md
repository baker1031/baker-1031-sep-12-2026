# How to Start a 1031 Exchange: A Beginner's Roadmap

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Category:** 1031 Exchange · **Research:** Baker 1031 Research · **Updated:** June 2026 · **Reading time:** 16 min read

I keep seeing the same avoidable sequence: an owner sells first, then begins asking how a 1031 exchange works. By then the proceeds may have reached the owner’s account, or the 45-day period is already partly gone. The rules are not impossible. The order matters.

A 1031 exchange is as much a sequencing exercise as a tax one. This roadmap follows the process from the first decision—whether an exchange fits at all—through sale, written identification, and closing. For a separate walk-through of the mechanics, see [the order of operations, step by step](1031-exchange-process-step-by-step.html). The work becomes much less stressful when it starts before the property is listed.

First-order thinking sees a 1031 as a way to postpone a tax bill. Second-order thinking asks whether the replacement investment, the calendar, the debt, the liquidity, and the owner’s actual goals still make sense after the deferral. The tax is important. It should not force an unsuitable investment decision.

## First, what a 1031 exchange actually does

Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code can allow an owner to sell investment real estate and reinvest the proceeds in other investment real estate without paying the gain tax at that sale. The tax is deferred, not forgiven. The old basis carries into the new property, so the deferred gain remains until a later taxable sale.

Repeated exchanges can continue that deferral. If the chain ultimately ends with a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death, the result may be different for heirs. That possibility is part of many estate plans, but it depends on the facts, the law at the time, and advice from the owner’s own professionals.

The potential tax stack explains why the decision gets attention. An appreciated property can produce federal capital-gains tax of up to 20%, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) taxed at a maximum of 25%, the 3.8% [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state income tax that can add 5% to 13% in high-tax states. Together, those layers routinely exceed a third of the gain. A valid exchange keeps the deferred amount invested in the replacement rather than sending it to the Treasury at the sale.

The trade is precision. The replacement must be like-kind investment real estate. A [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) must prevent the investor from receiving the proceeds. Replacement property must be identified in writing within 45 days and acquired within 180 days. Starting early is what gives those rules room to work.

## Step 1 — Decide whether an exchange actually fits your goals

An exchange is a tool, not an obligation. Start with the purpose of the sale. If the objective is to keep building a real-estate portfolio, defer tax, and potentially leave property to heirs with a stepped-up basis, the tool may be well matched to the goal. If the owner needs cash for another debt, a business, or spending, more real estate may be the wrong answer and paying the tax may be cleaner.

There is also a middle ground. In a partial exchange, an investor can reinvest most proceeds and intentionally retain some cash—called boot. Tax is due on the amount kept, while the rest can remain deferred. It is much better to plan that outcome with the CPA than to discover it as an accident at closing.

Management appetite belongs in this first decision. Many owners are exhausted by tenants, repairs, and 2 a.m. calls. Passive replacements, including Delaware Statutory Trusts (DSTs), can let an owner defer tax and leave active management at the same time. Knowing that path exists can change the answer to “Should I exchange?”

## Step 2 — Estimate the tax you would actually defer

Before deciding how much effort a deferral deserves, estimate its dollar value. A CPA—or a calculator as a first pass—can model capital gain as sale price less selling costs and adjusted basis, then add prior depreciation subject to recapture, the 3.8% net investment income tax when income is above its threshold, and applicable state tax. That total is the money a 1031 may keep invested.

Depreciation recapture commonly surprises first-time exchangers. A rental bought two decades ago may have a much lower adjusted basis after annual deductions, sometimes approaching zero for the building even when market value did not rise much. The prior deductions can therefore create substantial gain. Recapture can be taxed up to 25% regardless of holding period and is often the largest layer of the stack.

The estimate also frames replacement math. Full deferral generally requires replacement property of equal or greater value, reinvestment of all net equity, and replacement of debt that was paid off. Reinvesting less means deferring less. The tax estimate lets an owner compare the value of complete reinvestment with a deliberately partial exchange.

## Step 3 — Assemble the team before you do anything else

The most common beginner error is hiring the team too late. The non-negotiable professional is the qualified intermediary. The QI must be independent and engaged before the relinquished-property sale closes. It prepares exchange documents, receives the sale proceeds directly, holds them in a segregated account, and releases them for the replacement acquisition. If the investor receives or controls the money—even for one day—the exchange is generally over.

The CPA is the next essential participant. The CPA confirms the numbers, models the deferral, flags individual traps such as related-party rules, partnership questions, state clawback issues, and year-end timing, and reports a completed exchange on Form 8824. A CPA can also tell an owner when an exchange is not worth doing. That is valuable advice before the clock starts.

An [experienced 1031 exchange advisor](why-work-with-a-1031-exchange-advisor.html) can source replacement property, organize the deadline-driven work, and introduce passive choices such as DSTs when appropriate. A real-estate attorney may join a more complex situation. The team’s combined fees are usually a small fraction of one tax layer, and early assembly changes the process from a late sprint into a managed decision.

> The qualified intermediary belongs in place before the sale closes—not after.

## Step 4 — Plan before listing the relinquished property

The relinquished property is the property being sold. The weeks before listing it are especially valuable because the owner controls the pace. Use that time to decide whether the replacement will be active or passive, one property or several, in the same market or elsewhere, and in which asset classes. Those decisions are harder when a 45-day countdown is running.

Build a replacement shortlist in advance. Formal identification cannot happen until after the sale, but research, tours, diligence, and shortlisting can happen well before it. Prepared investors use the 45 days to confirm a considered list. Investors who begin searching after closing often discover that the clock has compressed every choice.

Plan a backup too. A fast-closing DST identified before day 45 can be a practical fallback if the preferred deal later fails because of financing, inspection, title, or a seller’s change of mind. A pre-identified DST can typically close in days. It does not make an investment automatically suitable, but it can keep one broken direct deal from destroying the exchange.

## Step 5 — Line up replacement options

The like-kind standard for U.S. investment real estate is broad. A rental house can be exchanged for an apartment building, raw land for a warehouse, or a strip mall for a fractional interest in an institutional medical office. That range allows an owner to change management burden, geography, and property type in one transaction.

Active replacements are properties the investor owns and operates directly, alone or with partners. They offer direct control and the full upside, along with continued management and the risk of completing a particular transaction inside 180 days. Passive replacements can include DSTs or triple-net property with creditworthy tenants. They shift day-to-day operation to professional managers.

DSTs commonly have accredited-investor minimums of $25,000 to $100,000, can close quickly, and can spread proceeds across several properties. Those characteristics make them useful to some investors, not automatically right for every investor. A practical beginner list often includes one or two genuine primary targets plus a vetted DST backup.

The 3-property rule lets an exchanger identify up to 3 properties of any value. This can support a primary and one or 2 alternatives. The goal is to reach day 45 with more than one viable route to a closing, not to make a single preferred transaction carry the entire tax outcome.

## Step 6 — The sale and the moment the clock starts

At the sale, the details are decisive. The purchase and sale agreement should contain cooperation-clause language stating the exchange intent. The QI must be assigned into the contract before closing. Proceeds should move directly into the QI’s qualified escrow account—not to the investor, the investor’s attorney trust account, or another account the investor can access. That prevents constructive receipt.

The sale-closing date is day zero. The 45-day identification and 180-day closing clocks start at the same time; the 180-day period does not begin when the 45-day period ends. An investor who identifies on day 45 has at most 135 days left to close. The dates are calendar dates, including weekends and holidays, and cannot be paused or extended on request. Missing either generally fails the exchange.

Late-year sales add one subtle timing issue. The due date of that year’s tax return can arrive before the 180th day and shorten the exchange period. Filing an extension can restore the full 180 days. It is a small administrative step and one reason to bring the CPA in before the sale, not after it.

## Step 7 — Identify replacements within 45 days

Identification is a signed written notice, not an intention or a phone call. It needs to describe each replacement unambiguously, usually by street address or legal description, and be delivered to the QI on or before day 45. Delivery only to a real-estate agent or attorney does not satisfy the requirement. The QI supplies the form; use it and retain the delivery confirmation.

There are three principal identification approaches:

- **3-property rule:** up to 3 properties, at any value.
- **200% rule:** any number of properties whose combined value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property’s value.
- **95% rule:** any number and any value, but the exchanger must actually acquire at least 95% of the total identified value.

The 95% rule is rarely used because it leaves so little room for a broken deal. For most beginners, a 3-property list—one primary and one or 2 backups—is more workable. Treat that backup as insurance. After day 45, a property discovered on day 60 cannot be added to the list.

## Step 8 — Close within 180 days

The QI uses the held funds to acquire the identified replacement or replacements within 180 days after the relinquished sale. Title must pass to the investor, or the same-taxpayer entity that sold the relinquished property. A new property found on day 100 cannot replace an unlisted candidate.

For full deferral, the math again matters: replacement value at least equals relinquished value, all net equity is reinvested, and paid-off debt is replaced. If the replacement carries less debt, the shortfall is mortgage boot and can be taxable unless additional cash offsets it. A leveraged DST may use pre-arranged non-recourse debt that can help match old debt without a personal loan application.

Slow financing, a title problem, or a delayed seller can endanger the deadline. That is when a pre-identified, suitable DST backup can matter. If it can close in days, it may let the QI deploy the funds and complete the exchange even after the primary transaction has collapsed.

## Your first-week checklist

If the process starts this week, sequence the work this way:

- Write down the objective: continue investing or not, and active or passive replacement.
- Ask the CPA for a preliminary four-layer tax estimate.
- Interview and engage a QI before signing a sale contract. Review fund security, segregated escrow, bonding, and E&O insurance.
- Ask the CPA to review entity/title, related-party questions, and year-end timing.
- Engage an advisor to create a replacement shortlist, including a fast-closing DST backup if that is suitable.
- Put 1031 cooperation language in the sale documents and confirm QI assignment before closing.
- Once a sale date is known, mark day 45 and day 180 on every calendar and schedule reminders well ahead of both dates.

## The beginner mistakes this roadmap prevents

Most failed beginner exchanges are traceable to a few errors. Selling before engaging a QI can create constructive receipt and is not fixable after the fact. Searching only after closing wastes the pre-sale planning window. Identifying only one candidate removes margin for a failed deal. Forgetting to replace debt can create surprise mortgage boot. And buying a weak replacement only to beat the clock solves a tax problem by creating an investment problem.

There are quieter traps as well. A primary residence does not qualify for a 1031; it belongs under the separate Section 121 exclusion, which may cover up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 for couples. A former home genuinely converted into a long-term rental can become eligible over time. Changing the ownership entity in the middle of the transaction can breach the same-taxpayer rule. A related-party exchange carries a two-year holding requirement. A grounding in [the 1031 exchange basics for first-time investors](1031-exchange-basics-first-time-investors.html) and a properly timed team are more useful than trying to become a tax expert overnight.

## Key takeaways

- Begin before listing; that is the period when the investor controls the clock.
- Engage the QI before closing. Touching the proceeds can kill the exchange.
- Identify a primary and a suitable fast-closing DST backup so one stalled deal does not decide the outcome.
- To pursue full deferral, acquire equal-or-greater value and replace paid-off debt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When should I start planning a 1031 exchange?

Start before listing the relinquished property. Pre-sale weeks are the only time to choose a strategy, assemble the team, and calmly shortlist replacements. Once the sale closes, the 45- and 180-day clocks run without a pause.

### What is the very first step?

Engage a QI before the sale closes. The QI receives the proceeds so the investor never does. Receipt in the investor’s own account disqualifies the exchange and has no after-the-fact fix.

### Do I really need a CPA and an advisor too?

The QI is required. The CPA confirms the numbers, flags individual traps, and files Form 8824. The advisor sources replacements and coordinates the calendar. Their combined cost is generally small compared with the tax a single error can trigger.

### How much tax does a 1031 exchange defer?

Potentially federal capital gains up to 20%, recapture up to 25%, the 3.8% net investment income tax, and state tax. In combination, those layers often exceed a third of gain. The exchange retains the amount in replacement real estate rather than paying it at the sale.

### What are the two deadlines?

There are 45 days after sale to identify replacement property in writing and 180 days after sale to close. They start together, so day-45 identification leaves at most 135 days. Weekends and holidays count; there is no requested extension.

### Can I look for replacement property before I sell?

Yes, and it is prudent. Formal identification follows closing, but research, tours, and shortlisting can happen first. Preparation turns the 45 days into a confirmation period instead of a rushed search.

### What if I cannot find a replacement in time?

Identify a suitable fast-closing DST backup under the 3-property rule before day 45. If the primary transaction breaks later, a pre-identified DST can typically close in days. It is a fallback, not a reason to skip diligence.

### Can I take some cash out and still exchange?

Yes. A partial exchange intentionally keeps cash boot and pays tax on that portion while deferring the balance. Plan it with the CPA so the tax is known rather than accidental.

### Does a 1031 work if I want to stop being a landlord?

It can. A management-intensive property can be exchanged for passive DSTs or triple-net leased real estate, allowing the investor to defer tax while professional operators take over daily operations.

### Can I exchange my primary residence?

No. Section 1031 applies to investment or business property. A primary residence uses Section 121, which may exclude up to $250,000 of gain or $500,000 for couples. A former home converted to a genuine long-term rental can become 1031-eligible over time.

### How many replacement properties can I buy?

More than one, subject to identification rules. The 3-property rule allows up to 3 properties of any value. The 200% rule can allow more as long as their combined value remains below 200% of what was sold. One property can become several passive replacements.

### What happens if I miss a deadline?

Missing the 45-day or 180-day deadline generally fails the exchange and makes the sale fully taxable. There is no grace period or requested extension, except for limited federal disaster relief.

### Do I have to replace my mortgage?

For full deferral, generally yes. Less replacement debt than paid-off debt creates taxable mortgage boot unless cash offsets the difference. A leveraged DST can help match prior debt without a loan application.

### How long does the whole process take?

The formal transaction runs from the sale through replacement closing within 180 days. The preparation that determines whether it works should begin weeks or months before listing.

## Glossary

- **1031 Exchange:** A transaction under IRC Section 1031 that defers tax on investment real estate reinvested in like-kind real estate.
- **Relinquished Property:** The property sold in the exchange.
- **Replacement Property:** The like-kind property acquired to complete it.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party that holds sale proceeds and documents the exchange so the investor does not receive them.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Access to or control over proceeds, which disqualifies the exchange.
- **45-Day Identification Period:** The post-sale window to identify replacements in writing.
- **180-Day Exchange Period:** The post-sale window to close on the replacement.
- **Boot:** Cash or other non-like-kind value received in an exchange and taxable up to the gain.
- **Mortgage Boot:** Taxable gain caused by replacement debt below debt paid off, unless additional cash offsets it.
- **3-Property Rule:** A method allowing identification of up to 3 replacements of any value.
- **200% Rule:** A method allowing any number of replacements with combined value up to 200% of the relinquished property.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A passive, securitized fractional interest in institutional real estate that can qualify as 1031 replacement property.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation deductions, up to 25%, that a 1031 can defer.
- **Partial Exchange:** A transaction in which an investor intentionally retains taxable boot while deferring the rest.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form used to report a like-kind exchange for the year of sale.
- **Same-Taxpayer Rule:** The seller of the relinquished property must also acquire the replacement property.
