# Inside the 721 UPREIT Exchange: Trading Control for Liquidity


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**UPREIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 6 min read**

This week, I have been thinking about the familiar call from an owner who has had enough of one building. A 721 exchange can be the quiet exit at the end of that real-estate journey: contribute a property or DST interest to a REIT operating partnership, receive OP units, and swap direct control for diversification, income, and a possible route to liquidity. The catch is a one-way door.

First-order thinking sees a tax-deferred contribution and an easier life. Second-order thinking asks who controls the capital next, what liquidity actually means, and which choices disappear once the units are accepted. That is the trade worth understanding.

## Key Takeaways

- A 721 exchange contributes property—often a DST interest at the end of its hold—to a REIT operating partnership for OP units, tax-deferred under Section 721.
- You surrender control of one building; the REIT manager decides what the whole portfolio buys, sells, refinances, and distributes.
- In return, you receive exposure across many assets, distributions generally comparable to REIT shares, professional management, and an interest that can divide cleanly among heirs.
- OP units are generally convertible into REIT shares that can be sold in pieces. Conversion is taxable, and selling shares ends deferral on the portion sold.
- It is a one-way move. OP units cannot be 1031 exchanged back into direct real estate.
- A step-up in basis at death may erase the deferred gain for heirs, which is why estate planning often drives the decision.

## What a 721 UPREIT exchange actually is

UPREIT means umbrella partnership real estate investment trust. The label sounds more complicated than the mechanics. Most REITs own a controlling interest in an operating partnership, and that operating partnership owns the buildings. A 721 exchange lets an outside owner contribute property to that partnership and receive partnership units rather than cash. Section 721 generally treats that contribution for a partnership interest as a non-recognition event, so capital-gains tax does not come due at contribution.

In practice, an individual rarely contributes a building held outright. More often, the path starts with a [1031 into a DST](1031-to-721-exchange.html). The investor holds the DST through the sponsor's business plan. When the trust reaches the end of its life, the sponsor may offer a 721 contribution of the DST real estate into an affiliated REIT operating partnership. The investor receives OP units sized to the DST-interest value, and the deferred gain travels from original property to DST to OP units. The [full guide to 721 exchanges](721-exchange-guide.html) covers that structure end to end.

An OP unit is a partnership interest. It is not REIT stock, and it is not a deed to one building. The holder has a pro rata claim on the operating partnership's cash flow and value alongside other unit holders, including the REIT. That shift—from a discrete asset to a fractional interest in a pool—creates the control given up, the diversification received, and the eventual liquidity path.

## The control you give up

Owning a building means making the calls: rents, property manager, refinance date, sale date. After a 721 exchange, those decisions belong to the REIT sponsor. The sponsor runs the portfolio under its strategy and decides which assets to buy or sell, when to add debt, how much to distribute, and what cash stays behind for reserves or acquisitions.

That is an actual loss, not a footnote below the upside. A hands-on owner who likes operating buildings, or believes they can out-execute a professional manager, is relinquishing the thing they do well. The holder cannot ask the sponsor to sell the office tower they dislike or change rents in a single submarket. Once the units are accepted, the holder is passive and depends on management's judgment for income and value.

For someone tired of tenant calls, roof problems, vacancy, and thirty years of operating work, that can be exactly the point. The owner can hand the keys to a professional team and stop managing the property. Still, the exchange is direct control of one asset for a passive portfolio stake the investor does not steer. Going in with that clear is what separates a considered decision from disappointment.

## What you gain in return

The offsetting benefits can be meaningful. First is diversification. One building concentrates capital, income, and risk in one asset, tenant base, and local market. OP units spread exposure across the REIT portfolio, potentially dozens or hundreds of properties, multiple regions, and several property types. One troubled tenant or weak submarket does not have to threaten the entire position.

Second is passive income. The operating partnership distributes cash flow to unit holders. Those distributions are generally comparable to the REIT's shareholder distributions because OP units and REIT shares sit on roughly equal economic footing. The holder receives income without leasing, maintenance, or late-night operating calls.

Third is divisibility. Leaving one building to four children can force a debate about holding, selling, refinancing, or management. OP units can be divided into defined amounts; each heir can receive units and make an independent choice. That clean division, alongside passive income and diversification, is why a 721 contribution can be the last move in a long real-estate career.

## The liquidity path

Direct real estate is illiquid. An owner cannot sell a third of a building to cover a medical bill or a grandchild's tuition. OP units can create an exit path, although it runs through a taxable step. After the holding period specified in the partnership agreement, units are generally convertible into REIT shares, often on a one-for-one or formula basis. Shares can then be sold in increments.

That incremental sale is the practical liquidity benefit. An investor needing $50,000 can convert and sell only enough shares to raise that amount, pay tax only on the converted slice, and leave the remaining position deferred and intact. With direct real estate, the choice is usually the entire building or nothing. Units and shares can turn part of the net worth into a faucet opened a little at a time.

The quality of that faucet depends on the REIT. Public-REIT shares trade on an exchange at the market price of the day. A non-traded REIT uses the sponsor's redemption program instead. Such programs commonly limit quarterly redemptions, may redeem below net asset value, and can gate or suspend requests when many holders want out—often under the same stressed conditions that prompt requests. Liquidity is real, but conditional. Redemption terms deserve reading before an investor assumes cash is available on demand.

## The one-way door

The point often softened in sales conversations is simple: the 721 is a one-way door. Once property has been contributed to the operating partnership for OP units, the holder cannot do a 1031 exchange back into direct real estate. Section 1031 applies to investment real property. A partnership interest is not real property for that purpose, so OP units do not qualify. The decades-long chain of 1031 exchanges ends when the units are accepted.

The 721 contribution does not itself end the deferral. Gain continues inside the OP units. But the ways to keep it deferred narrow to holding the units or holding the REIT shares received after conversion. Conversion recognizes built-in gain, and a share sale is taxable like a stock sale. There is no exchange back into a building to restart the 1031 clock; the 721 is the last exchange, not a stop along the way.

A 1031 into a DST remains reversible in spirit: at the DST sale, the investor may 1031 into another DST or a building and continue. A 721 does not provide that option. The [downsides of a 721 exchange](721-exchange-downsides.html) deserve attention before commitment. After the door closes, the choices are to hold or to sell and pay.

## The typical two-step path

Few investors do a 721 in isolation. The usual sequence begins with a 1031 and ends with a 721. Step one: the investor sells a building and completes a 1031 exchange into a DST, deferring gain in a passive, professionally managed position. Step two, often years later: at the end of the DST hold, the sponsor sells or repositions the underlying property and may offer a 721 contribution into an affiliated REIT operating partnership. The investor receives OP units and continues deferral.

The DST life cycle, not the investor, sets the second step's timing. A DST is finite. When its business plan ends, the trust must address the property. A sponsor may have structured a 721 rollup into its affiliated REIT as one exit. The offering documents set out the timing, and the [DST full cycle](dst-full-cycle.html) explains how these holds wind down. The 721 is available only at the DST's end and only where the sponsor has built an UPREIT exit into the deal.

That sequence should set expectations before purchase. A DST designed for a 721 exit leads toward an eventual one-way move into a REIT, not an endless series of DST-to-DST exchanges. Other DSTs are designed to sell and return capital for another 1031. Knowing which design an investor owns before the purchase prevents a surprise at the end of the hold.

## The estate-planning angle

For many investors, estate planning is the reason to take the final step. Under current law, appreciated property held at death generally receives a basis stepped up to fair market value on the date of death. Deferred gain carried through a chain of 1031 exchanges, into a DST, and then into OP units may be erased for heirs. They receive the units at the stepped-up basis and can sell with little or no capital-gains tax on the earlier appreciation.

The divisibility makes the case stronger. An older owner can hold units, collect passive income for life, and leave separate portions to heirs, each with a fresh basis. There is no single building to fight over and no decades of deferred gain necessarily waiting at the next sale. Lifetime income, clean division, and the step-up are why the 721 can make sense as a final move. [721 exchanges and estate planning](721-exchange-estate-planning.html) explains the mechanics in more depth.

No step-up is guaranteed. It depends on the estate-tax rules in effect at death, and those rules can change. This is fact-specific planning for an attorney and CPA, not a brochure. But the structural fit between a passive, divisible, deferred position and a step-up at death is real, and it is the single most common reason investors accept the one-way nature of the 721 with their eyes open.

## The tax mechanics, plainly

Three tax facts keep surprises to a minimum. First, OP units are partnership interests. The holder receives a Schedule K-1 rather than the 1099-DIV issued to a REIT shareholder. The K-1 reports the holder's share of partnership income, deductions, and other items; it can arrive late in tax season, so the CPA should know it is coming.

Second, exchanging OP units for REIT shares is taxable. Conversion is treated as a disposition of the partnership interest and recognizes deferred built-in gain on the portion converted. That is why some holders convert gradually: they can choose when and how much gain to recognize.

Third, a holder who has REIT shares owes capital-gains tax on a sale, based on the difference between basis and sale price and the applicable long- or short-term holding period. The comparison in [721 versus 1031](721-exchange-vs-1031-exchange.html) is direct: 721 defers; conversion and sale recognize; a step-up at death may erase. The investor's stopping point determines what tax is ultimately paid.

## How to evaluate the destination REIT

Accepting OP units means accepting the REIT portfolio, its debt, and its distribution policy. The destination needs the same scrutiny as any large investment. Four questions matter: What does the portfolio own? How much leverage does it carry? Has it maintained distributions through different markets? And what do the redemption terms actually permit?

| What you keep with direct real estate (1031/DST) | What changes when you move to OP units (721) |
| --- | --- |
| You control one specific building's decisions | The REIT's manager controls a whole portfolio; you are passive |
| Concentrated in one asset, tenant base, and market | Diversified across many properties, regions, and property types |
| Illiquid; you sell the whole asset or none of it | Path to liquidity by converting units to shares and selling in pieces |
| Can keep 1031-ing into new real estate indefinitely | One-way door; cannot 1031 the OP-unit position back out |
| Annual reporting via your own returns | Annual K-1 from the partnership; 1099 once you hold shares |

*Illustrative comparison of a direct-real-estate position versus an OP-unit position. General, not specific to any offering.*

Look at the **portfolio**: the assets, property types, geographies, and whether that is exposure the investor wants. Examine **leverage**, because debt amplifies both return and risk, and that debt becomes indirectly the unit holder's. Review **distribution history** through multiple markets. Then read **redemption terms** most closely; for a non-traded REIT, they set the only realistic exit route. Read the offering documents and the overview of [how these REITs work](reits-guide.html) before signing. In proportion, accepting the units is buying the REIT.

## Who it fits, and who it doesn't

The 721 can fit an older owner who is done managing property, prefers passive income and diversification to a concentrated bet, and is planning around a basis step-up at death. For that owner, the one-way door is the destination. The goal is no longer to trade buildings, but to hold a passive, divisible, deferred position and leave it cleanly to heirs.

It fits poorly for an investor who wants operating control, expects to outperform a sponsor's portfolio, or wants flexibility to 1031 into another building. It also fits poorly for anyone needing immediate liquidity, because a non-traded REIT may gate or suspend redemptions precisely when cash is most wanted. An investor relying on a step-up should confirm current law with a qualified attorney because estate-tax rules can change.

The exchange trades control for diversification, a conditional liquidity path, and an estate-planning fit. It cannot be undone. For the owner at the end of a long real-estate run, that may be a sound trade. For the owner who still wants to steer buildings, it is the wrong tool. These offerings are available only to accredited investors through a private placement memorandum, and the decision belongs with the investor's CPA and attorney, not a sales pitch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a 721 UPREIT exchange?

It is a tax-deferred Section 721 contribution of real estate—often a DST interest at the end of its hold—into a REIT operating partnership for operating-partnership units. Capital-gains tax is not due when the units are received.

### What do I give up in a 721 exchange?

Direct control of a specific building. The holder moves from owning a property to holding units in a partnership managed across a portfolio. The sponsor decides purchases, sales, refinancings, and distributions; the unit holder becomes passive.

### How do OP units provide liquidity?

After an initial holding period, OP units are generally convertible into REIT shares. Shares can be sold in pieces. A public REIT trades on an exchange; a non-traded REIT uses a redemption program that may cap, gate, or suspend redemptions.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange out of OP units later?

No. OP units are partnership interests rather than real property, so they do not qualify for a 1031 exchange. The holder can keep holding, or convert and sell; sale ends deferral on that portion.

### Is converting OP units to REIT shares taxable?

Yes. Conversion is a disposition of the partnership interest and recognizes the deferred built-in gain on the converted portion. A later sale of the shares is also taxable like any stock sale. Gradual conversions can help control timing and amount of recognized gain.

### How does the two-step exchange work?

First, an investor completes a 1031 exchange into a DST and moves into a passive position. Then, at the DST's life-cycle end, a sponsor may offer a 721 contribution into an affiliated REIT operating partnership for OP units, continuing the deferral.

### Why does the estate-planning angle matter?

Under current law, a step-up in basis at death can erase deferred gain for heirs. Units also divide cleanly among heirs in a way a single building does not. The step-up depends on the law in effect at death.

### How should I evaluate the destination REIT?

Treat the decision as buying a proportional share of the entire REIT. Examine the portfolio, leverage, distributions through different markets, and redemption terms. For a non-traded REIT, the redemption terms define the practical liquidity path.

## Glossary

**721 Exchange (UPREIT)**  
A contribution of real property into a REIT's operating partnership in exchange for OP units, tax-deferred under Section 721. UPREIT stands for umbrella partnership real estate investment trust.

**Operating Partnership (OP)**  
The partnership a REIT controls and through which it holds its real estate. Contributors receive units in this partnership rather than shares in the REIT itself.

**OP Units**  
Partnership interests received in a 721 exchange. They share in the operating partnership's cash flow and value and are generally convertible into REIT shares.

**REIT**  
A real estate investment trust that owns a portfolio of properties, typically through an operating partnership, and distributes most of its taxable income to holders.

**Conversion**  
The exchange of OP units for REIT shares after an initial holding period. It is a taxable event that recognizes the previously deferred gain on the converted portion.

**Redemption Program**  
The mechanism a non-traded REIT uses to repurchase shares from holders. Programs commonly cap, gate, or suspend redemptions and may price them below net asset value.

**Two-Step Exchange**  
The common path of a 1031 exchange into a DST followed years later by a 721 contribution of that property into an affiliated REIT's operating partnership.

**Step-Up in Basis**  
The reset of an asset's tax basis to fair market value at the owner's death, which under current law can erase the deferred gain for heirs.

**Section 721**  
The provision of the tax code that treats a contribution of property to a partnership in exchange for a partnership interest as a non-recognition event.
