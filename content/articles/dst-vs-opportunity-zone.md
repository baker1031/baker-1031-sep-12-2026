# DST vs. Opportunity Zone Fund: Which Fits Your Gain?

Comparison · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 12 min read

Both shelter capital gains, but they start from different gains and end in different places. A DST defers a real-estate gain; an Opportunity Zone fund can eliminate tax on a decade of new growth. Here's how to choose.

I keep seeing the DST-versus-QOF question framed as a search for the better tax benefit. That misses the practical decision: each structure accepts a different gain, demands a different holding period, and exposes the investor to a different kind of real estate.

First-order thinking asks which tax label sounds more attractive. Second-order thinking starts with the gain, then tests the 45-day and 180-day clocks, the amount that must be reinvested, the investment's liquidity, and whether its business plan is worth owning on its own.

<p class="lead">The Delaware Statutory Trust and the Opportunity Zone fund are two of the most popular ways to shelter a capital gain, and investors often weigh them against each other. But they are built for different starting points and deliver different endings. The DST is a passive way to defer a real-estate gain through a 1031 exchange. The Opportunity Zone fund accepts any capital gain and can wipe out tax on the fund's future appreciation. The right one depends mostly on what kind of gain you have and how long you can leave the money alone. This memo draws the comparison cleanly, then walks through the 2026 rule changes that now sit on top of the OZ side of the decision.</p>
<div class="takeaways">
<div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>A DST requires a real-estate sale and defers the gain; an Opportunity Zone fund accepts any capital gain, including gains from stock and business sales.</li>
<li>A DST defers tax and continues the 1031 chain; an OZ fund defers the original gain and can eliminate tax on the fund's own appreciation after ten years.</li>
<li>A DST can be exited through another 1031 or a 721 roll-up into a REIT; an OZ fund's headline benefit is locked to a fixed ten-year hold.</li>
<li>The 2026 law made Opportunity Zones permanent starting January 1, 2027, with a rolling five-year deferral and a single 10% basis step-up. Investments made under the original program still recognize their deferred gain on December 31, 2026.</li>
<li>The paperwork differs: a DST reports on a 1099 and grantor letter, while a qualified opportunity fund reports on a K-1 plus Forms 8997 and 8949.</li>
<li>Choose by gain type and horizon. A real-estate gain and a wish for flexibility favor the DST; any gain and a genuine decade favor the OZ fund.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 id="gains">What gains qualify for each</h2>
<p>Start with the gain, because it often decides the matter before any other factor comes into play. A <a href="dst-guide.html">DST</a> is 1031 replacement property, so it requires a <strong>real-estate</strong> sale. You sell investment or business-use real property, follow the 1031 rules, and exchange the proceeds into a fractional interest in the trust. The relinquished asset has to be real estate held for investment, and the trust interest has to be identified within 45 days and closed within 180. If you never sold real estate, you have nothing to exchange, and the DST is simply off the table.</p>
<p>An <a href="opportunity-zones-guide.html">Opportunity Zone fund</a> is far more flexible about the source of the money. It accepts <strong>any capital gain</strong> — from appreciated stock, the sale of a private business, a crypto position, a collectible, or real estate — as long as you invest the gain amount into a qualified opportunity fund within 180 days of the sale. You reinvest only the gain, not the full proceeds, which is the opposite of a 1031 exchange, where you generally roll the entire equity and replace the debt. That single distinction routes a large share of decisions: if your gain is not from real estate, the DST cannot help you and the OZ fund is your route. If it is from real estate, both are on the table and the rest of this comparison applies.</p>
<p>One more qualifying nuance matters for timing. The 1031 clock for a DST starts at the closing of your relinquished property and is unforgiving, with no extensions for weekends or holidays. The OZ clock also runs 180 days, but for gains that flow through a partnership or S corporation the investor can elect to start the window at the entity's year-end, which can buy months of additional planning time. Investors who sit on a flow-through gain often have more room than they realize.</p>
<h2 id="benefit">Deferral versus elimination</h2>
<p>The benefits differ in kind, not just in degree. A DST <strong>defers</strong> your gain. Capital gains tax, depreciation recapture, and the net investment income surtax are all postponed, and you can continue the 1031 chain into another exchange when the DST sells. If you hold the position until death, your heirs receive a step-up in basis and the deferred gain can disappear entirely. The DST's elimination, in other words, is an estate-planning outcome that arrives at the end of a life, not the end of a holding period.</p>
<p>An OZ fund does two distinct things. First, it <strong>defers</strong> your original gain. Second, and this is the headline, it can <strong>eliminate</strong> tax on the fund's own appreciation if you hold the investment for at least ten years. The original deferred gain is still taxed when the deferral period ends, but every dollar the fund earns on top of your investment over that decade can come out free of capital gains tax. So the DST's elimination comes at death through a basis step-up, while the OZ fund's elimination comes from a decade of tax-free growth that you can actually spend. Same word, very different mechanism and timing.</p>
<p>A worked sketch makes the contrast concrete. Sell an investment property for a $1,000,000 gain and exchange into a DST, and you defer roughly $200,000 to $300,000 of combined federal tax, keep earning distributions on the full pre-tax amount, and preserve the option to exchange again later. Take a $1,000,000 stock gain into an OZ fund instead, and you defer that gain to the recognition date, then, if the fund doubles over ten years, the second $1,000,000 of growth can be excluded from tax altogether. The DST keeps more capital working today on a real-estate gain; the OZ fund offers a cleaner path to permanent savings on long-horizon growth.</p>
<h2 id="changes">The 2026 rule changes you can't ignore</h2>
<p>The Opportunity Zone side of this decision changed materially in 2025, and the effects land in 2026 and 2027. The 2026 tax law made the program <strong>permanent</strong> rather than letting it expire, but it reset the mechanics for money invested after the original window. Investments made under the original program still recognize their deferred gain on <strong>December 31, 2026</strong>, so an investor who put a gain into a fund in 2024 or 2025 should plan for that tax bill on schedule. The ten-year appreciation exclusion on those original investments is unaffected and still runs from the date of investment.</p>
<p>For new money, the program restarts on <strong>January 1, 2027</strong> with a rolling structure. A gain invested after that date can be deferred for five years, or until you sell if that comes sooner, rather than to a single fixed calendar deadline. The basis benefit is simplified to a single <strong>10% step-up</strong> at the five-year mark; the extra 5% step-up that the old program granted at seven years is gone. Governors begin nominating a fresh map of census tracts on July 1, 2026, and the new map takes effect January 1, 2027, with a redesignation cycle every ten years thereafter. None of this touches the DST, which runs on the 1031 rules and is not affected by the OZ legislation. The practical takeaway: if you are weighing a 2026 OZ investment, know which version of the rules applies to your dollars, and read our <a href="opportunity-zone-10-year-rule.html">guide to the ten-year rule</a> alongside this memo.</p>
<h2 id="horizon">Horizon, liquidity, and exit</h2>
<p>Both vehicles are illiquid, but their time structures differ in ways that should shape the choice. A DST runs to the sponsor's <strong>full-cycle sale</strong>, typically five to ten years, after which proceeds return to investors. At that exit you have real optionality: take cash and pay the deferred tax, complete another 1031 exchange into a new DST or direct property, or accept a <a href="1031-to-721-exchange.html">721 exchange</a> of your interest into a REIT's operating partnership for further deferral and eventual liquidity. The DST does not hand you a fixed end date you must hit; the sponsor's business plan does, and you keep choices when it concludes.</p>
<p>An OZ fund's economics are tied to a hard <strong>ten-year hold</strong>. Exit before that mark and you forfeit the appreciation exclusion that is the entire reason most investors choose the structure. Some funds offer limited redemption mechanics, but the design assumes patient, locked capital for a full decade. So the DST offers more flexibility to redeploy at exit, while the OZ fund rewards a fixed, uninterrupted commitment. Neither offers meaningful near-term liquidity, and an investor who may need the principal within a few years is a poor fit for either. Treat both as money you can leave invested for years and possibly a decade.</p>
<h2 id="risk">Risk profiles</h2>
<p>The underlying investments tend to sit at different points on the risk spectrum. DSTs are frequently <strong>stabilized, income-producing properties</strong> — apartments, industrial, medical office, net-lease retail — bought already leased to generate monthly distributions. OZ funds, by contrast, often involve <strong>ground-up development or substantial redevelopment</strong> in emerging areas, because the rules effectively require the fund to improve the property. That development orientation means higher potential return paired with higher execution and market risk: construction can run over budget, lease-up can stall, and an emerging submarket can stay emerging longer than the model assumed.</p>
<p>That makes the OZ fund the more aggressive of the two on the real-estate side, suited to investors who are comfortable with development risk in exchange for the elimination benefit. The DST leans toward current income and relative stability, though it remains illiquid, sponsor-dependent, and exposed to the same interest-rate and property-market forces as any commercial real estate. Neither is low risk. Both are private placements that can lose principal. The honest framing is that you are choosing between two distinct risk profiles, not between a safe option and a risky one, and the tax benefit should never be the only reason you accept the underlying business plan.</p>
<h2 id="reporting">Reporting and paperwork</h2>
<p>The tax forms each vehicle generates are easy to overlook until filing season, and they differ in a way that surprises investors. A DST is treated as a grantor trust, so your share of income, deductions, and depreciation is reported to you on a <strong>1099 and an accompanying grantor letter</strong>, not a Schedule K-1. That grantor-trust treatment is the same feature that makes a DST eligible for 1031 exchange under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, so the reporting and the tax benefit are two sides of one structure.</p>
<p>A qualified opportunity fund is usually a partnership, so it issues a <strong>Schedule K-1</strong>, and the investor also files <strong>Form 8997</strong> each year to report holdings in the fund and <strong>Form 8949</strong> to elect deferral of the original gain. The OZ paperwork is heavier and runs for the life of the investment, while the DST's is lighter and more familiar to anyone who has owned rental property. Our <a href="real-estate-tax-forms.html">guide to real-estate tax forms</a> walks through each document, and either way you should give your CPA a heads-up in the year you invest so the elections are made correctly and on time.</p>
<h2 id="which">Which fits your situation</h2>
<p>Reduce the decision to two questions. <strong>What kind of gain do you have?</strong> If it is not real estate, the OZ fund is your tool, because the DST simply will not accept it. <strong>How long can you commit, and what do you want from the money?</strong> A real-estate gain, a desire for current income, and a wish to preserve 1031 flexibility point to the <strong>DST</strong>. Any gain, a true ten-year horizon, and an appetite for development-style upside point to the <strong>Opportunity Zone fund</strong>.</p>
<p>Three quick profiles show how the questions resolve in practice. A retiring landlord selling a fully depreciated rental, who wants to stop managing tenants but keep income and avoid a large recapture bill, is a classic <strong>DST</strong> case: the gain is real estate, the goal is passive income, and the 1031 chain plus a step-up at death can carry the deferral for life. A founder who just sold a company for a large capital gain and has no real estate to exchange is an <strong>OZ fund</strong> case by default, because the DST cannot accept the gain and the ten-year exclusion rewards the long horizon a younger seller can accept. An investor who sold both an apartment building and a block of appreciated stock in the same year can run <strong>both</strong> in parallel, sending the property proceeds into a DST and the stock gain into an OZ fund. The point is that the gain type usually assigns the tool before personal preference enters.</p>
<p>Plenty of investors use both in the same year, and the two do not compete for the same dollars. A common pattern: roll the proceeds of an investment-property sale into a DST, and place a separate stock or business gain into an OZ fund, letting each tool handle the gain it was built for. Our <a href="tax-deferral-strategies-compared.html">full strategy comparison</a> places these two alongside the straight 1031 exchange and the 721 roll-up, so you can see all four routes side by side. Whichever you choose, model the after-tax outcome with your CPA before you commit, and read the offering's private placement memorandum in full, because the underlying real estate, not the tax label, is what you actually own.</p>
<h2 id="mistakes">Common mistakes to avoid</h2>
<p>A handful of errors come up again and again, and most are about timing or expectations rather than the tax law itself. The first is <strong>missing the window</strong>. A DST exchange has to clear the 45-day identification and 180-day closing deadlines, and an OZ investment has to be funded within 180 days of the gain. Both clocks are firm, and a missed date converts a tax-deferred plan into a taxable sale. Line up the vehicle before you close the sale, not after.</p>
<p>The second is <strong>reinvesting the wrong amount</strong>. A 1031 exchange into a DST generally requires you to roll your entire equity and replace the debt you paid off; hold cash back and that portion becomes taxable boot. An OZ fund is the reverse: you reinvest only the gain, and putting in the full proceeds does not buy you a larger benefit. Confusing the two leads to either an unexpected tax bill or idle capital.</p>
<p>The third is <strong>treating the ten-year OZ hold as flexible</strong>. The appreciation exclusion is all-or-nothing at the ten-year mark, so an OZ fund is a poor home for money you might need in year six. The fourth is <strong>buying the tax benefit and ignoring the real estate</strong>. A DST tied to an over-leveraged property or an OZ fund built on an unrealistic development pro forma can lose money regardless of the tax treatment. The deferral is worthless if the underlying asset underperforms. Read the private placement memorandum, weigh the sponsor's track record, and make sure the investment would stand on its own even without the tax advantage.</p>
<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<div class="faq">
<details open="">
<summary>What's the main difference between a DST and an Opportunity Zone fund?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>A DST requires a real-estate sale and defers the gain through a 1031 exchange. An Opportunity Zone fund accepts any capital gain and can also eliminate tax on the fund's appreciation after a ten-year hold. The DST is built for real-estate proceeds; the OZ fund is built for any gain you want to grow tax-free over a decade.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I use a DST for a stock or business gain?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>No. A DST is 1031 replacement property and requires a real-estate sale to fund the exchange. For a stock or business gain, an Opportunity Zone fund is the available tool, since it accepts any capital gain reinvested within 180 days.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Which one eliminates tax, not just defers it?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>The Opportunity Zone fund can eliminate tax on the fund's appreciation after ten years, though the original deferred gain is still taxed when the deferral period ends. A DST defers; its tax elimination comes only from a step-up in basis if the position is held until death.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>How did the 2026 law change Opportunity Zones?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>The program was made permanent starting January 1, 2027, with a rolling five-year deferral and a single 10% basis step-up at year five. Investments made under the original program still recognize their deferred gain on December 31, 2026. The changes do not affect DSTs, which run on the 1031 rules.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Which is riskier?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Often the Opportunity Zone fund, because many OZ deals involve ground-up development with higher execution and market risk. DSTs are frequently stabilized, income-focused properties, though both are illiquid private placements that can lose principal.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Do they report on different tax forms?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Yes. A DST reports on a 1099 and grantor letter because it is a grantor trust. A qualified opportunity fund usually issues a Schedule K-1, and the investor also files Form 8997 annually and Form 8949 to elect deferral of the original gain.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I use both?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Yes. Many investors use a DST for real-estate sale proceeds and an Opportunity Zone fund for a separate stock or business gain in the same year. The two vehicles do not compete for the same dollars, so each can handle the gain it fits.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Which has better income, a DST or a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>They are built for different things. A DST typically holds stabilized, income-producing real estate and is designed to pay regular distributions from day one — income is the point. A Qualified Opportunity Fund is usually a development-and-operate strategy whose payoff is tax-free <em>appreciation</em> after ten years; current income is often modest or absent in the early years while the project is being built and leased up. So a DST generally suits an investor who needs cash flow now, while a QOF suits one who can forgo near-term income in exchange for tax-free growth. Neither's distributions are guaranteed.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>What is "tax-free growth" in an Opportunity Zone fund?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>It's the QOF's signature benefit: if you hold your fund interest for at least ten years, you can elect to step up its basis to fair market value at sale, so the appreciation earned inside the fund escapes capital-gains tax entirely. Note this applies only to the fund's <em>growth</em> — the original deferred gain you rolled in is still recognized at the program's recognition date. A DST has no equivalent appreciation exclusion; its tax advantage is continued 1031 deferral and, if held until death, a step-up that erases the deferred gain for heirs.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Which is better for estate planning, a DST or a QOF?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Both interact with the step-up in basis at death, but differently. A DST acquired through a 1031 lets you keep deferring; held until death, the step-up can erase the entire deferred gain for heirs ("swap till you drop"). A QOF's original deferred gain is recognized at the program's recognition date regardless, but its post-ten-year appreciation is already tax-free, and the interest also receives a step-up at death on its own basis. For erasing a deferred real-estate gain at death, the DST path is generally cleaner; for tax-free growth on a non-real-estate gain, the QOF is the tool.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I split one sale between a DST and an OZ fund?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>For a single real-estate sale, you generally choose one track for a given dollar of gain — you can't claim both 1031 deferral (into a DST) and OZ benefits on the same gain. However, you can allocate: route part of the proceeds through a 1031 into a DST and elect to invest a different portion of the gain into a QOF (within the 180-day window), since a partial 1031 leaves a taxable gain that could itself be eligible for OZ deferral. This is technical and fact-specific — confirm the structure with your CPA before relying on it.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Can I move from a DST into an OZ fund later, or vice versa?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Not as a tax-free swap. When a DST goes full-cycle, the gain can be deferred again via another 1031 (into another DST or property) or rolled into a REIT through a 721 exchange — but moving those proceeds into a QOF would be a new, separate election based on the realized gain, not a continuation. There is no like-kind exchange between a DST and a QOF. Each is its own track, chosen at the time you have an eligible gain to place.</p></div>
</details>
<details>
<summary>Do DSTs and OZ funds have different minimum investments?<span class="ic"></span></summary>
<div class="ans"><p>Both are accredited-investor private placements and both typically carry minimums, but they vary by sponsor. DST minimums commonly start around $25,000–$100,000, sized to fit fractional 1031 exchanges. QOF minimums are set by the fund and can run higher, often $50,000–$250,000 or more, reflecting their development orientation. Confirm the specific minimum, fees, and terms in each offering's documents before investing.</p></div>
</details>
</div>
<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)</dt><dd>A passive, 1031-eligible fractional interest in institutional real estate that defers a real-estate capital gain.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Opportunity Zone Fund (QOF)</dt><dd>A qualified opportunity fund that accepts any capital gain, defers it, and can eliminate tax on the fund's appreciation after a ten-year hold.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>180-Day Window</dt><dd>The period to reinvest a gain — into a DST exchange or a qualified opportunity fund — measured from the triggering sale (or, for flow-through OZ gains, from the entity's year-end).</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Ten-Year Hold</dt><dd>The minimum holding period required to claim the Opportunity Zone appreciation-exclusion benefit.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Basis Step-Up</dt><dd>An adjustment to an asset's tax basis. At death it can erase a DST's deferred gain for heirs; under OZ rules it reduces the deferred gain by 10% at year five.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Full-Cycle</dt><dd>The point at which a DST's property is sold and proceeds, plus any gain, are returned to investors.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Grantor Letter</dt><dd>The annual tax statement a DST issues alongside a 1099, reflecting grantor-trust treatment, in place of a partnership K-1.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Eligible Gain</dt><dd>The gain each tool can accept — a DST takes only a real-property gain via a 1031, while a QOF accepts any capital gain.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Tax-Free Growth</dt><dd>The QOF benefit allowing appreciation earned over a ten-year hold to be excluded from capital-gains tax; a DST has no equivalent.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Ten-Year Exclusion</dt><dd>The election, after a ten-year QOF hold, to step the fund interest up to fair market value so its appreciation escapes tax.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Development Risk</dt><dd>The execution risk of a QOF's build-and-lease strategy, distinct from a DST's stabilized, income-producing properties.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Qualified Intermediary (QI)</dt><dd>The independent party that holds 1031 proceeds for a DST exchange; a QOF investment uses no QI.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Accredited Investor</dt><dd>The investor-qualification standard both DSTs and most QOFs require, since both are private placements under Regulation D.</dd></div>
</dl>
