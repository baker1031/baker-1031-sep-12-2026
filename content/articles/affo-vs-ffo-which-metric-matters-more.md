# AFFO vs. FFO: Which Metric Matters More?

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing the same REIT screen: yield, price, and a P/FFO multiple lined up neatly in a few columns. It is a useful beginning. It is also where the harder work starts.

FFO and AFFO are the two central REIT cash-flow measures, but they are not competing answers to the same question. FFO is the common language for recurring operating performance and comparison. AFFO attempts to answer a more practical question: after the recurring cash required to keep properties competitive, how much cash may actually support the dividend?

First-order thinking says that a high FFO number and an apparently reasonable payout ratio settle the matter. Second-order thinking asks what must be reinvested to sustain that FFO, how the issuer defines the adjustments, and whether the dividend is covered by cash rather than by a friendly accounting presentation. That distinction matters most when a yield looks inviting.

This is educational information, not investment advice. Verify current details and each REIT's own definitions before relying on them.

## What AFFO Adjusts For

**FFO**, or Funds From Operations, starts with net income, adds back non-cash real-estate depreciation and amortization, and removes one-time property-sale gains. That makes it a useful measure of recurring operating performance.

But FFO leaves two important matters unresolved. A property portfolio needs recurring reinvestment to remain competitive and leased, and reported income can include non-cash items. **AFFO**, or Adjusted Funds From Operations, begins with FFO and subtracts those remaining items to get closer to distributable cash.

A workable shorthand is:

> **AFFO = FFO − recurring maintenance capex − straight-line-rent adjustments − other company-specific normalizations**

Other normalizations can include non-cash compensation, leasing costs, capitalized interest, or amortization of financing items. The exact definition varies by REIT. The central point does not: AFFO tries to remove recurring reinvestment and non-cash income that can make FFO look larger than the cash truly available to shareholders.

## Maintenance Capex and Straight-Line Rent

The two core AFFO adjustments explain much of the gap between FFO and AFFO.

**Maintenance capital expenditures** are the recurring cash a REIT must spend to keep its existing properties functioning, competitive, and leased. Think roofs, HVAC systems, parking lots, tenant improvements, and leasing commissions. These are real cash outflows. FFO ignores them; AFFO subtracts them. That is usually the largest reason AFFO comes in below FFO.

**Straight-line rent** is a GAAP convention for leases with scheduled rent increases. GAAP records average rent evenly over the life of the lease instead of recognizing the actual cash received in each period. In the early years, reported rent can exceed cash received; later, it can be lower. FFO includes that non-cash smoothing. AFFO removes it to bring the measure closer to the rent actually collected.

FFO can act as though the roof never needs replacing and rent already earned on paper has arrived in the bank. AFFO subtracts the upkeep and strips out the non-cash rent adjustment. That is why it usually lands closer to the cash available for a dividend.

## Why AFFO Tracks Dividends

The dividend is paid with cash, not with an accounting label. Because AFFO subtracts recurring maintenance capex and non-cash items, it is often a better estimate of the cash available to distribute than FFO.

That makes the **AFFO payout ratio**—dividends paid divided by AFFO—a more meaningful measure of dividend sustainability than the FFO payout ratio. A ratio comfortably below **100%**, often in the **70s** or **80s**, can indicate coverage and a cushion for reinvestment or a downturn. A ratio approaching or exceeding **100%** means the REIT is paying out most or all of its distributable cash, or more. The risk is a dividend cut or reliance on borrowing or asset sales to keep the payout intact.

The ratio is not a verdict by itself. It is a prompt to ask where the cash came from and whether the adjustments are credible.

## FFO vs. AFFO in Practice

FFO and AFFO have a useful division of labor.

FFO is more standardized. Nareit defines it, so FFO is calculated on a broadly consistent basis and works well for comparing REITs' growth, FFO per share, and valuation through the P/FFO multiple. It is the common denominator when several issuers are on the screen.

AFFO is more economically focused on cash, but less standardized. There is no single mandated AFFO definition. Companies can differ in how they treat maintenance capex, straight-line rent, non-cash compensation, capitalized interest, leasing costs, and other normalizations. The maintenance-versus-growth-capex distinction involves genuine judgment: keeping a property competitive is not always cleanly separable from improving it.

So use FFO for standardized comparison and valuation. Use AFFO to assess a particular REIT's dividend coverage and cash generation. Then read the reconciliation rather than accepting either label as self-explanatory.

### Key takeaways

- AFFO refines FFO by subtracting recurring maintenance capex, straight-line-rent adjustments, and other normalizations.
- Maintenance capex is real property reinvestment that FFO ignores and is commonly the largest reason AFFO is lower.
- The AFFO payout ratio is generally more useful than the FFO payout ratio when testing dividend sustainability.
- FFO is more comparable across REITs; AFFO is closer to economic cash but needs closer review. Use both.

## AFFO's Limitations and the Work of Scrutiny

AFFO's advantage is also its vulnerability. Because it is not standardized, it can be presented more generously than the economics justify.

The important judgment is maintenance capex. Capital spent to keep a property competitive should reduce AFFO. Capital classified as growth or value-add may not. The line can be blurry, and a REIT that counts too little spending as maintenance can report higher AFFO and a more comfortable-looking payout ratio than reality warrants.

Other adjustments vary as well: straight-line rent, non-cash compensation, capitalized interest, and leasing costs can all be treated differently. No enforcement body mandates a single approach.

The practical defense is plain:

- Read the REIT's reconciliation from net income to FFO and AFFO.
- See precisely how management defines maintenance capex.
- Compare capex intensity with sector norms and comparable REITs.
- Be cautious when AFFO looks unusually generous.

AFFO is only as useful as the judgment beneath it. Read the fine print, not just the headline number.

## Using Both Metrics

The disciplined workflow starts with FFO. Compare FFO per share and P/FFO across the REIT group to see growth and valuation on a common basis.

Then move to AFFO. Review AFFO, the AFFO payout ratio, and the reconciliation that shows how the issuer got there. Test whether maintenance capex and the other normalizations make economic sense.

Finally, add the measures neither FFO nor AFFO can replace: balance-sheet leverage, debt maturities, NAV, occupancy, same-store growth, and the operating conditions of the underlying real estate. FFO and AFFO are complementary. A decision based on only one leaves part of the picture out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is AFFO?

AFFO stands for Adjusted Funds From Operations. It takes FFO and subtracts recurring maintenance capex, straight-line-rent adjustments, and often other company-specific normalizations, producing a closer estimate of distributable cash. FFO already adjusts net income for real-estate depreciation and sale gains, but it can still overstate available cash. AFFO is especially helpful for testing dividend sustainability, though its definitions vary by company.

### What is the difference between AFFO and FFO?

FFO begins with net income, adds back real-estate depreciation and amortization, and removes property-sale gains. AFFO builds on FFO by deducting maintenance capex, straight-line rent, and other adjustments. FFO is more standardized and suitable for peer comparison and P/FFO valuation. AFFO is more focused on distributable cash and dividend coverage.

### Which is more important, FFO or AFFO?

Neither is universally more important. For peer growth and valuation, FFO matters more because it is broadly comparable. For dividend safety and true cash generation, AFFO matters more because it accounts for upkeep and non-cash income. Use both rather than treating one as universally superior.

### What does AFFO adjust for?

The two core adjustments are recurring maintenance capex and straight-line rent. AFFO can also adjust non-cash compensation, capitalized interest, leasing costs, and amortization of financing costs. These adjustments remove recurring reinvestment and non-cash items from FFO.

### What is maintenance capex?

Maintenance capex is the ongoing spending needed to keep existing properties competitive, functional, and leased. It can include roofs, HVAC, parking lots, tenant improvements, and leasing commissions. It differs from growth or value-add capital used to expand or substantially improve a property. The distinction requires judgment, which is why a REIT's definition deserves attention.

### What is straight-line rent?

Straight-line rent is the GAAP practice of recognizing the average scheduled rent over a lease term rather than the cash rent received each period. Early in a lease, reported rent can exceed cash collected; later, it can fall below cash. AFFO removes the adjustment so the measure is closer to actual cash rent.

### Why does AFFO track dividends better than FFO?

AFFO better approximates the cash that funds a dividend because it deducts upkeep and non-cash items that FFO leaves in. An FFO payout ratio can appear comfortable while real cash is tighter. A near-or-over-**100%** AFFO payout ratio can signal a possible cut or dependence on borrowing or asset sales.

### What is the AFFO payout ratio?

It is dividends paid divided by AFFO, expressed as a percentage. A ratio below **100%**, often in the **70s** or **80s**, may show a cushion; a ratio near or above **100%** raises questions about coverage. The result still depends on how the REIT calculated AFFO.

### Why isn't AFFO standardized?

Unlike FFO, AFFO has no single authoritative definition. Maintenance-capex classification is the biggest area of variation, but straight-line rent, non-cash compensation, capitalized interest, and leasing costs can differ too. That is the cost of its cash focus: greater economic specificity with less comparability.

### How do I use FFO and AFFO together?

Start with FFO per share and P/FFO to compare REITs. Then study AFFO and the AFFO payout ratio, read the reconciliation, and inspect the maintenance-capex definition. Complete the work with leverage, debt maturities, NAV, occupancy, and same-store growth.

### Is AFFO always lower than FFO?

AFFO is almost always lower because it subtracts maintenance capex, removes straight-line rent, and may make other deductions. A large gap can point to a capital-intensive property type; a small gap can indicate less recurring reinvestment. Unusual periods or definitions can net differently, so the gap is a signal to investigate, not a conclusion.

### Can a REIT manipulate its AFFO?

A REIT can present AFFO favorably by treating more capital as growth rather than maintenance or by selecting favorable normalizations. That does not necessarily mean fraud; it can reflect valid judgment differences. Read the reconciliation, compare maintenance spending with peers, and question an unusually generous result.

### Do non-traded REITs report FFO and AFFO too?

Yes. Non-traded REITs commonly report FFO, AFFO, per-share figures, and reconciliations in periodic reports and offering materials. Because they are illiquid and priced at periodic NAV rather than continuously in the market, AFFO coverage can be especially helpful when assessing whether distributions are supported by cash flow rather than return of capital or borrowing. Review fees, redemption terms, and the underlying real estate as well.

### What does the gap between FFO and AFFO tell me?

The gap shows how much reported operating performance may need to be reinvested to sustain the portfolio. Net-lease REITs, where tenants often cover maintenance and improvements, can have smaller gaps. Office REITs, which may fund substantial tenant improvements and leasing commissions, can have larger gaps. The gap is a useful view of capital intensity and cash conversion.

## Glossary

- **AFFO:** Adjusted Funds From Operations—FFO refined into a closer estimate of distributable cash.
- **FFO:** Funds From Operations—the standardized REIT operating measure on which AFFO builds.
- **Maintenance Capex:** Recurring property reinvestment FFO ignores but AFFO subtracts.
- **Growth Capex:** Capital to expand or improve property, often not subtracted in AFFO.
- **Straight-Line Rent:** A non-cash GAAP convention averaging scheduled rent, removed in AFFO.
- **Distributable Cash:** The genuine cash a REIT can pay out, which AFFO approximates.
- **AFFO Payout Ratio:** Dividends divided by AFFO—the key dividend-sustainability gauge.
- **FFO Payout Ratio:** Dividends divided by FFO—a looser measure than the AFFO version.
- **Nareit:** The body that standardizes FFO, but not AFFO.
- **P/FFO:** Price-to-FFO multiple—the standardized REIT valuation gauge.
- **Standardization:** FFO's consistent definition, which AFFO lacks.
- **Normalization:** Company-specific AFFO adjustments beyond capex and rent.
- **Reconciliation:** The disclosure showing how a REIT derives FFO and AFFO.
- **Dividend Coverage:** Whether distributable cash, approximated by AFFO, supports the dividend.
- **Capex Intensity:** How much a REIT must reinvest, signaled by the FFO-to-AFFO gap.
- **Non-Cash Items:** Accounting entries, such as straight-line rent, that AFFO removes for cash accuracy.

## FFO vs. AFFO at a Glance

| Factor | FFO | AFFO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full name | Funds From Operations | Adjusted Funds From Operations |
| Starts from | Net income, adding back depreciation | FFO, then further adjustments |
| Maintenance capex | Not subtracted | Subtracted—reflects upkeep cost |
| Straight-line rent | Included | Normalized out |
| Standardization | Defined by Nareit; widely reported | No single standard; varies by REIT |
| Tracks dividends | Approximately | More closely—closer to true distributable cash |
| Best for | Quick, comparable screening | Judging dividend sustainability |

## Which Should You Choose?

Use FFO for quick, apples-to-apples comparisons across REITs, because it follows a common Nareit definition and is widely reported.

Lean on AFFO when judging whether a dividend is truly covered. It subtracts recurring maintenance capex and normalizes the accounting items FFO leaves in. Before comparing it across REITs, confirm how each issuer calculates it.
