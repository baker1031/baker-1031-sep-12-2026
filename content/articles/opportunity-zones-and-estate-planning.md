# Opportunity Zones and Estate Planning

[Home](baker1031.html)

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

Opportunity Zone Funds · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 17 min read

I keep seeing long-hold investments described as though the holding period belongs to one person and one calendar. It does not. First-order thinking treats a death as a clean reset; second-order thinking asks what survives, which tax clock keeps running, and whether the estate has cash when the bill arrives.

<div class="article-intro"><p style="margin:0;">Opportunity Zone investments raise important and nuanced estate-planning questions. This guide explains how QOF interests pass to heirs, what happens to the deferred gain (which is income in respect of a decedent), the basis rules at death, coordinating with the 10-year hold, and estate-planning strategies — all to be confirmed with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div>

<article class="article">
<p class="lead">An Opportunity Zone investment is a <a href="who-should-invest-in-opportunity-zone-funds.html">long-term commitment</a>, so it is natural to ask what happens to it in your estate plan. Death can intersect with the deferred original gain and the 10-year hold in surprising ways. QOF interests pass to heirs like other property, but the deferred original gain is income in respect of a decedent (IRD), so it generally is not eliminated by death: the heirs or estate still recognize it at the recognition date. Heirs can generally continue the holding period toward the 10-year mark and the tax-free exclusion. Basis at death is nuanced because the deferred gain is IRD. Coordinate these complex, evolving rules with your estate attorney and CPA; this is educational information, not tax or legal advice.</p>

<h2 id="passing-to-heirs">Passing QOF interests to heirs</h2>
<p>A QOF interest is property that can pass through your estate plan like other investments. It may be held in your name, a trust, an entity, or another structure and generally passes to heirs or beneficiaries under your will, trust, or beneficiary designations. It is part of your estate.</p>
<p>Importantly, transfer at death is generally not a taxable disposition—an inclusion event—that accelerates deferred gain during life. Death itself does not trigger an immediate sale. Heirs step into the QOF interest and can generally continue to hold it.</p>

<h2 id="deferred-gain-at-death">What happens to deferred gain</h2>
<p>The central nuance is that the <a href="opportunity-zone-case-study-deferring-a-500k-gain.html">original capital gain you postponed when you invested in the QOF</a> is generally not eliminated at death. It is income in respect of a decedent (IRD): income the decedent earned or deferred but had not recognized. It carries to the estate or heirs, who generally recognize it at the applicable recognition date.</p>
<p>Unlike many assets whose built-in gain may be addressed by a <a href="1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html">step-up in basis</a>, deferred OZ gain is IRD. The heirs or estate generally still owe tax at the recognition date—December 31, 2026 for OZ 1.0, or the rolling 5-year date for OZ 2.0. Death is not an escape hatch for the deferred OZ gain.</p>
<div class="pullquote"><p>Death does not erase the deferred Opportunity Zone gain. Because it is income in respect of a decedent, the heirs or estate generally still owe the tax on that original gain at the recognition date.</p></div>

<h2 id="basis-at-death">Basis rules at death</h2>
<p>QOF basis at death is nuanced because deferred gain is IRD. Inherited property generally receives a basis step-up to fair market value under Section 1014, eliminating built-in gain. That step-up does not apply to the IRD portion. The deferred OZ gain therefore does not receive a basis step-up that erases it.</p>
<p>The interest can pass to heirs, but the normal favorable step-up is limited for deferred gain; that IRD remains taxable. Inherited basis, IRD, OZ-specific basis rules—including a future 10-year step-up—and any deduction for estate tax attributable to IRD require careful analysis. Confirm the precise treatment with your CPA and attorney.</p>

<h2 id="coordinating-10-year">Coordinating with the 10-year hold</h2>
<p>There is a favorable side. Heirs can generally continue toward the 10-year mark. The holding period carries over from the original investment date rather than restarting. If an investor had held for 7 years at death, heirs can generally hold the remaining time to reach 10 years and qualify for the exclusion on appreciation.</p>
<p>The tax-free exclusion of a QOF investment's appreciation after a 10-year hold can therefore remain available even though deferred original gain is not eliminated. Heirs can complete the hold and elect the basis step-up to fair market value at sale for the appreciation. Death does not forfeit the exclusion.</p>
<div class="takeaways"><div class="tk-h">Key Takeaways</div>
<ul>
<li>QOF interests pass to heirs through your estate plan, and death generally is not a taxable disposition of the investment.</li>
<li>The deferred original gain is income in respect of a decedent (IRD)—not eliminated by death; heirs or the estate still recognize it at the recognition date.</li>
<li>Basis at death is nuanced: the normal Section 1014 step-up generally does not erase the IRD (deferred gain).</li>
<li>Heirs can generally continue the holding period toward the 10-year mark and still achieve the tax-free exclusion on the appreciation.</li>
</ul></div>

<h2 id="estate-strategies">Estate-planning strategies</h2>
<p>Several strategies can apply, all designed with your estate attorney and CPA. A QOF interest in a trust—such as a revocable living trust or, for advanced planning, an irrevocable trust—can help with probate avoidance, control, and orderly transfer to heirs who can continue the hold. A lifetime gift is possible but is generally <a href="opportunity-zone-exit-strategies.html">an inclusion event that accelerates the deferred gain</a>, so it is usually not a way to avoid IRD.</p>
<p>Plan for IRD by arranging liquidity for the heirs' eventual tax and considering any estate-tax deduction for IRD. Coordinate the 10-year hold across generations and integrate the QOF with trusts, entities, the estate-tax picture, and overall liquidity. This needs professional design.</p>

<h2 id="common-misconceptions">Common misconceptions</h2>
<p>Do not assume death eliminates deferred OZ gain. It does not: IRD survives death and the heirs or estate generally owe the tax. Nor does Section 1014 erase the IRD portion. Heirs generally do not restart the 10-year clock, and a lifetime gift is generally not a tax-free workaround because it accelerates the gain. The rules are nuanced and evolving, especially after OZ 2.0.</p>
<div class="pullquote"><p>The favorable half of the story is that heirs inherit your holding period — they continue toward the ten-year mark rather than starting over, keeping the tax-free appreciation benefit alive.</p></div>

<div class="faq">
<details open=""><summary>What happens to my Opportunity Zone investment when I die?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Your QOF interest passes to heirs through a will, trust, or beneficiary designation. Death is generally not an inclusion event that accelerates deferred gain during life, so heirs can generally continue holding it. The deferred original gain is IRD and is not eliminated by death; heirs or the estate generally recognize and pay tax at the recognition date. The holding period generally carries over, so heirs can still pursue the 10-year exclusion on appreciation. Coordinate these rules with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Does death eliminate the deferred Opportunity Zone gain?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>No. Deferred original gain is income in respect of a decedent (IRD), which is not wiped out at death. It carries to heirs or the estate and is generally recognized at the recognition date: December 31, 2026 for OZ 1.0 or the rolling 5-year date for OZ 2.0. Plan for the tax and confirm the mechanics with your CPA and estate attorney.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>What is income in respect of a decedent (IRD)?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>IRD is income a deceased person earned or was entitled to but had not recognized for tax purposes at death, such as unpaid wages, certain retirement-account distributions, and deferred gains. It does not receive the favorable basis step-up that erases it; it carries to the estate or heirs. For Opportunity Zones, deferred original gain is IRD. Heirs may be entitled to an income-tax deduction for estate tax attributable to the IRD.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Do my heirs get a basis step-up on the QOF interest?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Basis rules are nuanced. Inherited property generally gets a Section 1014 step-up to fair market value, but the step-up does not apply to the IRD portion. Because deferred OZ gain is IRD, heirs do not get a full step-up that wipes it out. Inherited basis, IRD, and OZ-specific rules, including the future 10-year step-up, require careful analysis; an IRD estate-tax deduction may apply.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Can my heirs still get the 10-year tax-free exclusion?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Generally, yes. The holding period generally carries over from the original investment date rather than restarting. If you held for 7 years at death, heirs can generally complete the remaining period to 10 years and elect the basis step-up to fair market value at sale for the appreciation. The deferred original gain remains taxable, but death does not forfeit the exclusion. Confirm the carryover and election with your CPA and attorney.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Should I hold my QOF interest in a trust?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Possibly. A revocable living trust or, for advanced planning, certain irrevocable trusts can aid probate avoidance, transfer control, and continued holding. The appropriate structure depends on your estate plan, goals, and tax situation. Some trust transfers can be inclusion events that accelerate deferred gain, while others, such as a revocable living trust during life, generally are not. Design it with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Can I gift my QOF interest to my children during my lifetime?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>You can, but carefully. A lifetime gift is generally an inclusion event that accelerates deferred original gain, meaning the donor recognizes and pays tax at the time of gift. Certain transfers, including to a grantor trust, may be treated differently, but lifetime gifting generally accelerates rather than extends deferral. Have your estate attorney and CPA determine whether the transfer is an inclusion event.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>What estate-planning strategies apply to QOF interests?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Strategies include trust ownership for probate avoidance, control, and orderly transfer; IRD planning and liquidity for heirs' deferred-gain tax; coordination of the 10-year hold across generations; and integration with trusts, entities, estate tax, and overall liquidity. Lifetime gifts need care because they generally accelerate deferred gain. Design the plan with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Is the OZ deferred gain subject to estate tax too?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>The QOF interest is part of the taxable estate, so its value can face estate tax when the estate exceeds the applicable exemption. Separately, IRD is subject to income tax when recognized. The same asset can therefore face estate tax on value and income tax on deferred gain; an income-tax deduction for estate tax attributable to IRD can reduce overlap. Coordinate the estate-tax, income-tax, and liquidity planning with your professionals.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Do my heirs have to recognize the deferred gain even if they keep the investment?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Generally, yes. Deferred original gain is recognized at the recognition date—December 31, 2026 for OZ 1.0 or the rolling 5-year date for OZ 2.0—or at an earlier inclusion event, whether or not heirs retain the QOF. Holding preserves the path to the 10-year exclusion on appreciation; it does not avoid the deferred-gain tax. Plan for that tax while continuing to hold.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>How do the OZ 2.0 changes affect estate planning?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>The 2025 OZ 2.0 legislation made the program permanent and changed deferral for post-2026 investments to a rolling 5 years from investment, rather than the fixed December 31, 2026 OZ 1.0 recognition date. That changes the timing of IRD recognition for heirs or the estate. Core issues—IRD, holding-period carryover, and basis—generally remain, but timing and specifics differ by program. Confirm the current treatment with your professionals.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>What's the biggest estate-planning mistake with OZs?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Assuming death eliminates deferred gain. It does not: deferred OZ gain is IRD. That mistaken assumption can leave heirs with an unexpected tax bill and insufficient liquidity. Other errors include assuming heirs restart the 10-year clock or that lifetime gifting is tax-free. Plan for the surviving deferred gain and its tax with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>How should I plan for the heirs' tax on the deferred gain?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>Ensure liquidity to pay the tax, whether from the estate, other assets, life insurance, or the investment itself. Anticipate the recognition date and deferred-gain tax amount so heirs do not need a distressed sale. Consider the income-tax deduction for estate tax attributable to IRD and integrate it with estate-tax and liquidity planning. Build the timing plan with your estate attorney and CPA.</p></div></details>
<details><summary>Are these OZ estate rules settled, or could they change?<span class="ic"></span></summary><div class="ans"><p>They are nuanced and evolving. The core principles reflect current understanding, but OZ 2.0 made the program permanent, changed deferral timing, and continues to be implemented through regulations. Precise estate treatment, especially under the newer framework, may be clarified or refined. Verify current treatment with estate counsel and a CPA rather than relying on old information.</p></div></details>

<h2 id="glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl class="glossary">
<div class="g"><dt>QOF Interest</dt><dd>Your ownership stake in a Qualified Opportunity Fund.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Deferred Gain</dt><dd>The original capital gain postponed via the OZ.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Income in Respect of a Decedent (IRD)</dt><dd>Untaxed income that survives death and is taxed to heirs.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Section 1014 Step-Up</dt><dd>The basis step-up to FMV at death (not for IRD).</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Basis at Death</dt><dd>The basis heirs take, nuanced by the IRD.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Recognition Date</dt><dd>When the deferred original gain is taxed.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Inclusion Event</dt><dd>An event accelerating the deferred gain (e.g., some transfers).</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Holding-Period Carryover</dt><dd>Heirs continuing (not restarting) the 10-year clock.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>10-Year Exclusion</dt><dd>Tax-free appreciation after a 10-year hold.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Estate Plan</dt><dd>Wills, trusts, and designations governing transfer.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Trust</dt><dd>A structure for holding and transferring assets.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Lifetime Gift</dt><dd>A transfer during life (generally an inclusion event).</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Estate Tax</dt><dd>Tax on the value of the estate at death.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>IRD Deduction</dt><dd>Income-tax deduction for estate tax on the IRD.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Heirs/Beneficiaries</dt><dd>Those who inherit the QOF interest.</dd></div>
<div class="g"><dt>Liquidity Planning</dt><dd>Ensuring funds to pay the deferred-gain tax.</dd></div>
</dl>
