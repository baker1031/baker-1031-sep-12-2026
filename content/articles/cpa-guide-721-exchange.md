# The CPA's 2026 Guide to 721 Exchanges (UPREITs)


[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**For Tax Advisors · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 20 min read**

A client can expect a 721 rollover to work like the next 1031 in a long chain. It does not. The client is moving from a DST at full-cycle into a REIT operating partnership, from Section 1031 rules to partnership-tax rules, and from Schedule E reporting to a K-1. That change is the whole point of the analysis.

First-order thinking sees nonrecognition at contribution. Second-order thinking follows the substituted basis, §704(c) built-in gain, debt, possible disguised sale, conversion tax, and the fact that the client cannot 1031 the OP units later. This guide is an advisor's refresher on those mechanics and the client diligence they require. It pairs with the client-facing [721 exchange guide](721-exchange-guide.html).

## Key Takeaways for Advisors

- Section 721 provides nonrecognition when property is contributed to a partnership—the REIT operating partnership—in exchange for OP units.
- The client becomes a limited partner. K-1 reporting, §704(c) built-in-gain allocations, and partnership-debt and §752 basis consequences apply.
- Conversion of OP units to REIT shares, or redemption for cash, is taxable and recognizes deferred gain. The move is one-way: there is no later §1031 path.
- OP units held until death can receive a §1014 step-up. Screen for the §721(b) investment-company exception and disguised-sale rules as well.

## How a 721 exchange works (advisor's refresher)

An UPREIT generally holds real-estate assets through an operating partnership (OP), with the REIT as general partner. In a 721 exchange, the client contributes property—or, in the usual two-step route, a DST interest at full-cycle—to the OP and receives OP units. Section 721 generally makes the exchange of property for that partnership interest nonrecognition: gain is not triggered at contribution.

The client then holds units that economically track REIT shares and receive matching distributions. The contrast with Section 1031 is important. There are no 45/180 deadlines because this is a contribution, not a like-kind exchange. But the deferral has moved into Subchapter K partnership rules, with the related basis, allocation, liability, reporting, and eventual-recognition consequences.

## Nonrecognition, basis, and §704(c)

The contributor receives a **substituted basis** in OP units equal to contributed-property basis, adjusted for liabilities under §752. The partnership receives a carryover basis in the property. Since contributed property commonly carries built-in gain, **§704(c)** requires the partnership to allocate that pre-contribution gain back to the contributor over time and when the OP later disposes of the property. It prevents that gain from being shifted to other partners.

For the CPA, the practical result is a K-1 that may contain special allocations tied to the contributed asset. Deferred gain can be recognized by the client's own conversion or redemption, and it can potentially be recognized if the OP sells the contributed property. Model these built-in-gain mechanics before recommending the structure.

## Debt and disguised-sale issues

Two partnership-tax issues require direct attention.

**Liabilities (§752).** If the contribution relieves the client of debt in excess of basis, gain can be triggered. Analyze contributed-property debt net of the client's share of OP liabilities.

**Disguised sales (§707(a)(2)(B)).** If the client receives cash or other consideration near the contribution, beyond qualified items, the IRS can recharacterize part of the arrangement as a taxable sale rather than a tax-free contribution.

These are classic traps: an intended nonrecognition event can become a partial recognition event. Structure them carefully with the REIT's counsel.

## OP units and ongoing reporting

While holding OP units, the client is a limited partner and receives a **Schedule K-1** for the distributive share of OP income, deductions, and credits. That replaces the Schedule E reporting of a DST. Cash distributions are generally nontaxable to the extent of basis, which they reduce, and §704(c) allocations remain layered into the position.

Track OP-unit basis carefully because it determines the taxable amount on conversion or redemption. Economically, distributions can resemble REIT dividends. The reporting does not: a partnership interest produces a different tax experience from direct REIT shares, and clients—and sometimes their other advisors—can find that distinction confusing.

## The taxable conversion event

Deferral within OP units is not permanent. Conversion of OP units to REIT shares, or redemption for cash, is generally a taxable disposition that recognizes deferred gain and any remaining built-in gain. Clients often convert gradually to spread recognition among years and manage bracket and NIIT exposure.

There is no continuing §1031 route from OP units. A partnership interest is not like-kind real property. The conversion decision is therefore a recognition-planning exercise for the advisor to manage and for the client to understand in advance.

## The one-way limitation

The most important client point is that a 721 is one-way. Once a client holds OP units, that equity generally cannot be used in a future §1031 exchange. The partnership interest is not like-kind real property, so a serial-1031 strategy that may have been used for years ends at the contribution.

This can suit a client ready to stop exchanging and consolidate in a diversified REIT. It is a poor fit for a client who wants to preserve flexibility. The client should make the choice deliberately; the client-facing [721 downsides memo](721-exchange-downsides.html) discusses the trade-offs.

## Estate planning with OP units

The 721 can have a strong estate-planning function. OP units held until death generally receive a **§1014 step-up** to fair market value, potentially eliminating deferred and built-in gain for heirs—the partnership analogue of the 1031 “swap-till-you-drop” endgame. Units are also more divisible among multiple heirs than a single property and may be converted into liquid shares to settle an estate.

Coordinate with estate counsel. Income-tax basis step-up and estate-tax inclusion are different issues, and holding a partnership interest carries its own considerations. The [estate-planning memo](721-exchange-estate-planning.html) provides the client-oriented overview.

## Client due-diligence checklist

- Confirm that the client is ready to relinquish §1031 flexibility; the move is irreversible.
- Analyze liabilities under §752, netting contributed-property debt against the client's share of OP liabilities to avoid triggered gain.
- Screen for disguised-sale risk by scrutinizing cash and other near-contribution consideration.
- Document §704(c) built-in gain and model when it might be recognized, including on an OP sale of the asset.
- Change reporting to K-1 and track OP-unit basis through distributions.
- Plan conversion timing and gradual recognition to manage brackets and NIIT.
- Coordinate estate planning for a possible step-up and division among heirs.

## What clients should know

A 721 continues deferral but changes the client’s position. The client becomes a partner with K-1—not Schedule E—reporting; the client generally cannot 1031 again; and liquidity through unit conversion to shares or cash is taxable. Diversification, eventual liquidity, a divisible estate-friendly asset, and a possible step-up at death are real benefits, but they arrive with irreversibility and partnership-tax complexity.

Frame the 721 as a destination for a client ready to stop exchanging, often after the two-step DST-then-721 sequence. It is not a way station.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What code section governs a 721 exchange?

Section 721. It provides nonrecognition for a contribution of property to a partnership—the REIT operating partnership—in exchange for a partnership interest, here OP units.

### How are OP units reported?

Through a Schedule K-1. The client is a limited partner reporting a distributive share of income, with §704(c) built-in-gain allocations; distributions are generally nontaxable to the extent of basis.

### Is converting OP units to REIT shares taxable?

Yes. Conversion to REIT shares, or redemption for cash, is generally a taxable disposition recognizing deferred and built-in gain. Gradual conversion can manage the timing of recognition.

### Can a client 1031 after a 721 exchange?

No. OP units are a partnership interest rather than like-kind real property, so the §1031 path ends. Confirm that the client accepts this one-way limitation before proceeding.

### What partnership-tax traps apply to a 721?

Liability netting under §752, where debt relief exceeding basis can trigger gain; disguised-sale recharacterization under §707(a)(2)(B) if cash is received; and §704(c) allocations of pre-contribution built-in gain.

### What's the estate-planning benefit of OP units?

OP units held to death can receive a §1014 step-up that eliminates deferred gain for heirs. Units are also more divisible among heirs than a single property and can be converted to liquid shares to settle an estate.

## Glossary

**Section 721**  
The nonrecognition rule for contributing property to a partnership in exchange for a partnership interest.

**OP Units**  
Operating-partnership units received in a 721 exchange; reported on a K-1 and convertible to REIT shares (taxably).

**§704(c)**  
The rule allocating pre-contribution built-in gain back to the contributing partner.

**Disguised Sale (§707(a)(2)(B))**  
Recharacterization of a contribution-plus-distribution as a taxable sale.

**§752**  
The partnership rules governing how liabilities affect a partner's basis.
