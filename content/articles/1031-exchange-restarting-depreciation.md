# 1031 Exchange and Restarting Depreciation

I keep seeing a 1031 exchange described as if the replacement were a fresh purchase for depreciation. It is not, at least not by default. The replacement basis is split: old basis carries its history forward, while new money gets a new depreciation schedule.

First-order thinking is, “A larger replacement means I restart depreciation.” Second-order thinking separates carryover basis from excess basis, projects the after-tax cash flow from both, and remembers that current deductions accumulate a recapture issue at a taxable sale. This is a technical area where the CPA should model the answer, not simply populate a return after closing.

## Carryover vs. excess basis

In a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html), replacement basis is generally the adjusted basis carried from the relinquished property plus additional investment used to buy a more expensive replacement. The carryover basis represents the deferred-gain portion. The excess basis represents new cash or financing put into the larger property.

For example, assume the relinquished property has a $200,000 adjusted basis. The investor buys a $500,000 replacement and adds $300,000 of investment. Replacement basis is $500,000: $200,000 carryover basis plus $300,000 excess basis. The two portions do not depreciate the same way.

## How depreciation continues

By default, the carryover basis continues the relinquished property's depreciation method and remaining recovery period as if no exchange occurred. It picks up where the old schedule left off; it does not restart.

That means an asset already depreciated for many years may have modest deductions and limited remaining life on its carryover portion. A fresh purchase depreciates its full cost from scratch. An exchange generally does not, which is a real item in an after-tax return forecast.

Taxpayers can elect out of the default split treatment and treat the whole replacement basis—carryover plus excess—as newly placed in service. That can sometimes improve the result, but it has trade-offs. Remaining life, excess-basis size, and the taxpayer's broader tax picture determine whether the election helps, so the CPA should model both treatments.

## Depreciating the excess basis portion

The excess basis is newly placed in service. It starts fresh under the applicable recovery period: generally 27.5 years for residential rental real estate and 39 years for commercial real estate under current rules.

This is one attraction of trading up. A substantially more expensive replacement creates more excess basis and therefore more fresh depreciation on top of the continuing old schedule. A same-value exchange creates little excess basis, so most depreciation may be the aging carryover schedule.

## Recapture at eventual sale

[Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html) carries through an exchange chain. At a later taxable sale, depreciation taken on carryover and excess basis, including prior exchanged property, is subject to recapture. For real property, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain can be taxed up to 25%.

The exchange defers that reckoning; it does not erase it. Depreciation deductions help while the property is held, but the recapture liability accumulates across successive exchanges and is due if the investor sells taxably.

The estate outcome differs. If the investor holds the property until death, a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can erase both deferred capital gain and accumulated depreciation recapture for heirs. That is why recapture matters in the distinction between a taxable exit and a long-term hold. The CPA should track the history throughout.

## Working with your CPA

The CPA establishes carryover and excess basis, sets the continuing and new schedules, handles Form 8824, tracks recapture, and evaluates the election. This work also informs the replacement's after-tax cash flow and the eventual choice to sell, exchange again, or hold.

## Key Takeaways

- Replacement basis splits between carryover basis and excess basis.
- By default, carryover basis keeps the old depreciation schedule; excess basis depreciates as new property.
- Trading up creates excess basis and fresh depreciation; the full-new-basis election may be available.
- Recapture accumulates through exchanges, is due at taxable sale, and can be erased by a basis step-up at death.

## Practical implications for investors

Post-exchange depreciation is often less generous than depreciation on a same-value fresh purchase because the carryover basis retains an old, partly used schedule. Include that in projections of tax shelter and after-tax cash flow.

Trading up can soften the effect by adding fresh depreciation on excess basis. More expensive replacement property may therefore matter not just to the equal-or-greater-value rule, but also to the depreciation profile.

The long-term decision remains clear: deductions provide value during ownership; a taxable sale brings recapture; a hold-until-death strategy may remove it through the step-up. The appropriate plan is individual and should be modeled with the CPA.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does depreciation work after a 1031 exchange?
Replacement basis is split. Carryover basis continues the old schedule by default; excess basis starts a new one. It is continuation plus a fresh schedule, not one new schedule on full value.

### What is carryover basis versus excess basis?
Carryover basis is adjusted basis from the relinquished property and represents deferred history. Excess basis is the additional investment in a more expensive replacement. Together they equal replacement basis.

### Does depreciation restart after an exchange?
Partly. It restarts on excess basis. Carryover basis continues unless the taxpayer elects whole-basis newly-placed-in-service treatment.

### Why is post-exchange depreciation less than a fresh purchase?
The carryover portion is often already partway through its schedule and may finish sooner. A fresh purchase starts the entire cost from scratch.

### Does trading up generate more depreciation?
Yes. The added investment creates excess basis, which depreciates newly and can add deductions beyond the carryover schedule.

### Can I elect to depreciate the whole basis as new property?
Yes. Taxpayers can elect out of the default split. It may or may not be better, and the CPA should test remaining life, excess basis, and tax facts.

### What is depreciation recapture in an exchange?
It is tax on gain attributable to prior depreciation, including across the whole exchange chain, generally up to 25% for real property's unrecaptured Section 1250 gain at taxable sale.

### Does step-up at death erase depreciation recapture?
Yes. A basis step-up at death can eliminate accumulated recapture along with deferred gain for heirs.

### How does recapture build up across exchanges?
Depreciation history follows the property through each exchange rather than disappearing. A final taxable sale recaptures the accumulated amount.

### Do DSTs pass through depreciation?
Yes. A DST passes depreciation on underlying real estate through to investors in its reporting; the investor's CPA coordinates the exchange-basis mechanics.

### Why do I need my CPA for post-exchange depreciation?
The CPA establishes the split schedules, considers the election, tracks recapture, and connects the result to returns and strategy.

### How does depreciation affect replacement-property returns?
It shelters some rental income and improves after-tax cash flow, but carryover depreciation can be lower than fresh-purchase depreciation. Excess basis improves it when trading up.

### What recovery period applies to excess basis?
Generally 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for commercial property, using the applicable method.

### Does carryover basis ever finish depreciating?
Yes. It ends when the relinquished property's remaining recovery period ends. Excess basis may continue on its separate new schedule.

### Is the election or default split treatment better?
It depends. The CPA should model both from remaining life, excess basis, and the tax picture.

### How do I avoid depreciation recapture entirely?
Holding until death can eliminate it through the step-up. A taxable sale triggers recapture.

## Glossary

- **Carryover Basis:** Adjusted relinquished basis continuing its old depreciation schedule.
- **Excess Basis:** New investment in a larger replacement, depreciated as new property.
- **Depreciation:** Cost-recovery deduction that can shelter rental income.
- **Recovery Period:** Depreciation length—27.5 years residential rental, 39 years commercial.
- **Newly Placed in Service:** Treatment of excess basis as new property.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on gain from prior depreciation, up to 25% for real property.
- **Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain:** Technical name for real-property depreciation recapture.
- **Election Out:** Option to depreciate full replacement basis as new property instead of using the split default.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Original cost reduced by depreciation.
- **Trading Up:** Acquiring a more expensive replacement and creating excess basis.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** Death-time basis reset that can erase gain and recapture.
- **Section 1.168(i)-6:** Regulation governing exchanged-property depreciation.
- **Form 8824:** IRS exchange and replacement-basis report.
- **After-Tax Cash Flow:** Income after tax, affected by depreciation shelter.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** Replacement that passes real-estate depreciation through to investors.
- **Exchange Chain:** Series of exchanges over which depreciation and recapture accumulate.
