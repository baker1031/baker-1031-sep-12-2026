# 1031 Exchange and Step-Up in Basis at Death

I have been thinking about the phrase “tax deferred.” It can sound final, but in a 1031 it is usually a promise carried forward in the basis, waiting for a taxable sale.

The step-up in basis at death is the event this source identifies as changing that outcome. If exchanged real estate is held until death, heirs generally receive a basis at fair-market value, eliminating the accumulated deferred gain for income-tax purposes. That is the capstone of the “swap-till-you-drop” approach—but only for an investor who can live with its holding, liquidity, legal, and estate-planning constraints.

## How deferral builds up

Each exchange defers the gain that would otherwise be recognized and carries the old, often low basis into the replacement. As an investor trades up, repositions, or diversifies through successive exchanges, the gap between low carryover basis and current property value becomes deferred gain.

Over decades, that gap can grow. Gain from the relinquished property rolls forward, and additional appreciation in each replacement can be deferred again in the next exchange. Someone starting with a modest property can eventually own a much more valuable portfolio whose bulk represents untaxed, deferred appreciation.

That is both the advantage and the liability. The investor compounds with the full pre-tax base. But a cash sale recognizes the accumulated deferred gain, potentially in one large taxable event. The deferral builds wealth and a latent tax liability at the same time.

## The step-up in basis at death

At death, the basis of real estate is generally stepped up to fair-market value. Heirs inherit a new, higher basis rather than the investor’s low carryover basis. For income-tax purposes, the appreciation accumulated during the investor’s ownership—including deferred gain from chained exchanges—is erased.

The basis gap disappears. If heirs sell around the stepped-up value, there is little or no gain to tax. The tax deferred throughout the investor’s life is not paid by the investor or the heirs under this source’s described outcome.

The condition is important: the real estate must be held at death. A cash sale before then recognizes deferred gain and gives up the step-up’s elimination. Continuing to exchange, and using passive structures such as DSTs when appropriate, can keep the chain intact until death.

> The deferred gain built across a lifetime of exchanges can vanish at the step-up, erasing the latent income-tax liability at death.

## Erasing a lifetime of deferred gain

Consider a portfolio built from a modest first property into a multi-million-dollar real estate portfolio through decades of exchanges. The source’s example assumes the bulk of value is untaxed appreciation. A cash sale could recognize all that gain and trigger the four-layer tax: capital gains, [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), and state tax. The bill could be seven figures.

At death, the step-up resets the heirs’ basis to current value. The decades of deferred appreciation and the associated embedded income-tax liability disappear for income-tax purposes. The heirs receive the full portfolio value without the deferred income tax attached.

This source characterizes the combination as the most tax-efficient real-estate strategy: tax-deferred compounding during life and complete elimination of the accumulated gain at death. A single 1031 defers tax. A lifetime of exchanges held to death can turn that deferral into elimination for heirs.

## Heir outcomes

Heirs receive a real estate portfolio with current-value basis and no embedded income-tax liability from the prior deferrals. They may sell shortly after inheritance with little or no gain, or keep the property and use a higher basis for larger depreciation deductions than the original investor had.

They can sell, hold, or begin their own [1031 exchanges](1031-exchange-guide.html) from that fresh basis if they want to reposition. The step-up gives the next generation a clean slate at current value rather than a low carryover basis carrying old deferred gain.

For families, that flexibility is central. Passive DST interests can also make the inheritance easier to divide among multiple heirs through fractional interests. The result is full value, clean basis, choice to sell or keep investing, and potentially easier division of a portfolio—not merely tax savings for the original investor.

## Planning the endgame

The endgame requires intent and coordination. The investor must hold rather than cash out, continuing to defer if repositioning is needed. A taxable sale before death recognizes the deferred gain and breaks the chain.

Later-life passive off-ramps can make that hold more workable. A standard 1031 exchange into a passive DST, or a DST’s full-cycle transition to a REIT operating partnership through a [721 exchange](721-exchange-guide.html), can offer passive income without a direct-property management burden while preserving the deferral and path to the step-up.

The CPA, estate attorney, and 1031 adviser need to work together. The CPA handles income-tax and basis aspects; the estate attorney addresses titling, transfer mechanics, and estate-tax questions; the 1031 adviser helps with exchanges and passive structures. That coordination is how the step-up is captured cleanly and wealth passes as intended.

### Key Takeaways

- Deferred gain is the latent tax liability created by the gap between low carryover basis and current value.
- A step-up at death resets heir basis to current value and erases accumulated deferred income-tax gain.
- Heirs receive the full value with clean basis and flexibility to sell, hold, or exchange.
- The plan depends on holding until death, using DST or 721 passive off-ramps where appropriate, and working with estate counsel.

## Important caveats and considerations

The strategy requires holding until death. A cash sale loses the described benefit, which ties wealth to real estate and can be unsuitable for someone who expects to need liquidity. Hold-to-death intent must be genuine.

The step-up and Section 1031 are features of current tax law, not permanent certainties. Proposals to limit or remove the step-up recur, and a multi-decade strategy carries legislative risk. The rules have been durable, but investors and their advisers should follow developments and keep plans adaptable.

The income-tax step-up is not the same as estate tax. The step-up handles income tax on deferred gain. A larger estate can still face separate federal or state estate tax on wealth transferred at death. The estate-tax exemption is high enough that it is not a concern for many estates, but larger estates need distinct estate-tax planning with an estate attorney.

First-order thinking sees the potential tax elimination. Second-order thinking asks whether the investor can forgo cashing out, remain exposed to real-estate liquidity, stay adaptable to tax-law changes, and coordinate the estate plan. The endgame only works when those conditions fit the investor’s actual life.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does the step-up in basis eliminate deferred 1031 gain?

If real estate is held at death, heirs generally receive fair-market-value basis. That erases appreciation and deferred gain from the investor’s exchanges for income-tax purposes. A near-value heir sale has little or no gain.

### Does a 1031 exchange eliminate tax or just defer it?

A single 1031 defers tax because low basis carries forward. In the source’s lifetime strategy, holding exchanged property until death adds a step-up that eliminates the accumulated deferred gain.

### How does deferred gain build up over a lifetime?

Low carryover basis follows each exchange. As properties appreciate and are exchanged again, the difference between that basis and value grows. Over decades it may represent much of the portfolio.

### Why is holding until death necessary?

The step-up applies to property held at death. A taxable sale before death recognizes the deferred gain and forfeits the elimination, so the strategy requires continuing to hold or exchange through life.

### What do heirs receive under this strategy?

They receive a current-value basis without embedded deferred income tax. They can sell with little gain, hold with higher depreciation basis, or start their own exchange chain.

### How much tax can the step-up erase?

Potentially the entire deferred gain. In a multi-million-dollar portfolio where most value is deferred appreciation, a four-layer tax and a seven-figure bill can be eliminated at death for income-tax purposes.

### How do I go passive without breaking the chain?

Use the passive off-ramps: a standard 1031 into DSTs or, at a DST full-cycle, a 721 contribution to a REIT operating partnership. These can provide passive income while preserving deferral and the hold-to-death path.

### Is the step-up the same as avoiding estate tax?

No. The step-up addresses income tax on deferred gain. Estate tax is a separate transfer-at-death tax that can apply to larger estates, though the exemption protects many estates.

### Could the step-up rules change?

Yes. Proposals to limit or eliminate it arise periodically, and a multi-decade strategy carries legislative risk. Follow developments through advisers and keep the plan adaptable; Section 1031 could also change.

### What if I need to sell before death?

The deferred gain is recognized and the step-up’s elimination is lost. The strategy is not suited to investors who will need a cash-out, so liquidity needs matter.

### Who do I need to plan the endgame?

A 1031 adviser for exchanges and passive structures, a CPA for income-tax and basis issues, and an estate attorney for transfer and estate-tax matters. Their work needs to be coordinated.

### Is this strategy only for wealthy investors?

No. It can start with a modest property and grow through a lifetime of exchanges. Larger estates simply add estate-tax planning needs.

### Does the step-up apply to depreciation recapture too?

Yes. It resets basis at death and erases the gain attributable to prior depreciation recapture along with the other accumulated gain for income-tax purposes.

### What happens to the deferred gain if I gift the property instead?

A lifetime gift generally carries over the investor’s basis. It does not receive a step-up, so deferred gain remains and can be taxed when the recipient sells. The described strategy relies on transfer at death rather than a gift.

### Can my heirs continue the exchange strategy?

Yes. They receive stepped-up basis and can begin their own 1031 chain, allowing the swap-till-you-drop approach to repeat across generations.

### How do I make sure the step-up is captured at death?

Hold rather than sell for cash, and coordinate titling, estate planning, and transfer mechanics with an estate attorney. DST and 721 off-ramps can permit a hands-off hold; the CPA manages basis and income-tax issues.

## Glossary

- **Step-Up in Basis:** Resetting an asset’s basis to fair-market value at death, erasing accumulated gain for heirs.
- **Deferred Gain:** Unrecognized gain accumulated from chained exchanges, a latent tax liability erased by the step-up.
- **Carryover Basis:** Low basis carried through exchanges; its gap from value is deferred gain.
- **Swap Till You Drop:** Chaining exchanges and holding until death so the step-up erases deferred gain.
- **Date-of-Death Value:** Fair-market value to which basis is stepped up at death.
- **Latent Tax Liability:** Tax that would be due on deferred gain in a sale, eliminated by the step-up at death.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax on deferred gain.
- **Estate Tax:** A separate transfer-of-wealth tax at death, distinct from the income-tax step-up.
- **Estate-Tax Exemption:** The amount that can pass free of federal estate tax, high enough that most estates are not affected.
- **DST Off-Ramp:** A DST exchange used to go passive while keeping deferral and the path to the step-up.
- **721 Exchange (UPREIT):** A contribution to a REIT operating partnership for units, used as a passive off-ramp.
- **Hold-to-Death Intent:** A commitment to hold property until death to capture the step-up.
- **Legislative Risk:** Risk that step-up or Section 1031 rules change over a multi-decade strategy.
- **Heir:** The person inheriting property with stepped-up basis and no embedded deferred gain.
- **Multigenerational Wealth:** Real estate wealth transferred tax-efficiently through exchanges and the step-up.
- **Estate Attorney:** The professional coordinating transfer and estate-tax aspects of the endgame.
