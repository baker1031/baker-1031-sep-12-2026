# 1031 Identification Rules: The 3-Property, 200% and 95% Rules

I have been thinking about how often a well-planned exchange gets exposed by one unglamorous step: the list sent during the 45-day identification period. The rules are not hard to understand. They are hard to repair after the deadline.

The IRS limits both what an exchanger may name and, under some tests, the total value named. It also requires a particular form of notice. An identification that exceeds the applicable rule or is delivered improperly can invalidate an otherwise sound exchange. This memo covers the three tests, the notice mechanics, and a practical approach to choosing candidates.

## Why the identification rules exist

The rules balance two real needs. You need options in case the primary replacement fails, because nothing new can be added after Day 45. But the IRS does not want an exchanger to preserve an unlimited option on every property in the market by filing an unbounded list.

The three rules answer that problem by limiting either the property count or total value, with a narrow exception for a buyer that actually acquires nearly everything identified. Decide how the list will qualify before drafting it. If the list does not satisfy a rule, the identification fails.

## The three identification rules at a glance

Within 45 days of the sale, identify replacement property under one of the following rules. You need to satisfy only one.

| Rule | What it allows |
| --- | --- |
| Three-property rule | Identify up to three properties of any total value, then acquire one or more. |
| 200% rule | Identify any number of properties if their combined value is no more than 200% of what you sold. |
| 95% rule | Identify beyond those limits only if you acquire at least 95% of the total value identified. |

These tests sit within the broader 45-day clock covered in the [timeline memo](1031-exchange-timeline.html). They recognize that some investors need a primary target and backups, while others are assembling a diversified portfolio. The 95% rule is the strict backstop when the first two are not met.

## The three-property rule

This is the simplest and most-used choice. You may identify up to **three** replacement properties regardless of their value, then buy any one, two, or all three. No value calculation is required. Most investors use it for a primary target plus one or two backups.

Naming three properties does not obligate you to buy three. It preserves options. A backup or two can be useful insurance if the primary deal falls through.

## The 200% rule

When three candidates are not enough—usually because proceeds will be spread among smaller properties—the **200% rule** permits *any number* of properties whose combined fair-market value does not exceed **200%** of the relinquished property’s value. Sell for $1,000,000 and you may identify replacements totaling up to $2,000,000.

The constraint is aggregate value. “Just one more” candidate can push the list beyond 200% and invalidate the rule unless the 95% rule can be met instead. Keep a running calculation while building the list.

## The 95% rule

The **95% rule** is an escape valve, not a plan. If the list contains more than three properties *and* exceeds 200% of what was sold, it is valid only if you actually **acquire at least 95%** of the total value identified. That leaves almost no room for one deal to fail.

Sophisticated investors generally treat this test as a backstop for an overbroad list, not a deliberate strategy. If the exchange depends on acquiring nearly every named property, the original identification may have been structured poorly.

## Choosing the right rule

Most exchangers use the three-property rule. It accommodates one replacement plus one or two backups without tracking total value, and a high-value backup is no problem. Investors intentionally diversifying across several DSTs or smaller assets often use the 200% rule, keeping their list at or below twice the relinquished value. The 95% exception is the last resort for a buyer that expects to acquire nearly everything named.

Choose the rule before preparing the notice, and let it govern both count and value. A [qualified intermediary](insights.html) can check whether the intended notice fits the chosen rule before it is delivered.

### Key Takeaways

- **Three-property rule:** up to three properties of any value; it is the usual choice.
- **200% rule:** more than three properties, but total value cannot exceed 200% of the sale value; it suits diversification.
- **95% rule:** any number of properties, but at least 95% of the identified value must be acquired; it is rarely used.

## Worked examples of each rule

Assume a $1,000,000 sale. Under the **three-property rule**, an exchanger can name a $500,000 property, a $900,000 property, and a $1,200,000 property. Their total value does not matter, and the exchanger can acquire whichever works. A $1,000,000 primary target with two smaller backups is a familiar arrangement.

Under the **200% rule**, the exchanger could name five DSTs totaling $2,000,000, then acquire the ones that form the desired portfolio. Name six worth $2,200,000 and the cap is breached. Under the **95% rule**, the exchanger could identify ten properties worth $3,000,000, but must close on at least $2,850,000. The figures are illustrative; the governing limits are the point.

## How value is counted under the 200% rule

The 200% calculation uses the fair-market value of every identified property, summed across the list and compared with 200% of the relinquished property’s value. It is gross property value, not the exchanger’s equity.

Leverage does not change that measure. A $2,000,000 property purchased with $1,000,000 of debt still counts as $2,000,000. Use full values, maintain a running total, and ask the qualified intermediary to verify it before delivery. That clean count separates a valid 200%-rule notice from a failed one.

## A diversified 200%-rule example

Assume a commercial building sells for $2,000,000 and the investor wants several replacements rather than one. The 200% rule allows identified fair-market value up to $4,000,000.

The investor names five DSTs: a $1,200,000 multifamily DST, a $900,000 net-lease DST, an $800,000 industrial DST, a $600,000 medical-office DST, and a $500,000 royalty DST. Together they equal $4,000,000, right at the cap. Each has a defined value. The investor then acquires the four that best fit the target allocation, investing enough to meet equal-or-greater value and replace debt through leveraged DSTs. The result is one exchange spread over five sectors, multiple sponsors, and many geographies rather than a single building.

That result rests on a disciplined tally. The fair-market values were tracked, the list stayed within $4,000,000, and the qualified intermediary confirmed the values before delivery. Add a sixth DST and lift the total to $4,300,000, and the 200% rule has been breached unless at least 95% of the whole $4.3 million is acquired.

## How to write a valid identification

Meeting a value or count test is not enough. The notice itself must be:

- **In writing and signed** by the taxpayer.
- **Delivered by midnight on Day 45** to the qualified intermediary or another party to the exchange—not to the exchanger’s own agent, attorney, or relative.
- **Unambiguous.** Each property should be described by a street address or legal description, not “a property in Dallas.”

There is one useful qualification. Under the **incidental property rule**, property that accompanies a larger asset and is worth no more than 15% of the larger property’s value—appliances, furnishings, or equipment that come with a building, for example—generally does not need separate identification. It travels with the principal asset.

## Revoking and changing an identification

The first notice is not irrevocable. You may revoke and re-identify in writing as often as needed before the 45-day deadline. After midnight on Day 45, the identification freezes. What is on the list then is what remains for the exchange.

Do not wait for the last minute. Finalizing a day or two early creates time to correct a delivery problem or description error; a failed delivery on Day 45 has no spare day to fix it.

## Strategy: how many properties to identify

The rules are mechanical; judgment lies in how they are used. For a single replacement, the three-property rule with a primary target and one or two genuine backups is usually the sensible default. A fast-closing [Delaware Statutory Trust](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) can be an effective backup because it can settle in days.

Investors assembling a portfolio use the 200% rule but must watch the aggregate-value ceiling. Nearly everyone should avoid relying on the 95% rule, because it leaves no margin for a failed deal. Identify with the same care used to close: a sloppy notice can undo a technically sound exchange.

## Common identification mistakes

Most failures are procedural and avoidable.

- **Vague description:** “A retail center in Phoenix” is not enough. Use an address or legal description; ambiguity can void the designation.
- **Delivery to the wrong party:** The qualified intermediary or another exchange party must receive it. Your agent, attorney, or a relative does not count.
- **Oral or informal notice:** A phone call, text, or unsigned email does not work. The notice must be written and signed.
- **Breaching the 200% cap:** One additional candidate can put the list over the limit and leave only the 95% test.
- **Relying on 95%:** A 95% acquisition obligation leaves little room for a transaction to fail.
- **Waiting for Day 45:** A last-minute delivery error cannot be corrected after the deadline.

The defense is simple: a written, signed, unambiguous notice delivered to the right party with days to spare.

## Identifying DSTs and fractional interests

Replacement property need not be a whole building. A fractional interest, including a [Delaware Statutory Trust](1031-exchange-into-dst.html) or tenant-in-common interest, may be identified and acquired like other replacement property. Identify the specific offering or trust and the **percentage or beneficial interest** to be acquired. A street address alone may not remove ambiguity.

A DST can absorb a precise dollar amount and close in days. That makes a pre-identified DST a useful form of insurance: if the primary deal collapses late in the period, the fallback is already on the list and can close in the remaining window.

## How advisors use the rules in practice

Experienced advisors treat the identification rules as a risk-management tool, not merely a compliance hurdle. For a typical single-property replacement, they may use a three-property notice with a strong primary and one or two fast-closing backups, often including a DST.

For intentional diversification, they can build a 200%-rule list of several DSTs across sectors, sized so the combined value remains within the cap and the eventual acquisitions meet equal-or-greater-value and debt-replacement targets. The aim is to complete a fully deferred exchange while preserving options. The 95% exception rarely belongs in the plan except in large, near-certain portfolio acquisitions.

Preparation is the recurring advantage. Assemble and vet the shortlist before the sale, choose the right test, prepare an accurate notice, and confirm compliance before Day 45. That turns identification from a last-minute scramble into a deliberate transaction step.

## Identification in special situations

The same rules apply in special cases, though the measurements can change. When multiple relinquished properties are sold in one exchange, the 200% cap is measured against their combined value. That offers more room for replacements, but also more aggregate value to track.

With a partnership or LLC, the entity identifies and acquires as one taxpayer; individual partners cannot select separate replacements within that same exchange. Partners who want different assets usually need a drop-and-swap plan well in advance. In a construction or improvement exchange, identify the replacement property and describe the planned improvements precisely enough to identify the completed property; only improvements finished and paid for within 180 days count toward replacement value. Related-party exchanges add a two-year holding requirement. In every complex case, choose the rule that fits, track count and value, describe property unambiguously, and bring an experienced adviser and CPA in early.

## The rules in practice

Most exchanges rely on the three-property rule: a primary plus one or two backups, often including a DST. It is simple, flexible, and value-blind.

The 200% rule becomes useful when one exchange will be spread across several assets, commonly DSTs in different sectors and with different sponsors. The 95% exception remains rare. First-order thinking treats the 45-day list as a form to complete. Second-order thinking treats it as an irreversible allocation decision: choose early, build the list to its limits, and have the qualified intermediary confirm compliance before Day 45.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many properties can I identify in a 1031 exchange?

Up to three of any value under the three-property rule, or more under the 200% rule if combined value remains within twice the sale value. The 95% rule allows still more, but only when at least 95% of identified value is acquired.

### Do I have to buy every property I identify?

No. Identification preserves options; you can acquire one or more properties. The exception is the 95% rule, which requires acquisition of at least 95% of the identified value.

### How do I properly identify replacement property?

Use a written document signed by the taxpayer, deliver it to the qualified intermediary by midnight on Day 45, and describe each property unambiguously—usually with a street address or legal description.

### Can I change my identification after I submit it?

Yes, but only before the 45-day deadline. You may revoke and re-identify in writing through Day 45. After that, the notice is locked.

### What is the incidental property rule?

Property incidental to a larger asset and worth no more than 15% of the larger property’s value, such as building appliances or furnishings, generally does not require separate identification.

### Which identification rule is most common?

The three-property rule. It is simple and value-blind, allowing a primary and one or two backups of any value without a total-value calculation.

### How does the 200% rule work?

It allows more than three properties when their combined fair-market value does not exceed 200% of what was sold. A $1,000,000 sale permits a list totaling up to $2,000,000.

### What is the 95% exception?

It permits any number of properties at any value only if at least 95% of the total value identified is actually acquired. With little room for a failed deal, it is a rarely used last resort.

### Is property value or my equity counted under the 200% rule?

Gross fair-market value counts, not equity. A $2,000,000 replacement funded with $1,000,000 of debt still counts as $2,000,000 toward the cap.

### What happens if I identify too many properties?

Four or more under the three-property rule, or a list above 200% under the 200% rule, voids the identification unless the 95% exception is satisfied by acquiring 95% of the total identified value. A void identification generally causes the exchange to fail.

### How do DSTs fit the identification rules?

DSTs are defined offerings with defined values, making them straightforward to identify by trust and dollar or percentage interest and to count toward the 200% cap. A direct primary plus DST backup is common under the three-property rule; multiple DSTs can form a diversified 200% list.

### Can I change which rule I'm using after identifying?

The delivered identification must satisfy a rule by Day 45. You can revoke and replace it in writing during the window, but after Day 45 the list and the rule it satisfies are locked.

### Can I mix the identification rules?

The list must qualify under one rule for the exchange: the three-property rule, the 200% rule, or the 95% exception. In practice, up to three properties of any value fit the first; more than three fit the 200% rule if the value cap is respected.

### Do all identified properties have to be like-kind?

Yes. Each identified property must be qualifying like-kind real property held for investment or business. That can include DSTs and other fractional interests, but a non-qualifying property does not help the exchange.

### What's the safest identification strategy?

For many exchangers, a strong primary plus a fast-closing DST backup under the three-property rule is safest: it is simple, value-blind, and resilient when a primary deal fails. Diversifiers can use the 200% rule for several DSTs within the cap. In both cases, backups protect the exchange.

## Glossary

- **Three-Property Rule:** Allows identification of up to three replacement properties of any value.
- **200% Rule:** Allows any number of properties when their combined value is no more than twice the relinquished property’s value.
- **95% Rule:** Allows identification beyond the other limits only if at least 95% of the total identified value is acquired.
- **Incidental Property Rule:** Property incidental to a larger asset and worth no more than 15% of that asset’s value generally need not be separately identified.
- **Identification Notice:** The signed written document naming replacement property and delivered to the qualified intermediary by Day 45.
- **Fair-Market Value:** The gross value used against the 200% cap, rather than equity.
- **Diversification:** Distributing exchange proceeds across multiple properties to reduce risk.
- **Backup Identification:** A fast-closing option, often a DST, named so the exchange can close if the primary fails.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A defined fractional offering that is straightforward to identify and count toward the rules.
- **Revocation:** Written withdrawal of an identification within the 45-day window in order to re-identify.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The required party that receives the identification and can confirm rule compliance.
- **Equity vs. Value:** Under the 200% rule, gross property value counts toward the cap, not the exchanger’s equity.
- **Identification Period:** The 45-day window after the sale for identifying replacement property.
