# Interest-Rate Risk on Leveraged DSTs

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep coming back to a simple underwriting question: if a property needs to refinance at the wrong time, who carries the consequences? In a leveraged Delaware Statutory Trust, or DST, the answer reaches distributions, sale value, and the capital returned to the investor. Leverage can replace the mortgage debt from a relinquished property and improve current cash-on-cash yield. It also makes the investment sensitive to rates.

The first-order argument for leverage is yield and debt replacement. The second-order question is what happens when the loan matures in a less favorable rate environment, property values are under pressure, or a floating rate raises debt service before the asset has time to adjust. A DST’s tax structure gives it less flexibility than a direct owner has to manage that moment.

## How Leverage Adds Rate Risk

A leveraged DST owns property financed with debt, usually a non-recourse mortgage. The DST, not an individual investor, is responsible for the loan. Leverage is used for two important reasons in a 1031 exchange: it can lift current cash-on-cash yield, and it can [replace the mortgage debt from a relinquished property](1031-exchange-debt-replacement-mortgage-boot.html). Replacing that debt is generally necessary to fully defer tax when the sold property carried a loan.

Debt also adds interest-rate risk: changes in prevailing rates can change debt cost, property value, and investor returns. With a floating-rate loan, rising rates immediately increase the DST’s interest expense. With a fixed-rate loan, rate risk waits at maturity, when the debt must be addressed at then-current rates. Higher rates also tend to push real-estate capitalization rates higher and values lower. A leveraged property facing a sale or refinancing can be pressured on both its income and its value.

Leverage magnifies the swings in the equity investors own. That can help when the property and financing behave well. It can hurt when debt expense rises or values fall. The size of the effect depends on the rate type, loan-to-value, maturity, and property performance.

## Refinancing Risk at Loan Maturity

For a leveraged DST, refinancing risk at maturity is the central rate risk. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, the trustee is restricted from refinancing or renegotiating the existing loan. That restriction is one of the prohibited actions sometimes called the “seven deadly sins,” and it helps preserve the DST interest’s treatment as 1031-eligible real property. See the [1031 exchange guide](1031-exchange-guide.html) for the larger exchange framework.

An ordinary property owner can often refinance early, extend a loan, or alter terms to manage the rate cycle. A DST cannot freely do that. When the loan matures, the DST generally must sell the property or refinance as part of a sale or restructuring in the rate environment then available.

If rates are high, refinancing may be more expensive or harder to obtain. A sponsor could face a sale whose timing and price are driven by the loan rather than by an attractive market opportunity. The timing of maturity relative to the rate cycle is therefore a material factor that is largely outside an investor’s control after investing.

> A DST trustee cannot simply refinance to dodge a bad rate environment. A leveraged DST’s outcome is partly tied to where rates stand when its loan comes due.

## Impact on Distributions

Rate risk shows up in the cash an investor receives. On a floating-rate loan, a rate increase immediately raises interest expense. Distributions come from net cash flow after debt service, so more paid to the lender generally leaves less to distribute.

Fixed-rate debt can create a similar result later. A higher-rate refinancing can raise ongoing debt service and reduce cash available to investors after the old loan matures.

There are indirect effects as well. A sponsor facing rate pressure, weaker property value, or a looming maturity may preserve reserves or take defensive steps that temper distributions. If a high-rate environment forces a sale, distributions stop and the result turns on the sale proceeds, which higher rates may have reduced. A debt-free DST does not have a loan payment that can rise, but no DST distribution is guaranteed; distributions are projections based on property performance.

## All-Cash DSTs as a Hedge

An all-cash DST owns the property outright, without a mortgage. No loan means no floating-rate exposure, no refinancing risk at maturity, and no rate-driven forced sale caused by debt coming due. The trustee’s refinancing restriction does not matter when there is nothing to refinance.

That makes an all-cash DST a natural hedge against financing and interest-rate risk within the DST structure. It does not eliminate other risks. The property can still lose value, tenants can underperform, the investment remains illiquid, and sponsor execution still matters. See [other risks every DST investor should know](dst-risks-what-every-1031-investor-should-know.html).

The hedge has a cost. Without leverage, an all-cash DST generally offers a lower current yield than a comparable leveraged deal because debt is not amplifying the equity return. It also cannot, by itself, replace an exchanger’s mortgage debt. To fully defer tax, an investor whose relinquished property had debt may need to add cash or pair an all-cash DST with a leveraged DST. The trade is clear: less financing risk in exchange for lower yield and less debt-replacement utility.

## Key Takeaways

- Leverage adds interest-rate risk through floating-rate exposure, maturity refinancing, and the effect of rates on property value; it amplifies the equity swings.
- Because a DST trustee cannot freely refinance, a leveraged DST must refinance or sell into the rate environment at maturity.
- Higher rates can compress distributions during the hold through debt service and at exit through a weaker sale. Distributions are projections, never guarantees.
- An all-cash DST removes financing risk, but it commonly has lower yield and cannot alone replace a relinquished-property mortgage.

## Fixed vs. Floating Rates and Leverage Levels

Not all leveraged DSTs carry the same rate exposure. Two variables do most of the work: whether the loan is fixed or floating, and how much leverage the property carries.

A fixed-rate loan locks interest cost during its term, insulating debt service and distributions from rate moves during the hold. Its rate risk is concentrated at maturity. A floating-rate loan moves with a benchmark, so a rate increase affects the property immediately and throughout the hold. It can be cheaper if rates fall, but it carries continuing uncertainty and makes distributions more sensitive to the rate cycle.

The leverage level is measured by [loan-to-value](dst-loan-and-leverage-matching-debt-in-a-1031.html), the loan amount as a percentage of property value. A low LTV means less debt relative to equity. Rate changes and refinancing should have a proportionally smaller effect, and the property has more cushion if value declines. A high LTV can increase yield in good conditions but also increases rate sensitivity, refinancing strain, and the chance that falling value erodes the equity cushion.

Two questions tell an investor much of what they need to know: is the loan fixed or floating, and how much debt is riding on the property? Fixed-rate, lower-leverage structures are generally more conservative on rate exposure than floating-rate, higher-leverage structures.

## Assessing a DST’s Debt

Start with the offering’s [private placement memorandum](how-to-review-a-ppm.html), or PPM. Determine whether the DST is all-cash or leveraged. If it has debt, identify the loan amount and LTV, whether the rate is fixed or floating, and when the loan matures relative to the anticipated hold period. Those four points—presence of debt, LTV, rate type, and maturity timing—frame most of the rate risk.

Then consider the interaction. A loan maturing near or after the expected sale date concentrates risk at exit. A loan maturing well before the planned sale can create a mid-hold refinancing that does not align with the rate cycle. Floating debt merits more caution than fixed debt; high LTV merits more caution than low LTV.

An investor who wants more stability may favor lower leverage, fixed-rate debt, all-cash DSTs, or diversification across debt profiles. Ask the sponsor and broker-dealer about the financing terms. The broker-dealer’s independent due diligence and Regulation Best Interest obligations can help with suitability, but the amount of rate risk that fits an investor remains a personal judgment based on goals and tolerance.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a leveraged DST?

A leveraged DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust whose property is financed with debt, generally a non-recourse mortgage for which the trust is responsible. Leverage can raise cash-on-cash yield and help an exchanger replace mortgage debt from a relinquished property, which is generally necessary to fully defer capital-gains tax when that property had a loan. Non-recourse debt is secured by the property rather than the investors personally, so an investor’s exposure is generally limited to invested capital. The trade-off is added interest-rate and refinancing risk compared with a debt-free DST.

### How does leverage add interest-rate risk to a DST?

The DST now has a loan whose cost and refinancing depend on rates. A floating rate can raise interest expense and reduce distributions immediately. A fixed loan still has maturity risk. Higher rates can also increase capitalization rates and reduce property values, so a leveraged DST can be pressured on cash flow and sale value at once. More debt magnifies the gains and losses in equity. A debt-free DST avoids this particular form of risk.

### What is refinancing risk in a DST?

It is the risk that a maturing DST loan must be refinanced or the property sold in an unfavorable rate environment. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 restricts the trustee from refinancing or renegotiating the existing loan to preserve 1031 status. A DST cannot proactively lock in a favorable rate as an ordinary owner could. If rates are high at maturity, refinancing may be expensive or unavailable, and the sponsor may face a poorly timed sale. Maturity’s place in the rate cycle can drive the outcome.

### Why can’t a DST just refinance to avoid high rates?

The restriction is part of the tax structure, not an oversight. Revenue Ruling 2004-86 limits refinancing and renegotiation as one of the seven deadly sins required to keep beneficial interests treated as direct interests in real property for 1031 purposes. The structure trades debt-management flexibility for the 1031 eligibility that permits tax deferral. At maturity, the DST’s main choices are refinancing as part of a sale or restructuring, or selling, at prevailing rates.

### How do rising interest rates affect my DST distributions?

With floating-rate debt, rising rates increase interest expense immediately. Because distributions are paid from property cash flow after debt service, less cash remains. Fixed-rate debt can create the same effect when a maturing loan is refinanced at a higher rate. A sponsor may also increase reserves or take defensive action, which can temper distributions. A forced sale can end distributions and shift the result to potentially weaker sale proceeds. In all cases, distributions are projections, not guarantees.

### What is an all-cash DST?

An all-cash, debt-free DST owns the property outright. It has no floating-rate exposure, no maturing mortgage to refinance, and no sale forced by a loan coming due. That makes it a rate-risk hedge and a more conservative choice on financing risk. The price is generally lower current yield and no ability to replace relinquished mortgage debt on its own; an exchanger may need additional cash or a paired leveraged DST.

### Are all-cash DSTs safer than leveraged DSTs?

They remove one category of risk—financing and interest-rate risk—but are not categorically safe. An all-cash DST still faces vacancy, rent declines, tenant default, property-value risk, sponsor risk, and illiquidity with no secondary market. Distributions are still projections. It also generally offers lower yield. “Safer” should refer to the specific financing risk being addressed, not to every risk in the offering.

### What is the difference between fixed and floating rate DST debt?

A fixed-rate loan locks interest cost through its term, so debt service is protected from market-rate moves during the hold; refinancing risk appears at maturity. A floating-rate loan follows a benchmark, so rate increases affect interest expense and distributions immediately and continually. In a falling-rate period it can be cheaper, but uncertainty persists. Fixed debt concentrates rate risk at maturity; floating debt distributes it through the holding period. Rate type and leverage level are the two most important rate-risk questions.

### What loan-to-value is considered conservative for a DST?

There is no universal threshold. Lower LTV is generally more conservative because the loan is smaller relative to equity, rate movements and refinancing have a smaller proportional effect, and the property has more value cushion. Higher LTV can improve yield in good conditions but increases sensitivity to rates, refinancing, and falling values. Many DSTs use moderate leverage, while some are all-cash with zero LTV. Consider LTV with rate type, maturity, and personal tolerance rather than treating one number as decisive.

### How does interest-rate risk relate to a DST’s sale?

Higher rates tend to increase capitalization rates and reduce real-estate values. A DST sold in a high-rate environment may therefore return less capital. For a leveraged DST, a maturity can force that sale when values are pressured because the trustee cannot freely refinance. An all-cash DST has more timing flexibility without a debt maturity, though its sale value can still be affected by rates. Both the rate level and whether a loan dictates the timing matter.

### Can I combine all-cash and leveraged DSTs in one exchange?

Yes. An exchanger can allocate proceeds to leveraged and all-cash DSTs, using the leveraged part to replace debt and the all-cash part to reduce overall financing exposure. The combination can also diversify property, sponsor, and sector exposure. The right mix depends on debt-replacement needs, income goals, and tolerance for rate risk. Because the 1031 rules and debt math are technical, work through the details with a broker-dealer and tax advisor; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### Where do I find a DST’s debt terms?

The PPM is the central disclosure document. It should identify whether the DST is leveraged or all-cash and, if leveraged, loan amount, LTV, fixed or floating rate, maturity date, interest-only periods, prepayment terms, and financing risk factors. Review the financing summary and risk factors together. A broker-dealer can help locate and compare the terms, but judging only headline yield misses the debt profile that drives rate exposure.

### How does interest-rate risk compare to other DST risks?

Interest-rate risk applies specifically to leveraged DSTs and should be assessed with the offering’s other risks. A PPM can address market and tenant risks, including vacancy, rent decline, and tenant default; liquidity and sponsor risks, including no secondary market and dependence on sponsor competence; and tax and structural risks, including trustee restrictions and potential failure of a 1031 exchange to qualify. Rising rates can compound market risk by lowering values while increasing borrowing cost. All-cash ownership removes rate risk, not the other risks.

### Does interest-rate risk make leveraged DSTs a bad investment?

No. It is a real trade-off, not an automatic disqualifier. Leverage can raise current yield and replace mortgage debt, which is often why an exchanger considers it. The price is rate and refinancing risk. A moderately leveraged, fixed-rate DST with a sensible maturity may carry less rate exposure than a highly leveraged, floating-rate DST. All-cash DSTs or diversification across debt profiles may fit an investor who wants less financing risk. The question is whether the debt terms, property, market, goals, and risk tolerance fit together.

## Glossary

- **Leveraged DST:** A DST whose property is financed with debt rather than owned all-cash.
- **All-Cash DST:** A debt-free DST that owns property outright with no mortgage.
- **Interest-Rate Risk:** The risk that rate changes affect debt cost, value, and returns.
- **Refinancing Risk:** The risk of refinancing a maturing loan in a poor rate environment.
- **Non-Recourse Debt:** A loan secured by the property, not the investors personally.
- **Loan-to-Value (LTV):** The loan amount as a percentage of the property’s value.
- **Fixed-Rate Loan:** A loan whose interest rate is locked for its term.
- **Floating-Rate Loan:** A loan whose interest rate moves with a benchmark.
- **Loan Maturity:** The date a loan must be repaid or refinanced.
- **Debt Service:** The periodic principal and interest payments on a loan.
- **Debt Replacement:** Matching relinquished-property debt to fully defer 1031 tax.
- **Capitalization Rate:** Net operating income divided by property value; it tends to rise with rates.
- **Cash-on-Cash Yield:** Annual distributions as a percentage of invested capital.
- **Trustee Restrictions:** DST limits, including no refinancing, that preserve 1031 status.
- **Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling making DST interests 1031-eligible real property.
- **Distributions:** Cash income paid to DST investors, never guaranteed.
