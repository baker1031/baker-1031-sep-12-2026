# REIT Risks Every Investor Should Know

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**Filed under:** [REIT](insights.html) and [REITs](insights.html)  
**By:** Baker 1031 Research  
**Updated:** June 2026 · **16 min read**

I keep seeing REITs introduced through their income potential before anyone asks what could interrupt it. A REIT can lose value, cut a distribution, or underperform. Those possibilities are not side notes; they are part of the investment.

REITs can offer attractive income and diversification, but they are not risk-free. This guide covers interest-rate risk, sector and tenant concentration, leverage and refinancing risk, management and conflict risk, and market and liquidity risk—along with practical ways to manage them.

First-order thinking treats a high distribution rate as the whole story. Second-order thinking asks what supports it: rents, tenants, debt maturities, incentives, liquidity terms, and the price already discounting a problem. For structure context, see this [comprehensive REIT primer](reits-guide.html).

## Interest-Rate Risk

Interest-rate risk is fundamental for REITs. When rates rise, shares can fall as bonds and other fixed-income choices offer competing income. Higher discount rates also reduce the present value investors assign to future property cash flows and income-producing real estate.

Rates also reach the income statement. REITs use debt, so refinancing maturing debt at higher rates raises interest expense and can pressure cash flow and distributions. [REITs are sensitive to changes in interest rates](how-interest-rates-affect-reit-returns.html) through prices, valuation, and financing costs.

[Mortgage REITs are especially rate-sensitive](reits-and-rising-rates-a-sector-playbook.html). They earn the spread between mortgage-asset income and borrowing costs; rate or yield-curve shifts can compress that spread and distributions directly. The relationship is not perfectly mechanical—quality REITs with growing rents can offset some pressure—but sensitivity to rates is a defining risk.

## Sector & Tenant Concentration

Concentration magnifies a bad outcome. An office REIT faces remote-work pressure; retail faces e-commerce pressure; hotels face travel downturns. When one property sector falls out of favor or confronts a secular decline, a focused REIT can suffer a sharper and longer decline than a diversified one.

Tenant concentration works at the property level. A few large tenants can account for much of the rent, so a default, bankruptcy, or non-renewal can remove a large piece of income and threaten both value and distributions. Net-lease REITs can have meaningful single-tenant exposure even when their leases appear durable. Geographic concentration adds exposure to one local economy, regulatory setting, or regional condition.

Concentration can help when the chosen sector thrives, but there is less room to absorb a setback. Diversifying across sectors, tenants, and markets reduces that specific risk.

## Leverage and Refinancing Risk

Debt amplifies returns and losses. Borrowing helps acquire properties and can improve returns when conditions cooperate, but a highly leveraged REIT has less cushion when property value or income falls. In severe cases, excessive leverage can threaten solvency.

Refinancing risk is leverage viewed through timing. When debt matures, it must be repaid or refinanced. Higher rates increase interest costs; tight credit markets can make refinancing difficult and force asset sales at unfavorable prices. Large near-term maturities are riskier than a well-staggered debt maturity ladder with fixed-rate debt. Floating-rate debt adds direct exposure to rising rates.

The balance sheet therefore matters as much as a property story: both the amount of debt and when it comes due can determine whether a REIT can withstand stress.

## Management & Conflict Risk

REITs are run by people, and capital-allocation decisions shape shareholder outcomes. Weak management can overpay for properties, mistime the cycle, overborrow, or manage a portfolio poorly even when the real estate market is healthy. Relevant experience and a record of sound decisions matter.

Conflict risk is especially important in externally managed and non-traded REITs. A separate manager paid acquisition fees may grow assets, and its own fees, when growth is not in shareholders’ interests. Related-party transactions may not be at arm’s length. High fees are a drag, and misaligned incentives can direct value to the manager rather than investors.

Assess governance, fee structure, conflicts, and whether incentives align. Strong governance and alignment mitigate this risk; weak governance amplifies it.

## Market & Liquidity Risk

A publicly traded REIT offers daily liquidity, but its share price moves with markets. It can [fall sharply during a broad sell-off](reit-bear-markets-history-and-lessons.html) because of sentiment, rate expectations, or economic fears even if properties and rents remain stable. That volatility is the cost of daily liquidity.

A non-traded REIT has the opposite problem: illiquidity. Its shares are not exchange-listed, and the redemption program is generally the principal exit route. Redemptions are commonly capped around **2%** of NAV per month and **5%** per quarter, and may be reduced or suspended, particularly when many investors want to exit during stress. Capital may be unavailable when it is needed.

Both traded and non-traded structures face economic and real-estate-cycle risk. Recessions, falling occupancy, and declining rents can reduce income and value. Traded REITs carry market volatility; non-traded REITs carry limited liquidity; both carry cycle risk.

## Key Takeaways

- Interest-rate risk can pressure REIT prices, valuations, borrowing costs, and mortgage-REIT spreads.
- Sector, tenant, and geographic concentration amplify losses; diversification dampens them.
- Leverage and near-term maturities can squeeze distributions or force asset sales in a high-rate or tight-credit environment.
- Risk management rests on diversification, quality, due diligence, and appropriate position sizing—not on eliminating risk.

## Mitigating REIT Risks

Risk cannot be eliminated, but it can be managed. Diversify across multiple REITs, sectors, and geographies; a REIT fund or ETF can do this automatically. Diversification does not prevent losses, but it reduces the impact of one issuer, sector, tenant, or region.

Favor quality: aligned management, conservative leverage, staggered debt maturities, well-leased properties, and distributions covered by operating cash flow. Then do the work—review management, the balance sheet, portfolio, coverage, and fees before investing.

Finally, size positions so one failed thesis cannot derail the broader plan. Match the structure to liquidity needs: capital that may be needed soon does not belong in an illiquid non-traded REIT.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are REITs risky investments?

REITs are real risk assets, not risk-free income substitutes. They can lose value, cut distributions, or underperform because of rates, concentration, debt and refinancing, management conflicts, market volatility, and illiquidity. They also offer income, diversification, and passive real-estate exposure. The right approach is realistic expectations, diligence, diversification, and a position size that fits the investor’s goals and risk tolerance.

### How do interest rates affect REITs?

Higher rates can make bonds more competitive for income, lower valuation multiples through higher discount rates, and increase borrowing costs on refinancing. Mortgage REITs also face potential compression of mortgage-asset spreads when rates or the yield curve change. Rent growth at a quality REIT can offset some pressure, so the relationship is material but not automatic.

### What is concentration risk in a REIT?

It is over-reliance on one sector, a few tenants, or one region. An office, retail, hotel, or other concentrated portfolio can be hit by its sector’s specific cycle. A tenant default or non-renewal can remove significant rent, while one city exposes a REIT to local economic and regulatory conditions.

### Why is leverage a risk for REITs?

Debt can improve returns when property values and income rise, but it magnifies losses when they fall. Heavily leveraged REITs have less cushion in a downturn and, in severe cases, can face solvency risk. Evaluate leverage along with the quality of the underlying properties and cash flow.

### What is refinancing risk for a REIT?

It is the risk that maturing debt must be refinanced at higher rates or when credit is unavailable. The result can be higher interest expense, lower distributable cash, or asset sales on poor terms. Concentrated near-term maturities and floating-rate debt create greater exposure; staggered fixed-rate maturities are more resilient.

### What management risks do REITs have?

Management can overpay, mistime cycles, take excessive debt, or manage assets poorly. In externally managed and non-traded REITs, acquisition fees, high fees, and related-party transactions can create conflicts if the manager benefits from growing the fund more than shareholders do. Review incentives, experience, governance, and fee terms.

### Are non-traded REITs riskier than traded REITs?

They carry a different risk mix. Listed REITs have daily liquidity and visible price volatility. Non-traded REITs may show smoother periodic NAVs, but they are illiquid, can carry higher fees, have valuation uncertainty, and generally offer only capped, suspendable redemptions. Neither structure is automatically safer; liquidity needs and risk tolerance are central.

### Can a REIT cut its distribution?

Yes. Vacancies, tenant defaults, lower rents, rising operating or borrowing costs, and compressed mortgage-REIT spreads can reduce income. A poorly covered distribution, or one supported by return of capital, debt, or new shares, is particularly vulnerable. Treat distributions as variable income, not as a bond coupon, and review FFO, AFFO, coverage, leverage, tenants, and yield.

### Do REITs lose value in a recession?

They can. Recessions may lift vacancies, pressure rents, and increase defaults, reducing income, property value, and traded share prices. The effect differs by sector: healthcare, some residential, and net lease with strong tenants can be more defensive than hotels, some retail, and office. Sound balance sheets and covered distributions help, but real estate is not recession-proof.

### How can I reduce the risk of investing in REITs?

Diversify across REITs, sectors, and geographies; favor strong management, sound balance sheets, well-leased properties, and covered distributions; conduct diligence on management, portfolio, coverage, and fees; and size both positions and the total REIT allocation appropriately. Match illiquid structures to a long-enough time horizon.

### Does diversification reduce REIT risk?

Yes, especially concentration and issuer-specific risk. Multiple REITs, sectors, tenants, and geographies reduce the damage from one default, region, or sector. A fund or ETF can provide this breadth. Diversification cannot remove broad market, rate, or cycle risk, so it should be combined with quality selection, due diligence, and sensible sizing.

### Are mortgage REITs riskier than equity REITs?

Generally, mortgage REITs carry more acute rate, leverage, credit, and yield-curve risks. They finance real estate through mortgages and mortgage-backed securities rather than collecting property rents, and often use substantial leverage to amplify spreads. Their higher yields can compensate for higher volatility and less stable distributions; they should not be treated as stable income.

### Is a high-yielding REIT more dangerous?

Often. A high yield can result from a falling share price, excessive leverage or rate risk, or a distribution not covered by operating cash flow. It can be funded by return of capital, debt, or new shares rather than sustainable operations. Review FFO, AFFO, payout coverage, tenant and lease durability, leverage, and the sector and rate outlook rather than chasing the headline yield.

### Should REITs be a large part of my portfolio?

Usually a measured allocation is more suitable than a dominant one. REITs have equity-like, interest-rate, and property-cycle risk, and distributions are not guaranteed. The appropriate size depends on goals, income needs, time horizon, and risk tolerance. Diversify the REIT sleeve and the broader portfolio; a financial adviser can help determine a proportionate allocation, and non-traded offerings require suitability review.

## Glossary

- **Interest-Rate Risk:** The risk rising rates pressure REIT prices and income.
- **Concentration Risk:** Over-reliance on one sector, tenant, or region.
- **Tenant Risk:** The risk a major tenant defaults or doesn't renew.
- **Sector Risk:** The risk a REIT's property sector underperforms.
- **Leverage:** The use of debt, which amplifies returns and losses.
- **Refinancing Risk:** The risk of refinancing maturing debt on worse terms.
- **Debt Maturity Ladder:** The schedule of when a REIT's debt comes due.
- **Management Risk:** The risk of poor capital allocation by a REIT's leaders.
- **Conflict of Interest:** Misaligned incentives, common in external management.
- **External Management:** A REIT run by a separate firm for fees, with potential conflicts.
- **Market Volatility:** Daily price swings affecting publicly traded REITs.
- **Illiquidity:** The inability to readily sell a non-traded REIT interest.
- **Redemption Program:** A non-traded REIT's limited, often-capped liquidity feature.
- **Distribution-Cut Risk:** The risk a REIT reduces or suspends its dividend.
- **Diversification:** Spreading exposure across REITs, sectors, and markets.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A rate-sensitive REIT earning mortgage interest spreads.
