# The Role of Your CPA in a 1031 Exchange

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing investors treat a 1031 exchange as a property transaction with a tax form at the end. It is the reverse. It is a tax strategy that happens to require property transactions, and that makes the CPA a central part of the team from the beginning.

Your CPA quantifies the tax you may defer, helps test whether the structure delivers full or partial deferral, calculates boot and basis, reports the transaction on Form 8824, and addresses state-tax issues. The qualified intermediary (QI) handles exchange mechanics; an agent handles the property transactions. The CPA tells you the tax result those mechanics and transactions create.

First-order thinking says that replacing a sold property is enough. Second-order thinking asks what cash, debt relief, basis, state rules, and tax reporting mean today and years from now. An exchange without sound CPA involvement can produce unwelcome boot, a miscalculated basis, or a reporting problem. This is educational information, not tax advice; use your own CPA and attorney for your circumstances.

## Why your CPA is central

The purpose of an exchange is to defer tax on a sale. Your CPA is the tax expert who puts a number on that benefit, advises on the structure, and works through the tax consequences of [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), basis, and reporting. Without that expertise, you are pursuing a tax strategy without the person best equipped to evaluate its tax outcome.

The role runs through the whole exchange. Before the sale, the CPA models the tax due in a taxable sale and helps assess whether, and how, an exchange makes sense. During the exchange, the CPA reviews boot, basis, and structure. Afterward, the CPA reports the exchange on the tax return and tracks the carried-over basis. That sequence matters because the tax record created now affects the replacement property’s depreciation and a later sale or exchange.

The CPA also brings the tax perspective to the rest of the group: the QI on mechanics, the agent on transaction details, and an attorney on legal structure. Tax deferral is the benefit being measured, so the CPA is the person who helps ensure the exchange is both intentionally structured and accurately documented.

## Modeling the tax you’re deferring

One of the CPA’s first contributions is a model of the tax due without an exchange. The source calculation is a four-layer tax stack:

- Federal capital gains tax, up to **20%**.
- [Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), up to **25%** on the recapture portion.
- The **3.8%** [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html) (NIIT), where applicable.
- State income tax.

The calculation depends on the gain, depreciation taken, income level, and state. It turns a general notion of “tax deferral” into a number. A tax bill that is often a third or more of gain has different weight when the model says, for example, **$300,000** is at stake. You can also use a [deferred-tax calculator](1031-exchange-calculator-estimate-deferred-tax.html) as an estimate, but the CPA’s work applies the facts of your situation.

That model informs the structure. A full exchange seeks to defer all gain. A partial exchange accepts some taxable boot. A taxable sale may be the right comparison point in some cases. The question is not simply how much tax can be deferred; it is what deferral is worth relative to your present need for liquidity. The CPA can model those choices before deadlines and closing mechanics narrow them.

The lasting point is simple: the four-layer tax is the number that turns an abstract benefit into a decision about whether and how to structure the exchange.

## Calculating boot and basis

Two technical calculations sit at the center of the CPA’s job.

**Boot** is non-like-kind value received in the exchange. It can be cash taken out, debt relief that is not replaced, or other non-qualifying property. It is taxable. The CPA calculates any boot so you can understand it and, if the goal is full deferral, avoid it. No boot supports full deferral; some boot produces partial deferral and can create current taxable gain.

**Basis** is the other calculation. In an exchange, basis generally carries from the relinquished property to the replacement property as carryover basis, adjusted for boot and additional investment. Additional investment can create excess basis. The CPA calculates the replacement basis because it affects future depreciation and the gain on a future sale.

This is not only a current-year issue. The boot calculation determines any recognized gain now. The basis calculation establishes the tax treatment that follows the replacement property. Carryover and excess basis can have different depreciation treatment under the applicable regulations, and the split is technical. Good work here helps the current exchange reach its intended deferral and sets the replacement property up with the right tax record.

## Reporting on Form 8824

The exchange is reported on **Form 8824 (Like-Kind Exchanges)** as part of the tax return for the year of the exchange. The form reports the relinquished and replacement properties, relevant dates, realized and recognized gain, deferred gain, and basis in the replacement property.

Accurate reporting matters. Errors can invite IRS scrutiny or misstate the deferral, basis, or recognized gain. Form 8824 ties together the boot and basis work in the official record used to claim the deferral. It also establishes information that carries into future depreciation and a later sale or exchange. Preparing the form is not clerical cleanup; it completes the tax side of the transaction.

## State tax and clawback considerations

Federal tax is not the whole picture. State income tax is one of the four layers the CPA models and the reporting rules can vary by state. Rates range from zero in states without income tax to over **13%** in high-tax states such as California. A CPA must consider the relinquished-property state, the replacement-property state, and the investor’s state of residence. A multi-state exchange adds another layer of reporting and tracking.

California’s clawback rule is a useful example. It is tracked using **Form FTB 3840**. It can tax deferred California-source gain when a replacement property acquired in the exchange is later sold in a taxable transaction, even if that replacement property is outside California. An investor who exchanges California property into out-of-state property should understand that later sale risk. It is easy to miss, and it is precisely the kind of obligation the CPA should track.

## Key Takeaways

- A 1031 exchange is a tax strategy, so the CPA quantifies, helps structure, and reports it.
- The CPA models federal capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state income tax to put the deferral benefit into concrete terms.
- The CPA calculates taxable boot and carried-over replacement basis, which govern current recognition and future tax treatment.
- The CPA prepares Form 8824 and handles state reporting and clawback rules, including California’s Form FTB 3840.

## Coordinating with the QI and advisor team

The CPA does not work in isolation. The CPA works with the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) on exchange mechanics and their tax implications, with the agent on details such as cost allocations and possible boot, and with [an attorney handling legal structuring](role-of-your-attorney-in-a-1031-exchange.html) on issues such as entity matters or a drop-and-swap.

That coordination puts the tax lens into each decision. If a proposed structure may create boot, the CPA can flag it. If an entity question affects the same-taxpayer rule, the CPA and attorney can address it together. The investor’s role is practical: bring the CPA in early and keep the CPA current on the intended structure and both transactions. That gives the team a better chance of aligning mechanics, structure, and intended tax deferral.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is my CPA important in a 1031 exchange?

The exchange is a tax strategy, and the CPA is the tax expert who quantifies the deferral, helps the structure target the intended result, calculates boot and basis, and prepares Form 8824. Good CPA involvement reduces the risk of unexpected boot, a wrong basis calculation, and reporting errors.

### What does my CPA do before the exchange?

Before the sale, the CPA models the four-layer tax stack—capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax—to quantify the potential deferral. The CPA can compare a full exchange, a partial exchange, and other options so you can weigh deferral against your needs before the transaction is underway.

### What is boot, and how does the CPA handle it?

Boot is taxable non-like-kind value received: cash removed, debt relief not replaced, or other non-qualifying property. The CPA calculates it, explains its effect, and analyzes whether a structure produces no boot/full deferral or some boot/partial deferral. Boot determines any current taxable gain.

### How does the CPA calculate my basis?

The CPA begins with carryover basis from the relinquished property, then accounts for boot and additional investment/excess basis. The result affects future depreciation and future gain. The carryover and excess-basis depreciation rules are technical, which is why the calculation belongs with the CPA.

### What is Form 8824?

Form 8824 is the IRS Like-Kind Exchanges form. It records the properties, dates, realized and recognized gain, deferred gain, and replacement basis for the exchange year. Correct reporting documents the deferral and creates the basis record that matters later.

### Does the CPA handle state taxes too?

Yes. State income tax is part of the deferral model, and state reporting can be required in more than one jurisdiction. The CPA also tracks state clawback rules, including California’s Form FTB 3840, and handles the added complexity of a multi-state exchange.

### What is the California clawback?

California’s rule can tax deferred California-source gain when the replacement property is later sold in a taxable transaction, including when the replacement property is outside the state. Form FTB 3840 tracks that deferred gain. The CPA should understand and monitor the obligation.

### When should I involve my CPA?

Early—ideally before you sell. That gives the CPA time to model the tax, advise on the structure, and identify problems before the exchange is set in motion. Waiting until tax filing time misses the value of the upfront work.

### Does the CPA work with my QI and agent?

Yes. The CPA coordinates with the QI on mechanics and tax implications, with the agent on tax-relevant transaction details such as cost allocation and boot, and with an attorney on structures that carry tax consequences.

### Can the CPA tell me if an exchange is worth it?

The CPA can model the tax due in a sale and compare a taxable sale, a full exchange, and a partial exchange. That comparison makes the trade-off clear: a full exchange reinvests everything to defer gain; a partial exchange takes some cash as taxable boot. It informs, but does not replace, your own decision about liquidity and goals.

### Does Baker 1031 provide tax advice?

No. Baker 1031 supplies replacement options, including DSTs, and exchange coordination. We work alongside the CPA on items such as DST **1099** reporting and carried-over basis, while tax modeling, boot and basis calculations, and Form 8824 remain the CPA’s responsibility.

### What information does my CPA need from the exchange?

Provide closing statements for the relinquished and replacement properties, sale and acquisition dates, QI exchange documents, any boot received such as cash or debt relief, added cash or debt on the replacement, and prior depreciation schedules. Those records support the recognized/deferred-gain calculation, replacement basis, and Form 8824.

### Can my CPA help me decide between a full and partial exchange?

Yes. The CPA can quantify the tax consequences of reinvesting everything for full deferral versus taking cash as taxable boot in a partial exchange. The comparison helps put the deferral benefit beside your liquidity need.

### Does my CPA handle depreciation on the replacement property?

Yes. Carryover basis generally continues the relinquished property’s depreciation schedule, while excess basis from added investment is depreciated as new property. This split-basis work affects ongoing tax treatment and the gain on a future sale.

### How much does CPA involvement cost relative to the benefit?

The source’s point is proportionality: a CPA fee is modest relative to a four-layer tax bill that may be a third or more of a substantial gain, and relative to the cost of avoidable boot, basis, or reporting errors. The fee and scope are individual decisions, but skipping qualified tax work simply to reduce a modest fee can create much larger mistakes.

## Glossary

- **CPA:** Your tax expert for exchange modeling, calculations, and reporting.
- **Four-Layer Tax Stack:** Capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax.
- **Boot:** Taxable non-like-kind value received in an exchange.
- **Carryover Basis:** Basis carried from the relinquished property to the replacement.
- **Excess Basis:** Additional basis from new investment beyond carryover basis, with its own depreciation.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form reporting a like-kind exchange.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** The recapture tax layer, up to 25%, that the CPA models and tracks.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** The 3.8% tax layer included in the deferral model where applicable.
- **California Clawback:** California’s rule, tracked on Form FTB 3840, that can tax deferred gain on a later taxable sale.
- **Form FTB 3840:** California’s form tracking deferred gain subject to its clawback rule.
- **Tax Modeling:** Calculating the tax due in a sale to quantify the deferral benefit.
- **Full Deferral:** Deferring all gain by structuring the exchange with no boot.
- **Partial Deferral:** Deferring most gain while recognizing taxable boot.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The exchange-mechanics handler who coordinates with the CPA on tax implications.
- **Multi-State Exchange:** An exchange across states that adds state-tax and reporting complexity.
- **Same-Taxpayer Rule:** A requirement that can matter in entity and titling questions.
