# Government-Leased (GSA) DSTs

I have been thinking about how easily a strong tenant name can short-circuit real-estate underwriting. “The U.S. government pays the rent” is a powerful fact. It is not, by itself, a complete investment case.

Government-leased, or GSA, DSTs let 1031 investors own real estate leased to the U.S. government or its agencies. This guide covers how they work, government tenant credit, lease terms and renewal risk, income stability, and the review of a specific offering. Demand and return characteristics are general and non-promissory; distributions are never guaranteed, and Baker 1031 does not provide tax or legal advice.

For the broader framework behind any DST offering, read the [in-depth DST pillar guide](dst-guide.html).

First-order thinking focuses on the government's very low default risk. Second-order thinking separates a tenant's ability to pay from its obligation to stay. A government tenant can be exceptionally creditworthy while still retaining the ability to let a lease end, exercise a soft-term termination right, relocate, or consolidate space.

## How GSA-Leased DSTs Work

A government-leased DST follows the same basic structure as another Delaware Statutory Trust, but the tenant is distinctive. The DST owns one or more buildings leased to a federal agency, often through the General Services Administration (GSA), the federal government's real-estate arm, or directly to the agency. The space might be a field office, courthouse, laboratory, processing center, or another government use.

The agency occupies the property and pays rent under its government lease. The DST distributes that rent to investors after expenses. Investors own fractional beneficial interests in the trust rather than directly managing the property.

For a federal lease, the rent is ultimately backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. The GSA may structure a lease around an agency's mission and space needs, and the building may be built or fitted out for that use. That is why the combination can offer passive ownership with unusually strong tenant credit.

Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, fractional beneficial interests in a qualifying DST are treated as like-kind real property for a 1031 exchange. An exchanger can reinvest sale proceeds into a government-leased DST, defer capital-gains tax subject to the applicable rules, and own passive high-credit real estate. If the DST is leveraged, its non-recourse financing can help replace debt from the relinquished property.

## Government Tenant Credit

The credit is the category's central appeal. A federal government tenant is among the strongest payers in the market. Unlike a corporate tenant, it does not face ordinary commercial default risk arising from declining sales, restructuring, or bankruptcy. While the agency occupies the property under an in-force lease, rent is exceptionally reliable.

That can eliminate much of the tenant-default risk that causes losses in private-tenant real estate. It is also why income-focused investors may accept modest, stable returns for government-backed income.

The qualification matters. The federal government is highly unlikely simply to stop paying rent on an in-force lease. That does not mean it must renew after the committed term. Very low default risk and renewal risk are different risks.

## Lease Terms & Renewal Risk

Federal leases are often long in stated duration, but a stated term can contain two very different parts. The firm term is non-cancelable: during that period the government is committed and cannot terminate early. A soft term is a later period during which the government may have an early-termination right, usually with advance notice.

That distinction makes the remaining firm term more important than the full stated term. A typical corporate net lease is usually binding for its full term. A government lease with several years of soft term may look long on paper while providing less truly committed income than the headline duration suggests.

At lease end, the government can choose not to renew. It can vacate, move to another building, or consolidate offices. Budget pressures, mission changes, and government-wide facility consolidation are decisions outside the landlord's control. If the agency leaves, the property can be vacant and income can be interrupted.

For that reason, renewal and continuation risk—not default risk—is the central risk in government-leased real estate. The key question is whether the agency remains, not whether it can make an in-force rent payment.

## Income Stability

Government-leased DSTs can provide highly dependable income during the committed lease term. The strength of the tenant's credit is the practical reason [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) investors seeking passive income may consider the category.

But the stability is bounded by the lease. Beyond the firm term, and especially at lease end, continued income depends on renewal or continued occupancy. If the agency vacates, the DST may need to re-lease the building or sell it. Government space can be specialized for its tenant, which may make backfilling it more difficult.

Like all DSTs, distributions are projected, not guaranteed, and the interest is illiquid. The appropriate view is dependable income within the committed, occupied period—not unconditional income for the stated lease term.

### Key Takeaways

- A GSA-leased DST owns real estate leased to a federal agency, often through the General Services Administration, and rent on a federal lease is ultimately backed by the U.S. government.
- Government tenant credit creates very low default risk and is the main attraction of the category.
- Federal leases can pair a firm, non-cancelable term with a soft, early-terminable term. Renewal and continuation risk, not default, is the primary risk.
- Review firm and soft terms, the space's mission-criticality, renewal likelihood, and location before investing.

## Evaluating a GSA DST

Start with the lease. How much firm term remains? How much soft term carries early-termination rights? What do the termination, renewal, and escalation provisions say? A long remaining firm term generally provides more income certainty than a lease that has moved into terminable years.

Then assess whether the space is mission-critical. A courthouse, a specialized laboratory, a secure processing center, or a facility an agency has spent heavily to fit out can be harder and more expensive to replicate than generic office space. The agency's mission, its need for the location, and the cost of relocating are useful clues to renewal likelihood.

Location still matters even with a government tenant. It affects the property's backfill or sale prospects if the agency leaves. The DST structure itself requires review as well: sponsor record, fees, projected hold, projected distributions, and any non-recourse debt. None of those projections is a guarantee.

The discipline is to weigh exceptional credit against the possibility of discontinuity. A long firm term and mission-critical, purpose-built space can support durable income. They should not be used to ignore the lease's actual exit and renewal terms.

## GSA DSTs in a 1031 Exchange

Government-leased DSTs are often used as replacement property in a 1031 exchange. After a sale of appreciated investment real estate, the exchanger generally has 45 days to identify replacement property and 180 days to close, using a [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html). A GSA DST can be useful under that timetable because it is a pre-packaged, already-acquired property that can be identified and closed quickly. Its government-backed rent and very low tenant-default risk can also make it a reliable high-credit anchor on an exchanger's 45-day identification list.

The DST can also help with value and debt replacement. A leveraged GSA DST carries non-recourse financing that may replace mortgage debt from the relinquished property. A debt-free DST can suit an exchanger with no debt to replace, without introducing mortgage [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html). The exchange details are technical and should be addressed with a qualified intermediary, CPA, and attorney.

Some exchangers use a government-leased DST as a lower-volatility, credit-quality anchor in a diversified replacement portfolio, alongside DSTs in other sectors. That can balance government-lease renewal risk against the tenant-credit risks found elsewhere. It remains an allocation decision, not an assurance of tax results, income, or value.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a government-leased (GSA) DST?

A government-leased, often called GSA, DST is a Delaware Statutory Trust that holds one or more buildings leased to a U.S. government agency, typically through the GSA or directly to a federal agency. The building can house a field office, courthouse, laboratory, or processing center. Investors own fractional beneficial interests, and rent paid by the agency is distributed after expenses. For a federal lease, rent is ultimately backed by the U.S. government, which gives the income very low tenant-default risk while the lease is in force and the agency occupies the space. Under Revenue Ruling 2004-86, qualifying DST interests can be like-kind replacement property in a 1031 exchange, allowing passive high-credit real-estate ownership and potential capital-gains tax deferral subject to the rules.

### What is the GSA?

The General Services Administration procures, leases, and manages real estate and other resources for the U.S. government. When a federal agency needs office, court, laboratory, or other space, the GSA often handles the real-estate transaction and lease administration. A property may also be leased directly to a federal agency. Some government-leased properties have a state or local government tenant instead; their rent carries the credit of that government, not necessarily the U.S. government. Confirm the actual tenant and whether the lease is federal.

### Why is government tenant credit so strong?

For a federal lease, rent is ultimately backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. The government does not ordinarily face the corporate risks of declining sales, restructuring, or bankruptcy, so it is extremely unlikely simply to stop paying rent during an in-force lease. That creates very low tenant-default risk and can support modest, stable returns. It does not eliminate the chance that the agency chooses not to renew when the lease ends.

### What is renewal risk in a GSA DST?

Renewal risk is the possibility that the government does not renew and vacates when the lease expires. It can relocate or consolidate space for budget, mission, or facility reasons outside the landlord's control. A specialized or purpose-built building can be harder and more costly to re-lease after a departure. Focus on remaining committed term and renewal likelihood, not merely government credit.

### Are GSA DSTs 1031-eligible?

Yes, qualifying fractional beneficial interests in a DST are treated as direct interests in like-kind real property under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86. A seller of appreciated investment real estate can use a GSA DST as replacement property and potentially defer capital gain and depreciation recapture. If leveraged, its non-recourse financing can assist with debt replacement. The exchange must meet the 45-day identification and 180-day closing rules through a qualified intermediary. Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice; confirm the details with a tax advisor.

### What is a firm term versus a soft term in a government lease?

The firm term is the non-cancelable period during which the government is committed to the lease. A soft term is a later period during which it may terminate early, usually with notice. The firm term, rather than the full stated lease duration, is the more certain income period. A property with substantial firm term remaining offers more certainty than one operating in soft, terminable years, unlike the usual fully binding corporate net lease.

### What does “mission-critical” space mean and why does it matter?

Mission-critical space is essential to the occupying agency's work. It can be a courthouse, secure facility, laboratory, data or processing center, or heavily fitted-out location. Such space is harder or more expensive to replicate, may be tied to a location the agency must serve, and can be more likely to be renewed than generic office space. Mission-criticality helps evaluate whether the high-credit tenant is likely to stay beyond the committed term.

### How stable is the income from a government-leased DST?

It can be highly stable while the government occupies the property under an in-force committed lease. That stability is bounded by the firm term and renewal risk. If the agency leaves, the DST can face vacancy, specialized-space re-leasing challenges, or a sale. Distributions are projected, not guaranteed, and the DST is illiquid. Judge income stability by remaining committed term and renewal likelihood, not the stated term alone.

### How long do GSA DSTs typically last?

Like many DSTs, a government-leased DST commonly has a projected hold of roughly five to seven years, though sponsor plans, lease structure, and market conditions can make it shorter or longer. During the hold, the trust owns the property, collects rent, and distributes net income. At sale, investors receive their share of proceeds and may complete another like-kind exchange or cash out and recognize gain. Firm and soft terms can affect value and salability at sale. There is little or no secondary market, so plan to hold through the projected term and review the offering documents.

### Are distributions from a GSA DST guaranteed?

No. The DST distributes net rental income after expenses and debt service. Government credit makes income highly reliable during the committed term, but it is not an investor guarantee. A soft-term termination, nonrenewal, higher expenses, leveraged debt-service pressure, vacancy, re-leasing period, or sale can reduce or suspend distributions. Any stated distribution rate is an estimate based on assumptions, not a promise. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and an illiquid interest cannot simply be sold if income disappoints.

### How does a GSA DST compare to a corporate net-lease DST?

The trade-off is credit versus term certainty. A corporate net-lease, or NNN, DST usually has a tenant responsible for taxes, insurance, and maintenance under a binding long lease, but that tenant can experience credit deterioration, default, or bankruptcy. A GSA DST has exceptionally strong government-backed credit but can include soft, early-terminable years and a nonrenewal risk. Neither is universally better. Compare the actual tenant credit and committed term in each offering.

### Who can invest in a government-leased DST?

Government-leased DSTs are typically Regulation D private-placement securities for accredited investors. Accreditation is often based on income over $200,000 individually or $300,000 jointly in each of the past two years with an expectation of the same, or net worth over $1 million excluding a primary residence, among other qualifying criteria. Investors work through a broker-dealer and a suitability review of finances, goals, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. DST interests are not sold on an exchange or to the general public; they have minimums and a subscription process. Confirm current eligibility and suitability before committing capital to an illiquid, longer-term holding.

### Does a government-leased DST use debt?

It depends on the offering. Some GSA DSTs are leveraged with non-recourse mortgage debt, which can replace relinquished-property debt in a 1031 exchange and can amplify both returns and risk. Others are debt-free, avoiding refinancing and interest-rate risk and offering a lower-risk profile, but they do not replace relinquished debt and may offer more modest returns. Match leveraged or debt-free structure to debt-replacement needs and risk tolerance, and read the offering documents for terms.

### Can the government just leave the building?

Yes. It can leave at the end of the committed term and sometimes earlier under a soft term with notice. Budget constraints, mission changes, relocation, and government-wide consolidation can drive the decision. The result can be vacancy, and specialized space can be harder and costlier to backfill. That is why firm term, renewal likelihood, mission-criticality, and backfill prospects are central to a GSA DST review.

## Glossary

**Government-Leased (GSA) DST:** A DST holding real estate leased to a U.S. government agency.

**General Services Administration (GSA):** The federal agency that leases and manages government real estate.

**Government Tenant Credit:** Rent ultimately backed by the U.S. government's full faith and credit.

**Firm Term:** The non-cancelable period of a government lease.

**Soft Term:** A later lease period the government may terminate early.

**Renewal Risk:** The risk the government won't renew and vacates at lease end.

**Mission-Critical Space:** Space essential to an agency that is likely to be renewed.

**Early-Termination Right:** A government option to end a lease before its stated term.

**Backfill:** Re-leasing space to a new tenant if the government leaves.

**Income Stability:** Reliable rent during the committed lease term.

**Committed Term:** The remaining firm period the tenant is bound to.

**Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A trust holding real estate in 1031-eligible fractional interests.

**Rev. Rul. 2004-86:** The IRS ruling treating DST interests as like-kind real property.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income/net-worth thresholds for Reg D offerings.

**Suitability Review:** The broker-dealer's check that a DST fits the investor.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** DST mortgage debt that replaces a 1031 investor's relinquished debt.
