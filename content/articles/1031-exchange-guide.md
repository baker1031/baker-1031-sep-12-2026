# 1031 Exchanges: The 2026 Definitive Guide

I keep seeing investors focus on the tax bill at closing and then rush into a replacement they would not have bought without the exchange clock. The 1031 is useful because it can keep more equity invested. It is dangerous when the deferral becomes the only reason to buy.

A 1031 exchange, named for Section 1031 of the tax code, can defer the tax on a sale of real property held for investment or business use when the proceeds are reinvested in like-kind real estate. The gain carries into the replacement property’s basis. Done over a lifetime, the structure can preserve a pre-tax base for compounding rather than paying tax after every sale.

First-order thinking is, “Defer **100%** of my tax today.” Second-order thinking is, “Does the replacement justify its price, leverage, liquidity, tenant risk, and management burden even after I take the deferral out of the decision?” The rules make the exchange possible. The replacement quality determines whether it was worthwhile.

As of **July 11, 2026**, Section 1031 remains available for qualifying real property under current federal law. The result remains dependent on the property, taxpayer, timing, transaction documents, and then-current authority. Confirm the current rule with tax advisers before acting.

## 01 · What a 1031 Exchange Is

An outright sale of appreciated investment property can bring federal capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, the net investment income tax, and state tax. Together, those layers can take roughly a **quarter to a third** of profit. A 1031 exchange defers the qualifying tax by changing the transaction sequence: a qualified intermediary holds the proceeds and uses them for qualifying replacement real estate.

The deferred gain does not vanish at the exchange. It moves into the new property as a reduced, carryover basis. The benefit is that more equity stays in the investment base. Repeat the process over an investing life, and hold the final property until death, and heirs may receive a stepped-up basis that removes the deferred gain for income-tax purposes. That is the strategy often called “swap till you drop.”

This guide is the common foundation for every exchange. The specialized routes discussed here include [Delaware Statutory Trusts](dst-guide.html) for passive ownership, [mineral and royalty interests](mineral-rights-1031-guide.html) where perpetual interests qualify as real property, and the related [Opportunity Zone](opportunity-zones-guide.html) alternative. Opportunity Zones are not 1031 exchanges; the source describes them as an option that can defer gain and offer tax-free appreciation over **ten years**.

## 02 · What Qualifies

Four conditions define the basic eligibility analysis:

1. **Real property.** Since **2018**, only real property qualifies. Equipment, vehicles, and other personal property do not. The source also notes the **2017** tax law that limited like-kind treatment to real property.
2. **Held for investment or business.** A primary residence does not qualify under Section 1031; that is generally addressed through the Section 121 home-sale exclusion. Dealer inventory held for resale does not qualify either.
3. **Like-kind.** For real estate, this is broad. A rental house can be exchanged for an office building, raw land, a net-lease store, a DST interest, or qualifying mineral rights. Type and grade do not control; the investment-real-property character does.
4. **U.S. property.** Domestic real estate is not like-kind to foreign real estate.

## 03 · The Core Rules: Equal-or-Up and Boot

For full deferral, use the equal-or-up rule:

- Acquire replacement property worth at least the net sale price of the relinquished property.
- Reinvest all equity.
- Replace debt paid off with new debt or your own additional cash.

Fall short on any of those and the shortage is boot. **Cash boot** is sale proceeds retained rather than reinvested. **Mortgage boot** is debt relief: the replacement loan is smaller than the old debt and no cash fills the gap.

Boot does not invalidate the whole exchange. It is taxable up to the amount of gain, while the rest of the qualifying gain remains deferred. The boot-and-deferral calculator is intended to turn this into the investor’s own figures by estimating realized gain, taxable boot, deferred gain, and whether the plan is fully deferred.

Go equal-or-up in value, reinvest all equity, and replace debt. Miss any of the three, and the shortfall is taxable boot.

## 04 · The Deadlines: 45 and 180 Days

Two hard clocks begin when the relinquished-property sale closes. Identify replacement property in writing within **45 days**. Close within **180 days**. Weekends and holidays do not extend either clock.

There is an important date trap: the exchange period ends on the earlier of **180 days** or the due date for the sale-year tax return. A late-year sale can therefore require an extension to keep the full **180-day** period. The source’s date calculator is designed to take the relinquished closing date, show both deadlines, and flag a filing date that could shorten the exchange period.

## 05 · Identification Rules: 3-Property, 200%, and 95%

Within the first **45 days**, replacement candidates must be identified in writing under one of three rules:

- **3-Property Rule:** Identify up to **three** properties of any total value. This is the simplest and most common rule.
- **200% Rule:** Identify any number of properties provided their combined value does not exceed **200%** of the value of the relinquished property.
- **95% Rule:** Identify any number and any value, but acquire at least **95%** of the total value identified.

The deadline is absolute. Identify thoughtfully, with backups, rather than hoping a single preferred deal survives the full period.

## 07 · The Qualified Intermediary and Process

The operational rule is uncompromising: do not take possession of sale proceeds. If the money reaches the investor’s hands or bank account, the IRS can treat that as constructive receipt and the exchange fails.

The qualified intermediary (QI) is an independent third party engaged **before** the sale closes. It holds proceeds and uses them to fund the replacement. The normal sequence is:

1. Engage the QI and sign the exchange agreement before closing.
2. Sell the relinquished property; proceeds go directly to the QI.
3. Identify replacement property in writing within **45 days** under one of the identification rules.
4. Close within **180 days**, or by the earlier tax-return due date, with QI funds used for the purchase.
5. Report the exchange on **Form 8824** with the tax return for the sale year.

## 08 · Types of Exchanges

- **Delayed or forward exchange:** Sell first and buy later within the deadlines. This is the standard structure and accounts for the vast majority of exchanges.
- **Reverse exchange:** Acquire the replacement first, parking it with an exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT), then sell the relinquished property. It can help in competitive markets but is more complex and costly.
- **Improvement or construction exchange:** Use exchange funds to build on or improve the replacement property within **180 days**.
- **Simultaneous exchange:** Both sides close the same day. It is the original form of exchange and is now rare.

## 09 · Choosing Your Replacement Strategy

Rules are often easier than replacement selection. The clock can make a mediocre asset look acceptable. Resist that. The source presents these routes:

- **DST:** Passive, fractional institutional real estate that can be prepared to close quickly against a deadline.
- **Mineral and royalty interests:** Potentially high-yield oil-and-gas interests; qualifying perpetual interests may be like-kind real property.
- **Opportunity Zones:** Not a 1031, but a related alternative for deferring gain and, under the source’s description, earning tax-free growth after **ten years**.
- **Direct ownership and tenants-in-common (TIC) interests:** Routes for investors seeking a particular asset or direct ownership structure.

The source includes a four-question strategy selector to help compare these routes. It is a starting point, not a recommendation. Underwrite the price, liquidity, income, leverage, sponsor or tenant, and the risks in the specific offering or property.

## 10 · Taxes: Recapture, Basis, and “Swap Till You Drop”

A 1031 exchange can defer depreciation recapture as well as capital gain. If sold outright, depreciation recapture may be taxed at up to **25%** federally. The deferred gain and reduced basis carry into the replacement property, so the tax is deferred, not erased by the exchange itself.

For many long-term investors, the intended endgame is not a taxable cash sale. It is repeated exchanges followed by a stepped-up basis at death. The heir’s basis can reset to fair-market value, eliminating the deferred gain for income-tax purposes. That is why deferred tax, low basis, and estate planning need to be considered together.

## 11 · Common Mistakes and Pitfalls

- **Touching the money:** Constructive receipt can end the exchange. Use the QI.
- **Missing the 45-day identification:** This is a common failure and the deadline is absolute.
- **Buying down:** A cheaper replacement or insufficient debt replacement creates taxable boot.
- **Same-taxpayer problem:** The entity that sells must be the entity that buys. Partnership drop-and-swap issues require advance planning.
- **Related-party rules:** A related-party exchange generally needs a **two-year** holding period.
- **Wrong property type:** Primary residences, fix-and-flips held as dealer inventory, and partnership interests do not qualify.

## 12 · Frequently Asked Questions

### How much tax does a 1031 exchange defer?

Potentially all qualifying federal capital gains, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax when the investor follows the equal-or-up rule and reinvests all equity. Boot is taxed; the rest remains deferred.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange on my primary home?

No. A primary residence is not held for investment or business use. It may qualify for Section 121 instead. A mixed-use property can have an eligible investment portion.

### What is the difference between the 45-day and 180-day deadlines?

The **45-day** deadline is for written identification. The **180-day** deadline is for closing, unless the sale-year tax-return due date arrives first. Both run from sale closing and cannot be extended.

### Do I have to reinvest 100% of the proceeds?

To defer **100%** of qualifying tax, reinvest all equity, replace debt, and acquire property costing at least the net sale price. You can reinvest less, but the shortfall is taxable boot.

### Is the 1031 exchange going away?

As of **July 11, 2026**, IRS guidance continues to describe like-kind exchanges for qualifying real property. Proposals and legislation can change the analysis, and the result depends on current authority and transaction facts. Have a CPA and attorney confirm the rule before acting.

## 13 · Glossary

- **Like-Kind Property:** Investment or business real property broadly interchangeable with other U.S. investment real estate.
- **Boot:** Cash or non-like-kind value received in an exchange, taxable up to gain.
- **Equal-or-Up Rule:** Replace at least the value and debt given up and reinvest all equity to fully defer.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The independent party holding proceeds to prevent constructive receipt.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Access to sale proceeds that disqualifies the exchange.
- **Identification Period:** The **45** days to identify replacement property in writing.
- **Exchange Period:** The **180** days, or earlier tax-return due date, to close on replacement property.
- **Reverse Exchange:** Acquire the replacement before selling the relinquished property.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on previously deducted depreciation, also deferred by a qualifying 1031 exchange.
