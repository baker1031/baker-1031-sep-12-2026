# Investing in REITs With a Self-Directed IRA

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing the same account question framed as though the wrapper makes the investment safe. It does not. It can change the tax treatment of a REIT dividend, but it does not change the price paid, the liquidity of the holding, or the need for a suitable allocation.

First-order thinking is that an IRA is simply a place to avoid the annual tax bill on REIT income. Second-order thinking asks what that tax benefit costs, what account is actually needed, and whether an illiquid holding can coexist with future distributions and a diversified retirement plan.

REITs pay mostly ordinary-income dividends, which can make a tax-advantaged retirement account attractive. A regular IRA or 401(k) can shelter that income. A self-directed IRA is mainly relevant when an investor wants a non-traded or private REIT that a standard brokerage cannot custody. This is educational information, not tax advice; confirm the current rules and your own facts with a tax advisor.

If you are weighing an unlisted vehicle, start with the [in-depth REIT guide](reits-guide.html) before deciding where it belongs.

## REITs in an IRA: The Tax Case

The tax question begins with [how REIT dividends are taxed](reit-taxation-how-reit-dividends-are-taxed.html) in a regular account. A REIT pays little or no corporate tax because it distributes most of its income under the **90%** rule. As a result, most of its dividends are non-qualified and taxed at ordinary-income rates rather than the lower qualified-dividend rate that applies to many stock dividends. For a high earner, that can make a REIT materially less tax-efficient in a taxable account than long-term capital gains.

An IRA or 401(k) changes the annual timing of that tax. In a traditional IRA, dividends are not taxed as received; they compound tax-deferred and distributions in retirement are taxed at ordinary rates. In a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals are entirely tax-free. Either account can remove the yearly drag of ordinary-income taxation and improve after-tax compounding over time.

This is an asset-location decision: tax-inefficient ordinary-income assets may belong in a tax-advantaged account. It is not a universal answer. The tax wrapper does not make every REIT attractive, and it does not eliminate the need to evaluate the underlying business, price, fees, leverage, liquidity, and fit with the broader portfolio.

## Traded vs. Non-Traded REITs in an IRA

The practical dividing line is simple. A standard brokerage IRA can already hold publicly traded REITs, REIT mutual funds, and REIT ETFs just like other listed securities. If the aim is to shelter dividends from a listed REIT, no self-directed structure or special custodian is usually required. That covers the large majority of REIT investing.

A self-directed IRA (SDIRA) becomes relevant when the desired REIT is non-traded or private and a standard brokerage cannot custody it. Because the offering is not exchange-listed, the account needs an alternative-asset custodian that can hold the interest. The account choice follows the investment: traded REITs fit a regular brokerage IRA; non-traded and private REITs typically require an SDIRA.

That distinction should not be mistaken for a risk distinction. A non-traded or private REIT remains illiquid, periodically valued at net asset value (NAV), and subject to a suitability review regardless of the account that holds it. The IRA is a tax wrapper, not an exit strategy.

> You do not need a self-directed IRA for a publicly traded REIT. The self-directed structure matters when the REIT itself is non-traded or private.

## Avoiding UBIT Issues

Another common concern is [unrelated business income tax (UBIT) on REITs in retirement accounts](reits-and-ubit-in-retirement-accounts.html). UBIT can apply even inside a tax-exempt account when it receives certain unrelated business taxable income (UBTI). For typical REIT investors, the important point is that ordinary REIT dividends are generally portfolio income, which is excluded from UBTI. Publicly traded REIT shares in an IRA therefore typically do not generate UBIT.

The risk is narrower and depends on structure. UBIT, and the related unrelated debt-financed income (UDFI) rules, can arise when an IRA directly owns and operates a business or holds debt-financed real estate through a structure producing UBTI. Plain REIT-share ownership is different: the REIT owns and operates the real estate, while the IRA receives portfolio-type dividends. That generally insulates the shareholder from operating and debt complexity at the entity level.

“Generally” matters here. The rules are technical and a particular alternative-real-estate structure may differ. Confirm the treatment with a tax advisor before assuming an investment will not generate UBIT.

## Setting Up a Self-Directed IRA

An investor who wants a non-traded or private REIT in a retirement account generally starts by selecting a qualified SDIRA custodian. These firms specialize in non-traditional assets such as private real estate, non-traded REITs, and private placements. The account can often be funded by a direct transfer or a proper rollover from an existing IRA or eligible retirement account, which is generally a non-taxable event when handled correctly.

Once funded, the IRA—not the investor personally—makes the investment, and the custodian holds the REIT interest in the account’s name. The broader investment menu comes with additional responsibilities: avoid prohibited transactions, including self-dealing and dealings with disqualified persons; follow contribution and distribution rules; and understand the custodial fees that alternative-asset administrators typically charge.

The SDIRA does not alter the REIT. A non-traded REIT remains illiquid, NAV-priced, and suitability-gated. Before moving capital, coordinate the mechanics with the custodian and tax advisor rather than treating a rollover as routine paperwork.

### Key Takeaways

- Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, so an IRA can shelter a tax-inefficient income stream from current tax.
- A standard brokerage IRA can hold traded REITs; an SDIRA is mainly for non-traded or private REITs a regular brokerage cannot custody.
- Ordinary REIT dividends are generally excluded from UBTI, making UBIT and UDFI narrower, structure-specific concerns.
- The **20%** Section 199A deduction is unavailable inside an IRA, but sheltering ordinary dividend income can still favor the IRA for many high earners.

## The 199A Deduction Trade-Off

The most important tax nuance is the Section 199A deduction. In a taxable account, qualified REIT dividends are eligible for a [**20%** deduction under Section 199A](reit-dividends-and-the-20-percent-qbi-deduction.html). That lowers the effective top federal rate on those dividends to roughly **29.6%**. The source material notes that the deduction was made permanent by the **2025** OBBBA legislation.

Inside an IRA, the deduction cannot be used because the income is not currently taxed. An IRA owner therefore gives up the taxable-account 199A deduction on those same dividends.

That does not automatically make the taxable account better. For many high earners, sheltering the full ordinary-income dividend and compounding tax-deferred in a traditional IRA—or tax-free in a Roth—will usually outweigh the lost deduction. The answer depends on the tax bracket, time horizon, and traditional-versus-Roth choice. Run that comparison with a tax advisor rather than treating the deduction as a one-line decision.

> The **20%** Section 199A deduction is lost inside an IRA, but sheltering the full ordinary REIT dividend can still be the more valuable trade-off for many high earners.

## Suitability Considerations

Tax efficiency is only one part of suitability. For a non-traded or private REIT in an SDIRA, the underlying investment is still a longer-term, illiquid, NAV-priced security, usually available to accredited or otherwise suitable investors through a broker-dealer following a suitability review. The IRA changes the tax wrapper; it does not reduce the investment’s illiquidity or risk.

Retirement-account mechanics add their own constraints. Required minimum distributions (RMDs) eventually apply to traditional IRAs. If a large share of the account is tied up in an illiquid non-traded REIT, a required distribution can create a timing problem when redemption is not readily available. A public REIT can normally be sold to raise cash. A non-traded REIT may have a redemption program that is capped or suspended, and it cannot generally be sold on demand.

Concentration matters as well. An IRA should not be over-allocated to one illiquid holding. Weigh the investor’s goals, horizon, risk tolerance, liquid reserves, broader retirement plan, and traditional-versus-Roth tax result. The account and the investment both have to fit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I hold REITs in an IRA?

Yes. Publicly traded REITs, REIT mutual funds, and REIT ETFs fit in a standard brokerage IRA. The account shelters the mostly ordinary-income dividends from current tax: a traditional IRA defers tax until distribution and a Roth IRA can provide tax-free qualified withdrawals. A non-traded or private REIT generally needs an SDIRA with an alternative-asset custodian. The account needed depends on whether the REIT is traded or unlisted. This is educational information, not tax advice.

### Why hold REITs in an IRA instead of a taxable account?

Because REITs distribute most income under the **90%** rule and pay little or no corporate tax, most dividends are non-qualified ordinary income in a taxable account. That can create a larger annual tax drag for high earners than long-term capital gains. A traditional IRA defers that tax and a Roth IRA can make qualified withdrawals tax-free, supporting after-tax compounding. This is asset location. The **20%** Section 199A deduction is a countervailing taxable-account consideration.

### Do I need a self-directed IRA to hold REITs?

Not for most REITs. Listed REITs, funds, and ETFs can be held in an ordinary brokerage IRA. An SDIRA is for non-traded or private REITs that are not exchange-listed and cannot be held by a standard broker. Its specialist custodian can administer alternative assets such as private real estate and private placements.

### What is a self-directed IRA?

An SDIRA is an IRA with a broader permitted investment menu, administered by a custodian equipped to hold alternatives. You direct the investment choices, subject to the same core IRA rules on contributions, distributions, and traditional-versus-Roth tax treatment. It also creates added responsibilities: prohibited transactions and disqualified persons must be avoided, and alternative-asset custodial fees are common. It is the vehicle for a non-traded or private REIT, not a requirement for public REITs.

### Are REIT dividends taxed in an IRA?

Not when received. In a traditional IRA, dividends compound tax-deferred and are taxed at ordinary-income rates when distributions are taken in retirement. In a Roth IRA, qualified withdrawals, including accumulated dividends and growth, are tax-free if Roth rules are met. That removes the annual taxable-account drag. The trade-off is that the **20%** Section 199A deduction is unavailable inside the IRA; for many high earners, full sheltering still outweighs it. This is educational information, not tax advice.

### What is UBIT and does it apply to REITs in an IRA?

UBIT is tax on UBTI earned by an otherwise tax-exempt account. Ordinary REIT dividends are generally portfolio income excluded from UBTI, so a typical IRA holding of REIT shares does not create UBIT. UBIT and UDFI can arise more readily from a directly operated business or certain debt-financed real-estate structures. The REIT owns and operates the property; the IRA shareholder usually receives portfolio dividends. Confirm a particular structure with a tax advisor.

### Do publicly traded REITs create UBIT in an IRA?

Generally, no. A traded REIT’s ordinary dividends are normally passive portfolio income excluded from UBTI. The operating and debt-related property complexity resides in the REIT, not in the IRA shareholder. Unusual structures can differ, so tax-advisor confirmation remains appropriate, but typical traded-REIT ownership is usually a clean retirement-account holding from a UBIT perspective.

### How do I set up a self-directed IRA for non-traded REITs?

Choose an SDIRA custodian that handles alternative assets, open the account, and fund it—often with a direct transfer or proper rollover from an existing IRA or eligible retirement account. The IRA then makes the investment and the custodian holds the interest in the account’s name. Avoid prohibited transactions and disqualified persons, follow contribution and distribution rules, and understand fees. The non-traded REIT remains illiquid, NAV-priced, and suitability-gated. Work with the custodian and tax advisor; this is educational, not tax advice.

### Can I use the 199A deduction on REIT dividends in an IRA?

No. The **20%** Section 199A deduction applies in taxable accounts, where it can lower the effective top federal rate on qualified REIT dividends to roughly **29.6%**; the source notes the **2025** OBBBA made the deduction permanent. IRA income is not currently taxable, so there is nothing against which to apply the deduction. The comparison depends on bracket, horizon, and account type, but many high earners still benefit more from IRA sheltering. Ask a tax advisor to run the numbers.

### Is it better to hold REITs in a traditional or Roth IRA?

Both shelter current dividends, but the tax timing differs. A traditional IRA defers tax and may suit an investor expecting a lower later rate or valuing the upfront contribution deduction. A Roth is funded with after-tax dollars, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free and may suit an investor expecting a higher future rate or seeking tax-free retirement income. REIT income can make the Roth appealing, but traditional-IRA RMDs and the different Roth rules matter. The choice turns on tax outlook, liquidity planning, and goals.

### Are non-traded REITs in an IRA still illiquid?

Yes. A non-traded REIT is not listed on an exchange, is periodically priced at NAV, and usually offers liquidity only through a redemption program that can be capped or suspended. It cannot generally be sold on demand in either an IRA or a taxable account. In a traditional IRA, that can conflict with RMDs if too much of the account is illiquid. The IRA changes tax treatment, not liquidity. Plan concentration and cash reserves accordingly.

### What are required minimum distributions and how do they affect REITs in an IRA?

RMDs are mandatory annual withdrawals from traditional IRAs and similar accounts once the applicable age is reached. With traded REITs, shares can normally be sold to fund a distribution. With an illiquid non-traded REIT, an investor may have difficulty generating cash in a given year if a large portion of the account cannot be redeemed. Keep sufficient liquid assets in the IRA or across accounts, and verify current RMD rules with a tax advisor. Roth IRAs have different RMD rules.

### Who is a self-directed IRA REIT investment suitable for?

It is generally for longer-term, accredited or otherwise suitable investors who understand the trade-offs and do not need ready liquidity from that part of their retirement savings. The underlying REIT is illiquid, NAV-priced, and sold through a broker-dealer following a suitability review. It is less suitable for someone who may need ready access to the capital or who would be over-concentrated in one illiquid asset. RMD planning, liquidity elsewhere, risk tolerance, and traditional-versus-Roth treatment all matter.

### What are prohibited transactions in a self-directed IRA?

They are dealings tax rules forbid inside an SDIRA, and a violation can potentially disqualify the IRA and trigger taxes and penalties. The rules seek to keep the account at arm’s length from the owner and disqualified persons, generally including the owner, spouse, lineal ascendants and descendants, and entities they control. Examples include self-dealing, buying or selling between the IRA and a disqualified person, improper lending or borrowing, and personal use of IRA-owned property. Passive, custodian-held REIT ownership usually avoids these issues, but the rules are technical.

## Glossary

- **REIT:** A company that owns, operates, or finances income-producing real estate.
- **IRA:** An individual retirement account offering tax-advantaged investing.
- **Self-Directed IRA (SDIRA):** An IRA that can hold alternative assets such as non-traded REITs.
- **Traditional IRA:** An IRA in which investments grow tax-deferred until withdrawal.
- **Roth IRA:** An IRA funded with after-tax dollars; qualified withdrawals are tax-free.
- **Ordinary Income:** Income taxed at standard rates, as most REIT dividends are.
- **Qualified Dividend:** A dividend taxed at lower rates; most REIT dividends are not qualified dividends.
- **Asset Location:** Placing tax-inefficient assets in tax-advantaged accounts.
- **UBIT:** Unrelated business income tax that can apply inside an IRA.
- **UBTI:** Unrelated business taxable income; REIT dividends are generally excluded.
- **UDFI:** Unrelated debt-financed income, a narrow UBIT-related concern.
- **Custodian:** The firm that holds and administers IRA assets.
- **Prohibited Transaction:** An IRA dealing barred by tax rules, such as self-dealing.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The **20%** deduction on REIT dividends, usable only in taxable accounts.
- **Required Minimum Distribution (RMD):** A mandatory traditional-IRA withdrawal at a certain age.
- **Rollover:** Moving retirement funds between accounts, generally tax-free.
