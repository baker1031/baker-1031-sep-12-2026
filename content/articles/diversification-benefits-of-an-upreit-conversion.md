# Diversification Benefits of an UPREIT Conversion

[Home](baker1031.html) · [Back to All Insights](insights.html)

721 Exchange · By Jerry Baker · Updated June 2026 · 12 min read

I keep returning to concentration as the risk many property owners feel only after a problem arrives. One large tenant leaves. A local market slows. A property is damaged. A sector falls out of favor. When most of an owner’s real-estate wealth is tied to one asset—or only a few—the asset’s specific fate can carry an outsized weight.

An UPREIT conversion, also known as a 721 exchange, directly addresses that issue by turning a concentrated property interest into [OP units](op-units-explained.html) tied to a REIT portfolio. That portfolio may hold dozens or hundreds of properties across markets, property types, and tenants, all managed professionally. The result can reduce property-specific concentration risk, but it does not assure a profit, protect against loss in a declining market, or remove every form of concentration.

The decision is therefore not simply “one property versus many.” It is an exchange of one kind of concentration for another: less exposure to a single building and more dependence on the REIT, its sponsor, its management, and the broader real-estate market. This article explains the genuine diversification benefits, their limits, and the questions worth asking about the particular REIT.

## From one property to a portfolio

Before an [UPREIT conversion](721-exchange-guide.html), an owner may hold one property or a small number of properties. Their real-estate wealth is concentrated in the performance of those assets. After the conversion, the owner holds OP units representing a proportional economic interest in the REIT’s broader portfolio.

That can be an immediate and comprehensive change. Rather than buying multiple properties one at a time over many years, the owner receives exposure to a portfolio the REIT has already assembled. The owner is no longer asking only, “How did my property perform?” The relevant question becomes, “How did the portfolio perform?”

The portfolio may contain dozens or hundreds of properties. That breadth can be difficult for an individual owner to assemble alone given the time, capital, and operating effort involved. A 721 conversion can provide that exposure in a single transaction, with the REIT handling the portfolio management.

## Three forms of diversification

The conversion can spread exposure across more than a property count. It can diversify geography, property type, and tenants.

**Geographic diversification.** A REIT may own assets in multiple cities, markets, and regions. An owner whose original property was located in one city is tied to that local economy. A downturn in one market can still affect a REIT, but it may affect only a portion of a broader portfolio rather than the entire investment.

**Property-type diversification.** Depending on its strategy, a REIT may span apartments, retail, industrial, office, and other property types. A REIT can also remain sector-focused while owning many properties within that sector. In either case, the owner is no longer tied to the use and operation of one building. See [diversification by property sector in a 721 exchange](721-exchange-diversification-by-property-sector.html).

**Tenant diversification.** A portfolio of many properties generally has many tenants. A major tenant’s departure or default at one property can be severe for a single-property owner. In a broad portfolio, it is usually a smaller part of the total exposure.

First-order thinking says that more properties automatically mean less risk. Second-order thinking asks whether the spread is actually across geographies, sectors, and tenants, or merely across many similar assets in one narrow strategy. These dimensions are what determine the diversification the owner actually receives.

## How portfolio diversification can reduce concentration risk

Concentration risk is the risk of depending heavily on one property. When a major tenant leaves, a local market weakens, a property is damaged, or a sector declines, a single-property owner can suffer a large loss because the event affects a large share of their real-estate wealth.

Once exposure is spread over a portfolio, one property’s problem may represent only a small part of the investor’s stake. Other properties can cushion the impact of a vacancy, market softness, or asset-level setback. The conversion can therefore change an “all in” property position into a diversified exposure where one vacancy does not decide the year.

That benefit may be especially relevant for an owner whose net worth is heavily concentrated in one property, an older owner who cannot absorb a major loss from one asset, or an owner whose current holdings are otherwise not diversified. It is not a guarantee. Diversification reduces certain concentrations; it does not eliminate risk or ensure a positive return.

## Professional portfolio management and passive ownership

The portfolio is also professionally managed. The REIT’s management team acquires, manages, leases, finances, and disposes of properties. It brings institutional resources to issues such as tenant loss, needed property work, financing decisions, and different market conditions across a portfolio.

That is a different proposition from an individual assembling several properties and then having to manage the diversification themselves. The conversion provides a diversified holding and assigns the operating work to the REIT. The owner becomes passive: they do not manage the properties, leasing, financing, or portfolio allocation.

Professional management does not eliminate the consequences of poor decisions or weak market conditions. It does mean the portfolio is being managed at a scale and with resources an individual owner of one property may not be able to replicate.

## UPREIT diversification compared with direct-real-estate diversification

Diversification is not unique to a 721 exchange. An owner can diversify within direct real estate through multiple [1031 exchanges](1031-exchange-guide.html), acquiring [several properties or DSTs](diversifying-a-1031-across-multiple-dsts.html) across markets and property types. That approach can preserve direct real-property ownership, control, and future 1031 flexibility.

The REIT route is typically broader, more immediate, and more passive. A REIT portfolio may be far larger than the handful of properties or DSTs most individual investors can assemble. It can deliver broad exposure in one transaction rather than gradual acquisitions. And it is professionally managed rather than requiring the owner to oversee multiple assets or coordinate multiple DST interests.

The trade-off is control and optionality. A 721 conversion is generally a [one-way transition](721-exchange-one-way-door-losing-1031-eligibility.html): OP units are partnership interests and cannot generally be 1031-exchanged into another property. Direct-real-estate diversification may offer less breadth and more effort, but it preserves direct control and exchange flexibility. The choice depends on whether immediate, passive portfolio breadth is worth giving up those features.

## The limits of REIT diversification

REIT diversification has limits that should be evaluated directly.

First, there is single-REIT and single-sponsor concentration. The owner has diversified across a portfolio but concentrated their investment in one REIT, its management, and its operating partnership. If [the REIT underperforms](721-exchange-risks-reit-underperformance.html) or encounters management, fee, or portfolio problems, the entire OP-unit holding is affected.

Second, the investor remains in real estate. A REIT may diversify property-specific risk, but its properties remain exposed to broad real-estate risks, including interest rates and economic cycles. This is systematic risk: diversification within real estate does not diversify an owner out of the asset class.

Third, not every REIT has the same breadth. Some have a broad geographic and sector spread. Others focus on apartments, one specialty property type, or a single region. A sector-focused REIT can diversify across many properties, but it does not diversify across sectors. The degree of diversification depends on the actual portfolio, not the label “REIT.”

The conversion therefore trades property concentration for REIT concentration. It can substantially lower a single building’s influence, but it does not make the investment risk-free or remove dependence on the REIT and real estate as a whole.

## Key Takeaways

- An UPREIT conversion can replace exposure to one concentrated property with a stake in a REIT portfolio in a single transaction.
- Diversification can occur across markets, property types, and tenants, reducing the impact of a single tenant, property, or local-market event.
- Portfolio management is professional and passive for the investor, but diversification does not assure a profit or protect against loss.
- The conversion can reduce single-property concentration while introducing single-REIT and sponsor concentration and retaining broad real-estate market exposure.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How does an UPREIT conversion provide diversification?

It turns a single, concentrated property into a stake in a REIT portfolio that may include dozens or hundreds of properties across geographies, property types, and tenants. The investor’s wealth is spread across the portfolio in one transaction instead of through years of assembling properties. That can provide geographic, sector, and tenant diversification and help reduce concentration risk.

### What types of diversification does the conversion provide?

It can provide geographic diversification across multiple markets and regions; property-type diversification across sectors or many properties within one type; and tenant diversification across numerous tenants. Each addresses a different single-property concentration: one local economy, one property use, or one major tenant. The actual breadth depends on the REIT’s portfolio.

### How does diversification reduce my risk?

It spreads exposure so a problem at one property has a smaller effect on the investor’s overall stake. In direct ownership, a major tenant leaving, local downturn, sector decline, or property damage may affect a large share of the owner’s wealth. In a portfolio, other assets can cushion that impact. It may reduce concentrated risk, but it does not eliminate risk or assure a profit.

### Is the REIT portfolio professionally managed?

Yes. The REIT management team handles acquisitions, property management, leasing, financing, and dispositions. It manages tenant loss, needed work, and market conditions across the portfolio with institutional resources. The investor receives this management passively rather than operating the diversified holdings themselves.

### Is the conversion’s diversification better than diversifying with multiple properties?

It is typically broader, more immediate, and more passive. A REIT may hold hundreds of properties rather than the handful an individual could acquire, and the conversion gives portfolio exposure in one transaction. Direct-real-estate diversification through properties or DSTs can preserve control and 1031 flexibility, however. The trade-off is broader passive diversification versus direct-property control and optionality.

### Does the conversion eliminate all my risk?

No. It can reduce property-specific risk, but the investor remains exposed to one REIT and its sponsor or management, to the REIT’s own portfolio focus, and to broad real-estate market risks such as interest rates and economic cycles. Diversifying across properties is not the same as eliminating loss risk or diversifying out of real estate.

### Do I concentrate risk in one REIT by converting?

Yes, to a degree. The owner diversifies across the REIT’s properties while concentrating in that REIT, its operating partnership, sponsor, and management. If the REIT underperforms or has problems, the whole OP-unit stake is affected. This is the central trade-off: less single-property concentration and more single-REIT concentration.

### Am I still exposed to real-estate market risk after converting?

Yes. The portfolio may be spread across markets and property types, but it is all real estate. Interest-rate changes and economic cycles that affect real estate generally remain relevant. The conversion reduces property-specific risk, not the systematic risk of the real-estate asset class. Moving into other asset classes would require a sale and reinvestment, generally triggering tax.

### Does the degree of diversification depend on the REIT?

Yes. A broad REIT can have assets across sectors and regions. A focused REIT may own many properties but concentrate in one property type or one region. A sector-focused REIT diversifies among properties but not sectors. Review the property count, markets, sectors, and tenant concentrations of the particular REIT rather than assuming every REIT has the same diversification.

### Is diversification a good reason to do a 721 exchange?

It can be one of the strongest reasons for an owner heavily concentrated in one property. The ability to spread wealth across a portfolio can be a substantial benefit alongside deferral, passivity, potential liquidity, and estate planning. It must still be weighed against the one-way nature of OP units, loss of control, single-REIT concentration, and the investor’s broader objectives.

### How do I evaluate a REIT’s diversification?

Review the number of properties, number of markets and regions, property-type breadth, and tenant concentration. A portfolio with many properties across markets and types, without a dominant tenant or property, is generally more diversified than a small or focused portfolio. An advisor can help identify the portfolio exposure and the concentration that remains.

### Can I diversify across multiple REITs?

A single 721 exchange generally results in OP units of one REIT because the property goes to that REIT’s operating partnership. Diversification across multiple REITs generally requires separately buying multiple REIT shares with cash or after converting and selling units. The 721 itself diversifies within one REIT rather than across issuers.

### Does diversification reduce my returns?

Diversification primarily changes risk: it reduces volatility and the impact of one property’s problems. A portfolio return reflects the average of its holdings rather than one asset’s outcome. A single property might greatly outperform a portfolio or badly underperform it. Diversification trades the possibility of one property’s outsized gain for less concentrated, portfolio-level results. It is a risk-return trade-off, not necessarily a pure reduction in returns.

### Is diversification more important for some owners than others?

It can matter most to owners whose wealth is concentrated in one property, owners who cannot absorb a major loss from one asset, and owners for whom one property represents much of their net worth. Owners with diversified holdings or greater ability to bear a single-property loss may value the benefit less. Its importance depends on present concentration and capacity to bear that concentrated risk.

### Does the conversion diversify me out of real estate?

No. It diversifies within real estate across a REIT’s properties, markets, and possible property types. The investor remains fully exposed to real estate and its broad cycles. Diversifying into stocks, bonds, or other asset classes requires selling and reinvesting outside the 721 structure, generally with a tax consequence.

## Glossary

**UPREIT Conversion:** A 721 exchange that transforms direct property into a REIT portfolio stake.

**Diversification:** Spreading exposure across a REIT’s many properties.

**Concentration Risk:** The risk of depending on one property, which the conversion can reduce.

**Geographic Diversification:** Exposure across markets and regions.

**Property-Type Diversification:** Exposure across sectors or many properties of one type.

**Tenant Diversification:** Exposure across many tenants, reducing single-tenant risk.

**Portfolio:** The REIT’s collection of properties in which the owner has a diversified stake.

**Professional Management:** Institutional management of the portfolio by the REIT.

**Single-REIT Concentration:** Exposure to one REIT, its sponsor, and management after the conversion.

**Real Estate Correlation:** Broad real-estate risk that the conversion does not remove.

**Systematic Risk:** Market-wide risk such as interest rates and cycles that affects real estate generally.

**Property-Specific Risk:** Risk at one property that portfolio diversification can reduce.

**Sector-Focused REIT:** A REIT concentrated in one sector while holding many properties.

**Instant Diversification:** Immediate portfolio exposure received through the conversion.

**Direct-RE Diversification:** Diversifying through multiple direct properties or DSTs instead of a REIT.

**Sponsor Risk:** Risk related to the REIT sponsor and management.
