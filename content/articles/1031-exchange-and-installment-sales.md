# 1031 Exchange and Installment Sales

I have been thinking about how often seller financing gets treated as a small concession at the end of a property sale. In a 1031 exchange, it is not small. A seller-carried note can change what is deferred, what is recognized over time, who must handle the paper, and how the transaction is reported for years.

Two separate tax-deferral mechanisms can meet in that situation. A 1031 exchange defers gain by reinvesting into like-kind replacement property. An installment sale spreads gain over the years that payments are received. When a buyer pays partly in cash and partly through a seller-carried note, the cash can be used in a 1031 exchange while the note can be handled under installment-sale rules. The result can be a partial-deferral structure: cash gain deferred under the 1031, note gain recognized over payment years.

First-order thinking is, “I have found a way to defer the whole sale.” Second-order thinking is, “Which part is actually deferred, which part is merely spread, what income do I give up by moving the note into the exchange, and who is responsible if the buyer stops paying?” The distinction matters because a tax result and an investment result are not the same thing.

## How a 1031 and an installment sale can combine

The [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) and the [installment sale](installment-sale-real-estate.html) are distinct tools. In a standard 1031, sale proceeds are reinvested into like-kind replacement property, and the gain associated with that qualifying exchange is deferred. In an installment sale, gain is recognized as payments are received over time.

The combination comes up when a seller finances part of the buyer’s purchase price. The seller may agree to carry a note because the buyer needs financing, because it helps complete the sale, because the seller wants interest income, or because the seller wants taxable gain spread across payment years.

The note is the complication. A seller-carried note is a financial instrument, not real estate. It is non-like-kind property in a 1031 exchange and would normally be [boot](insights.html), which means it can cause current gain recognition. Installment-sale treatment may instead allow the seller to recognize the gain attributable to the note as payments arrive. That is why the two tools can work together: the cash portion can be 1031-deferred and the note portion can be installment-spread.

The two portions follow different tracks. Cash properly reinvested in like-kind property can remain deferred until a future taxable event. Gain on an installment note is not deferred indefinitely; it is spread over the life of the note. The combination can be useful, but it is more technical than an all-cash exchange or a stand-alone installment sale.

## The seller-carried note is the central choice

The note may be handled in two main ways.

### Bring the note into the exchange

The note can be brought into the 1031 exchange. In that approach, the qualified intermediary (QI) holds the note, and the note is sold or bought out so its value can be used in the replacement property. Done correctly, that can avoid the note being treated as boot and provide full 1031 treatment for that value.

The trade-off is economic as well as tax-related. The seller generally does not keep the note and its interest income. Full deferral of the note’s value may be the priority, but it is not the same as retaining a stream of payments from the buyer.

### Keep the note and use installment treatment

Alternatively, the seller can retain the note and use the installment method. The seller continues to receive payments and interest. Gain on the note’s principal is recognized as principal payments are received rather than all at once at closing.

This can spread taxable gain, but it does not defer that gain indefinitely in the way a qualifying 1031 exchange can defer the cash portion. The seller is choosing continuing note income and a staged tax result over complete 1031 treatment of the note’s value.

The seller-carried note can be brought into the exchange for full 1031 deferral or handled under the installment rules to spread its gain. That is the central structuring choice. In some cases, the facts support a combination, but the decision has to be deliberate before documents are signed.

## A partial-deferral example

Assume an investor sells property for **$1,000,000**. The buyer pays **$700,000** in cash and gives the seller a **$300,000** note.

If the **$700,000** cash is reinvested in like-kind replacement property through a properly structured 1031 exchange, the gain associated with that cash portion is deferred. If the **$300,000** note remains with the seller and qualifies for installment treatment, the gain on that note portion is recognized as the payments are received.

That is partial deferral. The structure does not make the note disappear from the tax picture. It can improve on immediate recognition of the note as boot by spreading the gain over time, but spreading is not the same as an indefinite 1031 deferral.

The proportions matter. A larger cash portion can mean more value in the 1031 exchange. A larger note means more value subject to installment spreading. Bringing the note into the exchange shifts more value toward 1031 treatment. Retaining the note preserves the potential interest-income stream but leaves the note gain on the installment schedule.

There is no universally correct split. The seller needs to weigh the buyer’s financing need, the credit risk of the note, the value of interest income, the desired amount of deferral, and the quality of the replacement property. The exchange should not turn a weak seller-financing decision into an attractive one merely because tax is deferred.

## Reporting is multi-form and multi-year

The two portions have separate reporting paths.

- **Form 8824, Like-Kind Exchanges,** reports the 1031 portion and establishes deferral of the qualifying cash portion.
- **Form 6252, Installment Sale Income,** reports the installment portion, including gain recognized as note payments are received.

The gain must be allocated correctly between the 1031-deferred and installment-spread portions. Basis, recognized gain, deferred gain, and installment income all have to be calculated precisely. This is not a do-it-yourself reporting task. It calls for a CPA experienced in both 1031 exchanges and installment-sale reporting.

The installment reporting continues. As the seller receives principal payments on the note each year, the appropriate installment income is reported on Form 6252 until the note is paid off. The 1031 portion takes a different path: its deferred gain carries into the basis of the replacement property.

That continuing work is easy to underestimate at closing. The sale may feel finished, but the tax reporting and credit exposure on the note may continue for years. A structure that is technically correct on day one still needs accurate reporting through the life of the note.

## Plan the structure before the sale

The time to decide the cash/note split and the note’s treatment is before the sale. The buyer’s need for seller financing and the seller’s need for deferral or note income should be set out early. The mechanics depend on it.

The first question is how much of the price will be cash and how much will be note. The second is whether the note will be brought into the exchange for 1031 treatment or retained under the installment method. If the note is brought into the exchange, it must be set up correctly from the beginning—for example, payable to the QI where that structure is used. The treatment affects the note documents and the later tax reporting.

The team has three distinct roles:

- The [qualified intermediary](insights.html) handles the 1031 mechanics and any note handling inside the exchange.
- The CPA models the gain allocation, handles installment reporting, and reviews the tax results.
- Counsel structures the note and related documents.

The combined transaction needs all three before the sale. Early coordination is what allows a seller-financed exchange to defer the cash portion and spread the note portion in the intended way.

## Key takeaways

- A seller-financed transaction can combine a 1031 exchange with an installment sale: cash can be 1031-deferred while note gain is installment-spread.
- A seller-carried note is non-like-kind property. It can be brought into the exchange for full 1031 treatment or retained under installment rules.
- Partial deferral means one portion is deferred and the other is spread over payment years. The structure determines the mix.
- Form 8824 and Form 6252 are both involved, with multi-year reporting for the note.
- Decide the note terms and tax treatment before the sale with a QI, CPA, and counsel.

## When the combination makes sense

The clearest use case is a sale in which seller financing is necessary or desirable and the seller also wants a 1031 exchange for the cash portion. A buyer may need the seller to carry a note. The seller may wish to help the sale close, receive interest income, or avoid having all note-related gain recognized immediately.

It can also make sense when a seller values the note as an income-producing asset. Retaining the note allows the seller to earn interest and spread the related gain under the installment method, while still deferring the cash portion through the 1031 exchange.

It is less necessary in an all-cash sale. A straightforward cash sale with a qualifying replacement can use a regular 1031 exchange without installment-sale mechanics. Likewise, a seller who does not want to retain the note may prefer to bring its value into the exchange and seek full 1031 treatment.

The combination is therefore a tool for seller-financed exchanges, not a standard feature to add to every sale. The goal is not maximum complexity. It is a structure that matches the actual financing and capital-allocation decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a 1031 exchange combine with an installment sale?

Yes. In a seller-financed transaction, the cash portion may move through the 1031 exchange while gain on the seller-carried note is recognized under installment rules as payments are received. They address different parts of the sale proceeds.

### Why does a seller-carried note complicate a 1031?

The note is a financial instrument, not like-kind real estate. It is non-like-kind property and would normally be boot unless it is brought into the exchange or handled under installment-sale rules.

### How is the seller-carried note handled?

The note may be brought into the exchange, with the QI holding it and the note sold or bought out so its value reaches the replacement property. Or it may stay with the seller under the installment method, with gain spread as payments arrive. The decision depends on whether the seller values full deferral or ongoing note income.

### What is a partial-deferral scenario?

It is a structure in which part of the gain is deferred through the 1031 and another part is spread through the installment method. In the **$700,000** cash and **$300,000** note example, the cash is 1031-deferred while note gain is installment-spread.

### Is the note’s gain deferred indefinitely?

Not under installment treatment. Gain is recognized over the years payments are received. To seek 1031-style deferral for the note’s value, the note must be brought into the exchange.

### How is a combined 1031-installment sale reported?

The 1031 portion is reported on Form **8824**. The installment portion is reported on Form **6252** over the years payments are received. The gain allocation and the two forms require experienced CPA handling.

### Should I bring the note into the exchange or use installment treatment?

Bringing it in can provide full 1031 deferral, but it requires the note to be sold or bought out and gives up the note’s interest income. Installment treatment lets the seller keep the note and its interest, but taxes the note gain over payment years.

### When does combining the two make sense?

When seller financing is necessary or desirable and the seller wants to exchange the cash portion, or when the seller values the note’s income while wanting 1031 deferral on the cash. For an all-cash sale, the simpler all-cash 1031 exchange may be enough.

### Can I keep the note and still defer the cash?

Yes. The cash portion can be reinvested in a qualifying 1031 exchange while the seller retains the note and spreads its gain under the installment method. The note’s gain is spread rather than indefinitely deferred, and the seller can receive interest.

### Is a combined structure do-it-yourself?

No. It involves the gain allocation, two reporting systems, multi-year installment reporting, and note structure. Use a CPA familiar with both 1031 and installment-sale reporting and counsel for the note.

### How do I plan a combined structure?

Before sale, decide the cash/note split and the note treatment, set up the note correctly from the outset, and coordinate the QI, CPA, and counsel. Where the note is brought into the exchange, it may need to be payable to the QI.

### Does the installment portion affect the 1031 deferral?

They are separate tracks. The cash portion’s 1031 gain is carried in the replacement property’s basis, while the note portion’s gain is recognized as payments are received. The structure must separate them correctly.

### What if the buyer defaults on the note?

The tax consequences depend on the facts and may include loss or repossession treatment, with installment reporting adjusted accordingly. The cash portion already reinvested in the 1031 exchange is not affected by a note default. A CPA should handle the note-side tax result.

### Can I use a DST for the cash portion and keep a note?

Yes. The cash portion may be exchanged into a DST while the seller retains the note and applies installment treatment to its gain. The source describes this as a way to combine passive, diversified real estate for the cash portion with spread taxation for the note, subject to CPA and QI coordination.

### Is the interest on the note taxable?

Yes. Interest on a seller-carried note is ordinary interest income, separate from gain on the principal that may be spread through the installment method. The CPA reports both.

### How long can the installment method spread the gain?

Over the life of the note. Gain is recognized proportionally as principal payments are received until the note is paid. A longer note spreads it over more years; Form 6252 reporting continues over the note’s term.

## Glossary

- **Installment Sale:** A sale with payments over time that spreads gain recognition across the payment years.
- **Seller-Carried Note:** A promissory note carried by the seller as seller financing; it is non-like-kind property in a 1031 exchange.
- **Seller Financing:** An arrangement in which the seller accepts payment over time through a note.
- **Installment Method:** The tax method that reports gain as payments are received, used for the note portion.
- **Form 8824:** The IRS form reporting the like-kind exchange, here the cash portion.
- **Form 6252:** The IRS form reporting installment sale income for the note portion over the payment years.
- **Boot:** Non-like-kind value received; a note would be boot unless brought into the exchange or installment-treated.
- **Partial Deferral:** A combined structure in which one part of gain is 1031-deferred and another is installment-spread.
- **Non-Like-Kind Property:** Property other than qualifying like-kind real estate, including a note.
- **Gain Allocation:** Dividing gain between the 1031-deferred cash portion and installment-spread note portion.
- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that handles 1031 mechanics and any note brought into the exchange.
- **Bringing the Note In:** Including the note in the exchange, payable to the QI and sold or bought out, for 1031 treatment.
- **Note Income:** Interest earned by the seller on a carried note.
- **Cash Portion:** The portion of the sale paid in cash and reinvested through the 1031 exchange.
- **Note Portion:** The portion carried as a note and handled by installment treatment or brought into the exchange.
- **Delaware Statutory Trust (DST):** A possible replacement for the cash portion of a 1031 exchange.
