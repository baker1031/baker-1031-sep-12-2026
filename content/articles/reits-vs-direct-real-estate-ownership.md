# REITs vs. Direct Real Estate Ownership

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read**

I keep seeing the same real-estate choice reduced to a question of which route has the better return. That skips the more useful question: which set of obligations, constraints, and risks do you actually want to own?

Real estate can be owned indirectly through a REIT—a Real Estate Investment Trust that owns, operates, or finances income-producing property—or directly, by buying and managing a property yourself. Both create real-estate exposure, but the experience is very different. A REIT can be passive, low-minimum, diversified, and professionally managed; if it is publicly traded, it is also liquid. In exchange, the shareholder has no control and must live with share-price volatility. Direct ownership can provide control, direct depreciation and other tax benefits, and potential 1031 eligibility, but it is hands-on, illiquid, capital-intensive, and concentrated.

This is educational information, not investment advice. REIT suitability depends on your circumstances, and current rules should be confirmed with your adviser. First-order thinking asks whether a REIT or a property looks more attractive today. Second-order thinking asks what happens when liquidity is needed, a tenant leaves, financing changes, a market reprices, or the owner no longer wants the operating job. That is the comparison worth making.

## Passive vs. hands-on ownership

The fundamental distinction is passive versus hands-on ownership. With a REIT, the investor buys shares. A professional management team acquires, leases, maintains, and finances the underlying real estate; it handles tenants and the rest of the operating work. The shareholder gets exposure without doing that work.

With direct ownership, the owner—or a manager the owner hires and supervises—handles the property, tenants, repairs, and financing. REIT investors can collect dividends and watch a share price without becoming landlords. Direct owners are landlords or developers, responsible for day-to-day operations and their results.

That trade-off is neither good nor bad by itself. Someone seeking real-estate income and diversification without management may prefer the REIT. Someone who values control and is prepared to do the work, or oversee it, may prefer direct ownership. A REIT spares the investor the landlord role; direct ownership makes that role part of the investment.

## Liquidity and minimums

Publicly traded REIT shares trade on an exchange at a daily price. An investor can generally buy or sell readily, much as with a stock, and can start with a single share or a fraction of one. That creates easy entry and exit with modest capital.

There is an important exception. Non-traded REITs are illiquid, are priced at net asset value (NAV), and usually have limited redemption programs. “REIT” is not a complete answer to a liquidity question; the structure matters.

Direct property is nearly the opposite. Selling can take months and involves meaningful transaction costs and effort. Buying typically requires a large down payment or the full purchase price, as well as closing costs. Capital is committed to something that cannot normally be sold on a trading day.

> A traded REIT lets an investor own a slice of institutional real estate for the price of a single share and sell it on a trading day. Direct property ownership, with its large capital requirement and months-long sale process, cannot offer the same flexibility.

## Control and management burden

Direct ownership gives the owner control over the property selected, how it is managed, when it is renovated, how it is financed, and when it is sold. Good decisions can create upside. The other side is the management burden: tenants, repairs, vacancies, financing, and the ordinary headaches of being a landlord.

A REIT shareholder does not make those property-level decisions. The choices are generally to buy, hold, or sell shares while the management team makes the operating and capital decisions. Giving up control is what removes the landlord burden.

This is a mirror-image trade-off. Direct ownership offers control and requires work. A REIT removes the work and the control. The relevant preference is not abstractly “better”; it is whether freedom from management or the ability to direct the investment has greater value to the particular investor.

## Return and risk comparison

REITs offer diversified, professionally managed exposure across many properties, dividend income, and, for traded REITs, daily liquidity. But a traded REIT share price moves with the market and can diverge from the value of the underlying bricks and mortar. It can therefore be more volatile day to day than the properties it owns.

Direct ownership offers control, potential mortgage leverage, direct depreciation and tax benefits, and potential 1031 eligibility. REIT shares are not 1031-eligible. Direct property also concentrates capital in one or a few assets, can be difficult to sell, and relies on the owner’s execution. A capable direct owner can do well, but concentration and execution risk are real.

Neither route is universally better. Goals, capital, risk tolerance, and the desire for control versus passivity all matter. A first-order reaction may favor the price movement that feels calmer. A second-order analysis separates visible daily volatility from concentration, illiquidity, leverage, and operating risk. Both routes carry risk, just in different forms.

### Key Takeaways

- REITs are passive, liquid when traded, low-minimum, diversified, and professionally managed, but shareholders have no control and traded REITs have market volatility.
- Direct ownership offers control, leverage, direct depreciation and tax benefits, and potential 1031 eligibility, but it is hands-on, illiquid, capital-intensive, and concentrated.
- REIT shares are not 1031-eligible; direct real property can be, a material distinction for a 1031 exchange.
- Many investors use both: REITs for passive, liquid, diversified exposure and direct ownership for control and tax benefits.

## Tax and 1031 differences

The tax distinction can be decisive. With direct ownership, the owner can take direct depreciation deductions that shelter some rental income, deduct expenses, and potentially defer capital-gains tax through a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html) when selling and reinvesting in like-kind real estate. Those are hands-on benefits tied to owning the property directly.

REIT shares cannot be used in or out of a 1031 exchange. They are securities rather than like-kind real property, so selling them is generally a taxable event, much like selling a stock. REIT dividends are mostly taxed as ordinary income and reported on Form 1099-DIV. The 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends, made permanent by the 2025 OBBBA, can soften that treatment. It does not create direct depreciation or 1031 eligibility.

The comparison is meaningful, but personal. Direct property offers depreciation, expense deductions, and possible 1031 deferral. REITs offer a Section 199A deduction on qualified dividends. Consult a CPA about the facts of any particular investment; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

## Blending both approaches

The choice need not be all-or-nothing. An investor may hold a directly owned property for control, leverage, depreciation, and 1031 eligibility while using REITs for passive, liquid, diversified exposure to property types or markets that would not otherwise be accessible.

A direct holding can serve as a core, hands-on position, with REITs as a complementary sleeve; the reverse can also be true. The combination can diversify across distinct risk profiles: concentration and illiquidity on one side, market volatility on the other. No single mix fits every person. Capital, time, expertise, and objectives determine whether a blend makes sense.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between a REIT and direct real estate ownership?

A REIT is passive: the investor buys shares while a professional team owns, operates, and finances the real estate, including acquisitions, leasing, maintenance, tenants, and financing. Direct ownership is hands-on: the owner, or a manager the owner oversees, handles the property, tenants, repairs, and financing. The differences then extend to liquidity and minimums, diversification and concentration, control, depreciation, and 1031 eligibility. Many investors hold both structures to use their separate strengths.

### Are REITs more liquid than owning property directly?

Publicly traded REITs are far more liquid. Their shares trade on an exchange at a daily price and can generally be bought or sold on a trading day. Selling direct property can take months and entails significant cost and effort. The exception is the non-traded REIT: it is NAV-priced, illiquid, and subject to limited redemption. Liquidity should be weighed alongside control, tax treatment, and diversification rather than on its own.

### Do REITs have lower minimums than direct ownership?

Usually, yes. A traded REIT can be bought for one share or even a fraction, allowing modest capital to gain real-estate exposure. Direct ownership generally requires a large down payment or the full purchase price plus closing costs. Non-traded and private REITs can have higher minimums and are often offered to accredited investors, but traded REITs remain an accessible entry point.

### Does direct ownership give more control than a REIT?

Yes. A direct owner decides what to buy, how to manage it, when to renovate, how to finance it, and when to sell. A REIT manager makes those choices, and the shareholder ordinarily decides only whether to buy, hold, or sell shares. Control comes with tenants, repairs, vacancies, and financing work. The REIT removes that burden along with the control.

### Which has higher returns—REITs or direct ownership?

Neither has uniformly higher returns. The investment, market, and the direct owner’s skill all matter. REITs offer diversified professional management, income, and traded liquidity but market and share-price volatility. Direct ownership offers control, potential mortgage leverage, and direct tax benefits but is concentrated and dependent on management. Leverage can amplify returns and losses. Past performance does not guarantee future results, and neither profile promises an outcome.

### Can I do a 1031 exchange with REIT shares?

No. A 1031 exchange applies to real property reinvested in like-kind real property; REIT shares are securities. An investor cannot 1031 into or out of REIT shares, and their sale is generally taxable like a stock sale. Direct investment property can qualify for 1031 treatment. A related 721 UPREIT strategy can allow some direct property owners to contribute property to a REIT operating partnership on a tax-deferred basis, but that is distinct from a 1031 exchange involving REIT shares. Confirm eligibility with a CPA; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How are REIT dividends taxed compared to direct rental income?

REIT dividends are mostly ordinary income, reported on Form 1099-DIV, rather than lower-rate qualified dividends. A 20% Section 199A deduction on qualified REIT dividends can reduce the effective tax cost. Direct rental income can be offset by direct depreciation and expense deductions, and gains may be deferred through a 1031 exchange. The more favorable outcome depends on the investor’s facts, so it should be reviewed with a CPA.

### Is a REIT or direct ownership better for diversification?

A REIT is generally more diversified. A single REIT commonly owns many properties across markets and sometimes property types, and an investor can add REITs from different sectors. Direct ownership places capital in one or a few assets, so a tenant problem, local downturn, or major repair has an immediate effect. Owning enough direct properties to reach comparable diversification requires substantial capital.

### Can I blend REITs and direct ownership?

Yes. A directly held property can provide control, leverage, depreciation, and 1031 eligibility while REITs provide passive, liquid, diversified exposure. The blend can balance a hands-on core with a passive sleeve and diversify concentration and illiquidity against market volatility. The appropriate mix depends on capital, time, expertise, and goals.

### Is direct ownership too much work for most investors?

It can be. Direct ownership requires management of property, tenants, repairs, vacancies, and financing, or the cost and oversight of a hired manager. For investors without the time, expertise, or interest in being landlords, that burden can be significant. A REIT removes it. Investors who value control and are prepared to do or oversee the work may still prefer direct ownership, and some investors split the difference with a blend.

### What are non-traded REITs, and how do they fit this comparison?

Non-traded REITs do not trade on a public exchange. They are priced at NAV and have limited redemption programs, so they are more passive and diversified than direct ownership but not liquid like a traded REIT. They are generally offered through broker-dealers and are commonly limited to accredited or otherwise-suitable investors after a suitability review. The SEC has published guidance worth reviewing because fees and illiquidity deserve careful consideration.

### Does direct ownership let me use leverage better than a REIT?

Direct ownership gives the owner direct control over a mortgage: the down payment, terms, and leverage level. That can amplify gains and losses. A REIT uses leverage at the entity level, so the shareholder bears the effect of management’s borrowing decisions without controlling them. Buying shares on margin is possible but is a different and riskier form of leverage. Direct control can help a sophisticated owner, but it requires expertise and carries real risk.

### Which is less risky—REITs or direct ownership?

Neither is categorically less risky. A traded REIT has market and share-price volatility and depends on management decisions, but its diversification reduces single-property risk. Direct ownership does not reprice daily on an exchange, but it concentrates capital, can be hard to exit, and depends on the owner’s execution. Both can lose value. The relevant question is which risks fit the investor’s capacity, and whether a blend can keep either one from becoming too large.

### Should beginners start with REITs or direct ownership?

Many beginners find REITs easier because they can be low-minimum, liquid when traded, passive, and diversified. Direct ownership requires more capital and skill in buying, financing, and operating property, with concentrated execution risk. A beginner with the capital, time, and willingness to learn may still choose direct ownership. This is general education, not individualized advice; an adviser can help assess fit.

## Glossary

**REIT** — A Real Estate Investment Trust that owns, operates, or finances real estate.

**Direct Ownership** — Buying and managing property yourself; hands-on ownership.

**Passive Investment** — Holding shares while professionals manage the real estate.

**Hands-On Ownership** — Managing the property, tenants, and financing yourself.

**Liquidity** — How readily an investment can be bought or sold.

**Traded REIT** — A REIT with exchange-listed, daily-priced shares.

**Non-Traded REIT** — An illiquid REIT priced at NAV with limited redemption.

**Minimum Investment** — The smallest amount needed to invest; low for traded REITs.

**Control** — The ability to direct an investment’s decisions.

**Management Burden** — The landlord work of direct ownership.

**Diversification** — Spreading capital across many properties, a feature built into REITs.

**Concentration Risk** — Risk from having capital in one or a few properties.

**Depreciation** — A direct-ownership deduction that can shelter rental income.

**1031 Eligibility** — Direct property can qualify; REIT shares do not.

**Section 199A Deduction** — The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends.

**Leverage** — Using debt to amplify returns and risk.

## REITs vs. direct ownership at a glance

| Factor | REITs | Direct Ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Involvement | Passive—managed for the shareholder | Hands-on, or the owner hires a manager |
| Liquidity | Public: daily; non-traded: limited | Illiquid; sell on the owner’s timeline |
| Minimums | Low—the price of a share | Full purchase price plus reserves |
| Control | No control over individual properties | Full control over decisions |
| 1031 eligible | Generally no | Yes |
| Tax | Dividends mostly ordinary income | Depreciation shelter; 1031 deferral |

## Which should you choose?

REITs can suit investors seeking passive, liquid real-estate exposure with low minimums and no management burden who do not need 1031 eligibility. Direct ownership can suit investors who value control, the depreciation tax shelter, and the ability to defer gains through a 1031 exchange, and who accept illiquidity and landlord work. Many use a managed direct property with REITs to diversify.
