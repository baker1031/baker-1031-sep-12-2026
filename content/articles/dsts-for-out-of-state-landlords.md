# DSTs for Out-of-State Landlords: Going Passive

*Delaware Statutory Trusts · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep hearing the same sentence from owners with a rental in another state: “It is only one property, but it keeps finding a way to interrupt the week.” A leaking roof, a turnover, a late payment, or a property-manager invoice is manageable when the building is nearby. From several states away, each one becomes a phone call, a judgment about someone else’s judgment, and often another expense.

The first-order view is that a remote rental is just a passive asset with a manager in place. The second-order reality is that distance can leave the owner with reduced control, one-property and one-market concentration, and responsibility without direct visibility. A 1031 exchange into Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) interests can be a way to trade that active remote obligation for professionally managed, passive real estate while deferring tax. It also requires accepting different risks: illiquidity, fees, sponsor reliance, no guaranteed income, and less control.

DST interests are securities offered to accredited investors after a suitability review. For a wider explanation of the structure, see the [in-depth DST pillar guide](dst-guide.html).

## The Out-of-State Management Problem

Remote ownership has a practical friction that a spreadsheet rarely captures. You cannot easily inspect the property, meet a contractor, or assess a tenant complaint in person. Instead, you depend on local property managers, contractors, and inspectors you cannot readily supervise. A leaking roof, a unit turnover, or a late payment becomes a logistical problem handled by phone, email, and trust, often with delay and added cost.

There are quieter burdens too. Travel and time zones make in-person response expensive. Local market knowledge fades. An underperforming property manager can erode returns through deferred maintenance, poor tenant selection, or padded invoices. Vacancies and repairs that a nearby owner might notice quickly can linger when the owner is remote.

The issue is not simply inconvenience. It is diminished control and heavier reliance on third parties, concentrated in one property and one market. That is why many out-of-state landlords begin looking for a passive alternative.

## Going Passive With DSTs

A DST is a trust that holds income-producing real estate. The investor owns a fractional beneficial interest, while a professional sponsor handles acquisition, leasing, property management, and eventual sale. The investor does not screen tenants, coordinate repairs, or supervise a distant manager.

For a remote landlord, that is the central change. Instead of trying to improve control over a property hundreds of miles away, the owner can exchange into professionally managed DST interests and become passive. The sponsor does the property-level work. The investor may receive a share of rental income, but that income is a projection, not a guarantee, and depends on property performance.

The fix for a building you cannot see is not always a better manager far away. Sometimes it is stepping out of management entirely.

## Diversifying Beyond One Market

Passivity is only part of the case. When all equity is in one rental in one city, the owner is exposed to that city’s economy, that property type, and that one asset. If the local market softens, a major employer leaves, or the property underperforms, there is no offset.

DST interests can be spread among multiple offerings and property types: apartments in one region, industrial in another, and net-lease retail in a third, for example. This can spread exposure across markets and sectors without adding landlord work because each DST is professionally managed. The number of DSTs available for an allocation depends on the exchange proceeds and the applicable minimums.

Diversification cannot remove investment risk. It can, however, replace a single distant property with several passive exposures so that one local market is less likely to determine the entire outcome.

## Tax Deferral on the Sale

The tax bill is often what keeps a remote owner from selling. An outright sale can trigger capital-gains tax on appreciation and tax on [depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html). For a long-held property, federal and often state tax can materially reduce the proceeds and make an owner feel trapped in a management situation they no longer want.

A 1031 exchange allows an investor to sell investment real estate and reinvest the proceeds in like-kind replacement real property while deferring gain. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, a DST interest is treated as qualifying real property for this purpose. The owner may therefore exchange the remote rental into one or more DSTs, defer capital gains and recapture, and keep the full equity working rather than paying tax immediately.

DSTs may also have non-recourse property-level financing in place. That financing can help satisfy the debt-replacement part of a 1031 exchange. The tax treatment, values, and debt analysis are fact-specific, so a CPA should confirm them.

## Key Takeaways

- Out-of-state ownership can mean remote tenants, repairs, hard-to-supervise managers, reduced control, and added cost and stress.
- A DST sponsor manages the real estate while the investor holds a passive fractional interest; distributions are not guaranteed.
- Multiple DSTs can spread proceeds across properties, markets, and sectors instead of leaving all exposure in one distant rental.
- A 1031 into DSTs can defer capital-gains tax and depreciation recapture, and non-recourse DST debt may help replace old debt.

## Income, Suitability, and What to Expect

Going passive is a genuine trade. A DST generally has a defined hold, commonly around five to seven years. During that period, the offering may aim to pass through current rental income. At the end, the sponsor sells the property and the investor receives a share of proceeds. The investor may take cash and pay deferred tax, begin another 1031 exchange into new DSTs or other like-kind real property, or, if the offering permits it, potentially move into a REIT through a 721 exchange.

DSTs are illiquid. There is little or no secondary market, so an investor should not use capital that may be needed in the near or medium term. They are Regulation D securities generally limited to accredited investors and offered through a broker-dealer after a suitability review. That review looks at financial situation, objectives, liquidity needs, and risk tolerance. See [accredited-investor requirements](accredited-investor-requirements.html) for more on that threshold.

The question is not whether passive ownership is automatically better. It is whether surrendering direct control and ready liquidity is worth the reduction in management work and the potential for broader diversification in this investor’s circumstances. Fees, projected income, accredited-only access, and sponsor dependence all belong in that judgment.

## Making the Transition

Sequence matters. The first step is to engage a qualified intermediary (QI) **before** the sale. The QI holds the proceeds so the investor does not take actual or constructive receipt. If the sale closes and the owner receives the money—even briefly—the exchange is generally disqualified, and the QI cannot be added afterward. Read more about the [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) role before closing.

Once the rental sells, the calendar starts. The investor has **45 days** to identify replacement property and **180 days** to close. A DST can often close in days after a suitable offering is identified and the suitability and subscription process is complete, because the real estate and financing are already in an existing structure. That speed can be useful when the 45-day identification period is tight or a primary replacement falls through.

The process should be coordinated with the broker-dealer, QI, and CPA: engage the QI before the sale, identify suitable DSTs within 45 days, complete the review and subscription process, and close within 180 days. The [45-day identification rules](1031-exchange-identification-rules.html) deserve careful attention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is owning an out-of-state rental so difficult?

Distance makes ordinary landlord tasks harder. An owner cannot readily inspect the property, meet a contractor, or evaluate a tenant issue, so local managers, contractors, and inspectors must be trusted. A leaking roof, turnover, or late payment can mean delays and added cost. Travel, time zones, fading local knowledge, deferred maintenance, poor tenant selection, padded invoices, and problems that linger all add to the burden. The exposure is often still concentrated in one property and one market.

### How do DSTs help an out-of-state landlord?

A DST lets an investor own a fractional interest in income-producing real estate while the sponsor handles acquisition, leasing, management, and sale. There are no tenants to screen, repairs to coordinate, or remote property manager to supervise. The investor may also diversify across several DSTs and use a 1031 exchange to defer tax on sale. It turns an active remote obligation into a passive investment, but income is not guaranteed and suitability still matters.

### Can I 1031 exchange my out-of-state rental into a DST?

Yes, subject to the rules and your facts. A DST interest qualifies as like-kind replacement property under Revenue Ruling 2004-86. A proper exchange can defer gain and depreciation recapture, and a DST’s non-recourse financing may help replace debt on the old property. The owner must engage a QI before sale, identify replacement DSTs within 45 days, and close within 180 days. Confirm eligibility and timing with the CPA and QI.

### Does going passive with DSTs mean I give up control?

Yes. The sponsor makes property-level decisions, and DST restrictions often called the “seven deadly sins” sharply limit new actions by the trust and investors. The investor cannot choose tenants, set rents, decide when to renovate, or decide when to sell. A remote landlord may already have only limited, delegated control; trading that stressful control for genuine passivity, diversification, and deferral can be worthwhile for some people. The suitability review is meant to weigh that exact trade.

### How do DSTs let me diversify beyond one market?

The exchange proceeds can be spread across several DSTs in different markets and property types rather than left in one rental. Apartments in one region, industrial in another, and net-lease retail in a third are one example. Each property remains professionally managed, so diversification need not add management work. The number of investments depends on available proceeds and minimums; the allocation should fit the investor’s goals with an advisor.

### What tax do I defer by exchanging into a DST?

A qualifying 1031 exchange generally defers capital-gains tax and the tax on depreciation recapture. An outright sale may incur federal and often state tax on appreciation and recapture. Reinvesting the full proceeds in qualifying like-kind property can keep the full equity invested. Deferral continues while the investor remains in qualifying like-kind property; if the property is held until death, heirs may receive a step-up in basis that can eliminate deferred gain entirely. Amounts and treatment depend on individual circumstances, so consult a CPA.

### Are DSTs liquid if I need my money back?

No. The investor generally remains invested until the sponsor sells the property, commonly after a five-to-seven-year hold, and there is little or no secondary market. Do not use DST capital for a near- or medium-term need. Investors who need ready liquidity may find a DST unsuitable or may want other liquid assets alongside it. When the sponsor sells, the investor can take cash, begin another exchange, or potentially move into a REIT if the structure permits.

### Do I need to be an accredited investor to use a DST?

Generally yes. DST interests are Regulation D securities limited to accredited investors. Qualification typically involves income during the prior two years or net worth above an applicable threshold excluding a primary residence. A broker-dealer performs a suitability review of finances, goals, liquidity, and risk tolerance. If the investor is not accredited, a DST generally is not available, so confirm status early and consider other replacement-property options.

### What happens at the end of a DST’s hold?

The sponsor commonly sells after a five-to-seven-year hold, winds down the DST, and distributes the investor’s share of proceeds. The investor may take cash and pay deferred tax, begin a new 1031 exchange into DSTs or other like-kind property, or potentially use a 721/UPREIT path into REIT operating-partnership units if the offering provides one. The defined hold means the exit should be planned in advance with the CPA and advisor.

### How quickly can a DST close for my exchange?

It can often close in days once a suitable DST has been identified and its suitability and subscription process is complete. The property and financing are already in place, unlike a direct acquisition that can take weeks or months to negotiate and close. This can help meet the 45-day identification and 180-day closing periods and can provide an identified backup if a primary replacement fails. Coordinate timing with the broker-dealer, QI, and CPA.

### Why do I need a qualified intermediary before selling?

A valid 1031 exchange requires that the seller not receive actual or constructive receipt of sale proceeds. The QI holds the funds and uses them to acquire the replacement DSTs. If the seller receives the money at closing, even briefly, the exchange is generally disqualified and the deferred tax is triggered. The QI must be engaged before sale, not after; coordinate its role, the closing, and the replacement identification from the start.

### Is going passive with DSTs right for every out-of-state landlord?

No. It may fit an accredited owner who wants to stop remote management, can accept less control and liquidity, does not need the capital soon, and values passive, diversified, tax-deferred real estate. It may not fit someone who needs liquidity, wants hands-on control, is not accredited, or has a short time horizon. The investor must weigh illiquidity, fees, sponsor reliance, and projected rather than guaranteed income against the benefits. A suitability review and CPA conversation are essential.

### Can I move from a DST into a REIT later for more diversification?

Possibly. A DST property may later be acquired by a REIT, often the sponsor’s REIT, in a 721/UPREIT exchange. That can convert the DST interest into operating-partnership (OP) units while preserving deferral. OP units may later be converted to REIT shares; converting to shares and selling is generally taxable then. The path depends on the specific DST and REIT, so confirm mechanics and timing with a tax advisor. At sale, alternatives can include cash, another 1031, or a potential REIT path.

### What are the risks of exchanging my rental into DSTs?

Risks include a multi-year illiquid hold, little secondary market, upfront loads and offering costs, ongoing management fees, sponsor reliance, property-level concentration, and non-recourse debt. Income is projected and can fall if rent or occupancy declines. A strict 1031 timing or process error can disqualify the exchange. Diversifying several DSTs and sizing the allocation prudently can reduce some concentration risk, but it cannot eliminate risk. Distributions and returns are never guaranteed, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Glossary

**DST:** A Delaware Statutory Trust holding 1031-eligible fractional real estate.

**Out-of-State Landlord:** An owner managing a rental property far from where they live.

**1031 Exchange:** A tax-deferred swap of like-kind investment real estate.

**Like-Kind Real Property:** The real estate a 1031 requires; a DST interest qualifies.

**Revenue Ruling 2004-86:** The IRS ruling treating a DST interest as real property for a 1031 exchange.

**Passive Ownership:** Owning real estate without managing it; the DST model.

**Sponsor:** The firm that acquires, manages, and sells the DST property.

**Qualified Intermediary (QI):** The party that holds 1031 proceeds so the investor never receives them.

**45-Day Identification:** The window to identify replacement property after selling.

**180-Day Closing:** The deadline to close on replacement property in a 1031 exchange.

**Depreciation Recapture:** Tax on prior depreciation, also deferred by a 1031 exchange.

**Non-Recourse Debt:** Property-level DST financing that helps replace old debt.

**Defined Hold:** A DST’s multi-year period, often ~5-7 years, before sale.

**Diversification:** Spreading exchange proceeds across multiple DSTs and markets.

**Accredited Investor:** An investor meeting income or net-worth thresholds for DSTs.

**Suitability Review:** Assessing whether a DST fits the investor before investing.
