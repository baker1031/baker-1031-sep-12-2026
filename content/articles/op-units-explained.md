# OP Units Explained: How Operating Partnership Units Work

*721 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read*

I have been thinking about the moment after an owner decides a 721 exchange is worth considering. The most important question then is often less glamorous than the exchange itself: what, exactly, will I own afterward?

The answer is operating-partnership units, usually called OP units. They are neither direct real estate nor quite REIT stock. That in-between legal form is what allows the UPREIT structure to work, and it is where tax deferral, income, liquidity, control, and estate planning begin to intersect.

First-order thinking treats OP units as stock with a different name. Second-order thinking recognizes the trade: a partnership interest can preserve tax deferral and provide passive portfolio income, but it can also reduce direct control and make liquidity taxable. The wrapper matters.

## Key Takeaways

- OP units are limited-partnership units in a REIT’s operating partnership, received when property is contributed in a 721 exchange.
- They are generally economically equivalent to REIT shares and pay distributions that track the REIT’s dividend.
- After a holding period, units can usually be converted to REIT shares or redeemed for cash. That is a taxable event that triggers the deferred gain.
- While holding units, the owner is a partner who receives a K-1 and generally does not have the voting rights of a REIT shareholder.

## What OP units are

An UPREIT does not hold its properties directly. It holds them through an operating partnership, while the REIT serves as the partnership’s general partner. OP units are limited-partnership interests in that operating partnership.

In a [721 exchange](721-exchange-vs-1031-exchange.html), an owner contributes property—or a DST interest—to the operating partnership and receives OP units in return.

The units are designed to be economically equivalent to the REIT’s common shares. A unit and a share generally have the same value and the same distribution per period. The legal form differs: one is a partnership interest and the other is stock. That difference carries tax consequences. An OP unit is, in effect, an interest in the REIT portfolio held in a partnership wrapper that permits the tax-deferred contribution.

## How you receive them

Property is contributed to the operating partnership under Section 721, which generally makes the contribution tax-free. The number of OP units is set by a conversion ratio: typically the property’s agreed value divided by the value of one unit, which tracks one REIT share. If a contributed interest is worth the equivalent of 10,000 units at the current unit value, the owner receives 10,000 units.

The economic exposure changes at that point. The owner moves from a concentrated, directly held property to an indirect interest in a diversified REIT portfolio. That is the core UPREIT trade and one examined in the [721 exchange downsides memo](721-exchange-downsides.html).

## How distributions work

OP units pay distributions that mirror the REIT’s dividend, generally on the same schedule and in the same per-unit amount as a REIT share. For many 721 owners, this is the reason for the structure: passive cash flow from a diversified portfolio without property management.

The tax reporting is different from that of a shareholder. An OP-unit holder is a limited partner and generally receives a Schedule K-1, rather than the Form 1099-DIV received by a REIT shareholder. The economics are designed to match, but reporting follows partnership rules. A tax advisor should remain involved.

## OP units vs. REIT shares

OP units are interests in the operating partnership that owns the real estate. REIT shares are ownership interests in the REIT that controls that partnership. Both represent economic exposure to the REIT portfolio, and OP units are typically convertible to REIT shares, often one-for-one. In economic terms, a unit and a share generally have comparable value.

Tax treatment and liquidity are the important differences. Holding OP units keeps the deferred gain deferred. Converting units into REIT shares generally triggers that deferred gain. Public REIT shares can be liquid and tradable on a market. OP units are less liquid and usually must be converted to shares before the holder can access that market liquidity, accepting the tax consequence.

The choice is therefore clear even if it is not easy: retain the tax-deferred partnership interest for income and deferral, or convert it to liquid corporate shares and recognize tax.

## Converting OP units to REIT shares

OP units are not necessarily permanent. After an initial holding period, often around a year and set by the partnership agreement, a holder generally gains the right to tender units. The REIT typically elects to deliver REIT shares, commonly one-for-one, or may deliver cash. For a publicly traded REIT, that creates a path to stock-market liquidity.

Conversion is generally taxable. Tendering OP units recognizes the deferred capital gain and depreciation recapture on the portion converted. The deferral preserved by taking units ends when those units are turned into shares. Some investors convert gradually to spread the tax across several years rather than recognizing it all at once.

## Redeeming for cash

Units can often be redeemed for cash instead of, or in addition to, being converted into shares. With a publicly traded REIT, that can be straightforward. With a non-traded REIT, redemption normally occurs through a program that can have caps and queues, and the sponsor can limit or suspend redemptions in stressed conditions.

Cash redemption, like conversion, is a taxable disposition that triggers the deferred gain. There is no tax-free way to take money out of OP units short of holding them until death. That is why estate planning often carries unusual weight in a 721 strategy.

## Voting and other rights

OP-unit holders are limited partners, not shareholders. They generally do not have the voting rights REIT shareholders have on matters such as electing the board. The REIT, as general partner, controls the operating partnership.

Partnership agreements can provide protective rights, including limits on actions that could unfairly trigger a holder’s built-in gain. Those protections should not be mistaken for a meaningful governance voice. Taking OP units means accepting passive, non-voting participation in the REIT’s fortunes. Owners who value governance influence should account for what they are giving up.

## How OP units are valued

OP units track REIT-share value. For a publicly traded REIT, that is a live and transparent market price. For a non-traded REIT, value is set through periodic net asset value (NAV) appraisals rather than a market price.

NAV can lag current conditions, and redemptions are generally based on that appraised figure. Knowing whether units were issued by a public or non-traded REIT helps an owner judge how current the stated value is and how real the promised liquidity may be.

## Tax treatment over the holding period

The deferred gain remains deferred while the owner simply holds OP units. The owner reports their share of partnership income on a K-1. Cash distributions are generally not taxable to the extent of the holder’s basis in the units, although they reduce that basis.

The deferred built-in gain can be recognized later through conversion or redemption. It can also arise if the operating partnership sells the contributed property in a manner that passes the gain through to the holder.

One favorable outcome comes from not selling. If units are held until death, heirs may receive a stepped-up basis that can eliminate the deferred gain. That estate-planning question is discussed in [estate planning with a 721 exchange](721-exchange-estate-planning.html).

## OP units and estate planning

The [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) at death generally applies to OP units. If units are held until death, heirs may receive a basis reset to fair market value at the owner’s death. That can erase the deferred gain embedded in the units, as well as the tax burden on further appreciation that conversion or sale would otherwise have triggered.

OP units are also divisible. A single property can be difficult to divide among multiple heirs without a sale or a shared-ownership problem. An owner can leave specific numbers of units to each heir, allowing cleaner division of real estate wealth.

The combination matters: an owner can hold units for life, receive distributions, defer gain, and pass divisible units to heirs. The step-up can erase gain while divisibility makes the transfer easier. This resembles the benefit of holding 1031 property until death, and it can make OP units useful in an estate plan.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are OP units?

They are limited-partnership interests in a REIT’s operating partnership. They are received when an owner contributes property in a 721 exchange, are generally economically equivalent to REIT shares, and pay matching distributions.

### How do OP units pay income?

They pay distributions that mirror the REIT’s dividend, generally on the same schedule and in the same per-unit amount as a REIT share. As a partner, the owner reports this through a Schedule K-1.

### Can I convert OP units to REIT shares?

Usually, after a holding period. The REIT typically delivers shares, often one-for-one, or cash. Conversion is taxable and recognizes deferred gain, so some investors convert gradually.

### Do OP unitholders get to vote?

Generally no. OP-unit holders are limited partners rather than shareholders, so they typically lack shareholder voting rights. Partnership agreements often provide limited protective rights.

### Are OP units taxed while I hold them?

The deferred gain stays deferred while units are held. Partnership income is reported on a K-1, and distributions are generally not taxable up to the holder’s basis. Tax can be triggered on conversion, redemption, or certain sales by the partnership.

### How do I get OP units?

Contribute property to the REIT’s operating partnership in a 721 exchange. The partnership issues units equal in value to the contributed property under Section 721. The unit count equals agreed property value divided by unit value, which is typically tied to the REIT share price. For example, a $4,000,000 property at $40 per unit yields 100,000 units. The owner receives the units without recognizing gain and becomes a limited partner.

### How do OP units differ from REIT shares?

OP units are operating-partnership interests; REIT shares are ownership in the REIT. They are economically similar and typically convertible, often one-for-one. Units preserve tax deferral and are less liquid. Public REIT shares trade on a market, but conversion to them triggers tax.

### Is converting OP units to shares taxable?

Generally, yes. Converting OP units to REIT shares or cash recognizes deferred gain on the converted units. It trades continued deferral for liquidity. An owner can convert gradually or hold units toward a potential step-up at death.

### Do OP units get a step-up in basis at death?

Generally, yes. If held until death, heirs may receive a basis reset to fair market value. That can erase the deferred gain in the units and further appreciation tax that sale or conversion would otherwise trigger.

### Are OP units divisible among heirs?

Yes. Specific unit amounts can be left to individual heirs, avoiding a forced sale or the complications of shared property. Combined with a step-up, that divisibility can support estate transfer.

### Are OP units liquid?

They are less liquid than REIT shares. Units are not typically market-tradable. A holder generally converts them to REIT shares to reach public-market liquidity, and that conversion triggers the deferred gain.

### Are OP units securities?

Yes. Receiving and holding OP units involves securities offered through a broker-dealer to suitable investors after a suitability review. Securities regulation and financial-professional evaluation distinguish this interest from direct real estate.

### How are OP unit distributions taxed?

Partnership and REIT tax rules govern the distributions, and a CPA should address the details. A distribution may include ordinary income or return of capital, each with different treatment, and the K-1 reflects the partner’s share of partnership income. That treatment is separate from the deferred gain on contributed property, which is triggered by conversion rather than by distributions.

### What happens to my OP units if I do nothing?

The owner continues receiving passive distributions while the deferred gain remains deferred and can hold indefinitely. Holding until death may provide heirs with a stepped-up basis. Continued income and deferral can be a valid long-term strategy.

## Glossary

**Operating Partnership:** The partnership through which an UPREIT owns its properties; the REIT is the general partner.

**OP Units:** Limited-partnership units in the operating partnership, economically equivalent to REIT shares.

**Conversion Ratio:** The formula that sets how many OP units are received for contributed property and how units convert to shares.

**Net Asset Value (NAV):** The appraisal-based per-unit value used by non-traded REITs instead of a market price.

**Schedule K-1:** The tax form reporting a partner’s share of partnership income, issued to OP-unit holders.

**Limited Partner:** The OP-unit holder’s role in the operating partnership.

**REIT Shares:** Corporate, tradable ownership in the REIT that can be received by converting OP units.

**Conversion:** Exchanging OP units for REIT shares, generally a taxable event.

**Distributions:** Income paid on OP units, comparable to REIT dividends.

**Section 721:** The code section under which OP units are received tax-deferred.

**Carryover Basis:** The property’s basis carried into OP units, holding the deferred gain.

**Step-Up in Basis:** The death-time reset that can erase deferred gain on OP units.

**Divisibility:** The ease of dividing OP units among heirs.

**Unit Value:** The per-unit value, tied to the REIT’s share price, that sets units received.

**Holding Period:** The time before OP units can typically convert to shares.

**Return of Capital:** A distribution component that may be non-taxable and reduces basis.

**Liquidity:** For OP units, access to tradable shares through taxable conversion.

**UPREIT:** The umbrella partnership REIT whose operating partnership issues OP units.
