# 721 Exchange Eligibility: What Property Qualifies

[Home](baker1031.html) › Insights › 721 Exchange Eligibility: What Property Qualifies

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I keep seeing “eligible” used as if it were a tax answer alone. For a 721 exchange, that is only the first gate. A property can meet the tax standard and still have no practical path because no REIT wants to own it.

Eligibility has two dimensions. The property must be investment or productive-use real estate that can be contributed to a partnership for an interest under Section 721. The REIT must also be willing to accept the particular property under its portfolio criteria. This guide covers qualifying property, REIT acceptance, property types, debt, encumbrances, and the property that does not qualify or fit.

The first-order question is whether the owner holds investment real estate. The second-order question is whether the property fits a specific REIT's type, quality, size, value, location, income, condition, debt, and capital structure. The second question is often the binding constraint. Tax and deal outcomes are fact-specific, and this is educational material rather than tax, legal, or investment advice.

## What Property Can Be Contributed

Section 721 generally allows nonrecognition when property is contributed to a partnership for a partnership interest. In the UPREIT context, the relevant property is usually real estate held for investment or productive use and contributed to the operating partnership for [OP units](op-units-explained.html). Investment rental property, commercial buildings, and similar income-producing or appreciation-oriented holdings are the core examples.

Section 721 is broad about contributed property in the abstract. A REIT operating partnership, however, owns real estate and needs the contributed asset to fit that business. The practical discussion therefore centers on investment or productive-use real estate, not merely on the statute's broad language. For transaction mechanics, see the [721 exchange guide](721-exchange-guide.html).

Personal-use property, such as a home, and dealer property held primarily for sale, such as developer inventory, do not meet the investment/productive-use character described here. The holding standard is similar to the one that applies in a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html).

## The REIT's Acceptance Criteria

The practical gate is whether a REIT actually wants the property. A tax-eligible property still needs to fit the REIT's portfolio strategy and acquisition standards. The REIT usually evaluates property type, institutional quality, location, size and value, financial performance, and physical condition.

A multifamily REIT may seek apartments. An industrial REIT may seek warehouses and logistics facilities. A net-lease REIT may seek net-leased retail, while an office REIT may seek office buildings. REITs generally prefer properties that are well located, well maintained, income producing, and large enough to justify transaction costs. A small, older asset in a non-target market can meet the tax standard yet fail the acquisition screen.

## Investment/Productive-Use Property

Investment property is held for income or appreciation. Productive-use property is used in a trade or business. Rental real estate and commercial buildings are common qualifying examples. Their character is different from a personal residence or dealer inventory held mainly for sale to customers.

For many owners of rental or commercial property, this tax dimension is straightforward. The harder question is often the REIT screen. Investment character does not guarantee that a specific REIT will accept the asset.

## Property Types REITs Want

REITs generally focus on defined sectors. Common 721 candidates include institutional-quality multifamily property, net-lease retail, industrial and logistics real estate, and comparable commercial assets. The property needs to match a REIT's sector focus.

Quality and scale matter. A well-located, well-maintained, income-producing property of sufficient size and value is more likely to attract a willing REIT. A lower-quality, smaller property, or a property in a sector few REITs target, may not find one. Practical eligibility depends on the match, not a generic real-estate label.

## Debt and Encumbrances

Existing debt adds tax and deal complexity. The partnership generally assumes the mortgage or takes the property subject to it. Partnership liability rules can affect basis and can create consequences if the contributor's liability share declines. The REIT also considers the amount and terms of debt and how it fits the REIT's capital structure.

Other encumbrances matter too: liens, easements, title problems, and physical-condition issues affect due diligence and acceptance. Clean title, sound condition, and well-documented financial information make an asset easier to underwrite. Problematic debt, title, or condition can prevent practical acceptance even when the tax standard is met.

### Key Takeaways

- Eligibility has two parts: tax-law eligibility for investment or productive-use real estate and acceptance by a REIT that wants the property.
- REIT criteria—type, quality, size and value, location, and financials—are often the binding constraint.
- Institutional-quality property in a REIT's target sectors, including multifamily, net-lease, and industrial, is more likely to fit.
- Debt and encumbrances affect partnership-tax structuring and a REIT's willingness to accept the property; advisers should address both.

## What Does Not Qualify or Fit

On the tax side, personal-use property and dealer property do not fit the investment/productive-use standard. On the practical side, a property no REIT wants does not fit a direct 721 contribution even if the tax-law test is met. Examples include non-target property types, lower-quality or poorly located property, property too small or low in value, and assets with problematic debt, title, or condition.

If a direct contribution is not feasible, alternatives can include a 1031 exchange into a DST that is structured for a later 721 exit, a 1031 into other real estate, or a taxable sale. A DST bridge can make a later transition to REIT ownership more accessible, but it has its own offering, timing, liquidity, and suitability considerations.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What property qualifies for a 721 exchange?

It requires both tax-law eligibility and REIT acceptance. The property generally must be investment or productive-use real estate contributed to an operating partnership for OP units under Section 721, and the REIT must want the particular asset under its portfolio criteria. Many properties meet the tax standard without fitting a given REIT. The REIT's decision is often the binding constraint.

### Does my property need to be investment property?

Yes, on the tax side it should be held for investment or productive business use. Rental property, commercial buildings, and other income-producing or appreciation-oriented real estate are common examples. Personal-use property such as a home and dealer property held mainly for sale do not meet the standard.

### What are the REIT's acceptance criteria?

The REIT considers sector fit, institutional quality, location, size and value, financial performance, and physical condition. It may seek property above a transaction-worthy scale in target markets with strong income and sound condition. Tax-eligible property still has to meet these acquisition standards to be accepted.

### What property types do REITs want?

It depends on the REIT. Multifamily REITs seek apartments; industrial REITs seek warehouse and logistics property; net-lease REITs seek net-leased retail; and office REITs seek office buildings. In general, they seek institutional-quality, well-located, well-maintained, income-producing property of sufficient size and value in their target sectors.

### Can I do a 721 exchange with a mortgaged property?

Often, yes, but the partnership must assume the loan or take the property subject to it. Debt affects partnership-tax basis and can create a tax issue if a reduction in liability share is treated as a distribution. The REIT also evaluates the debt's amount, terms, and fit with its capital structure. Review the mortgage with advisers before proceeding.

### What property doesn't qualify for a 721 exchange?

Personal-use property and dealer property fail the tax character test. A tax-eligible property may still not fit if it is outside a REIT's sector, too small, lower quality, poorly located, or burdened by problematic debt, title, or condition. Tax eligibility alone is not sufficient.

### What if no REIT wants my property?

A direct 721 may not be feasible. A 1031 exchange into a DST that has a later 721 path, a 1031 into other real estate, or a sale can be alternatives. The DST bridge is more accessible than a direct REIT contribution for some owners, but suitability and offering terms require separate review.

### Is my property's size important for eligibility?

Yes. A REIT often needs enough size and value to justify transaction costs and fit its portfolio. A very small asset can meet tax rules but not be worth acquiring directly. A DST bridge can sometimes be more accessible because the investment is pooled.

### Does the property's location matter?

Yes. REITs focus on target markets. A property in a market the REIT finds attractive is more likely to fit than one in a non-target or weak market. Location is assessed with type, quality, size, and financial performance.

### How do I know if my property is eligible?

Test both dimensions: investment or productive-use character, and a likely REIT fit based on type, quality, size, location, financials, debt, and condition. Investment rental and commercial property often satisfies the first test. A professional can assess the second and compare a direct 721 with a DST bridge or other path.

### Is 721 eligibility the same as 1031 eligibility?

They share the investment or productive-use holding idea and both exclude personal and dealer property. A 1031 focuses on like-kind investment real estate, broadly; a 721 also requires a particular REIT to want the property. That REIT-specific screen means 721 eligibility is generally more constrained.

### Can Baker 1031 help me assess my property's eligibility?

Yes. Baker 1031 helps evaluate investment character and potential REIT fit, including type, quality, size, location, financials, and debt. It can also discuss a direct 721 versus a DST bridge and coordinate with a CPA on tax eligibility and debt structure.

### Does partial ownership, such as a TIC interest, qualify?

It depends. A tenant-in-common interest can be contributable, but the REIT must want the whole property or accommodate the fractional interest. Co-owned property often requires all co-owners to agree or be addressed individually, creating added eligibility and structuring complexity.

### Can raw land or development property qualify?

It depends on the REIT, but typical REITs generally want stabilized, income-producing property rather than raw land or development projects. Specialized REITs may consider land. For most owners, a 1031 into income-producing property or a DST may be more feasible than a direct 721 of raw land or a development-stage asset.

### Does my property's condition affect eligibility?

Yes. The REIT reviews physical condition, title, and financials. A well-maintained asset with clean title and strong income is more readily accepted than one with deferred maintenance, title problems, or weak income. Defects can require remediation or make the property unacceptable.

## Glossary

- **721 Exchange Eligibility:** Whether property qualifies under both tax rules and REIT acceptance.
- **Investment Property:** Real estate held for income or appreciation and eligible on the tax side.
- **Productive-Use Property:** Property used in a business and eligible on the tax side.
- **Personal-Use Property:** A home or similar property not eligible for this purpose.
- **Dealer Property:** Property held primarily for sale and not eligible.
- **REIT Acceptance Criteria:** Required type, quality, size, location, and financial performance.
- **Property Type:** The sector, such as multifamily or industrial, that a REIT targets.
- **Institutional Quality:** The well-located, well-maintained standard REITs commonly seek.
- **Size and Value:** The scale a REIT requires for an acquisition to be worthwhile.
- **Target Market:** A location where a REIT seeks property.
- **Debt and Encumbrances:** Mortgages and liens affecting structuring and acceptance.
- **Partnership Liability Rules:** Tax rules governing contributed debt.
- **Binding Constraint:** A REIT's acceptance, often the practical eligibility test.
- **DST Bridge:** An alternative path when direct 721 participation is not feasible.
- **Section 721:** The code section governing nonrecognition on a qualifying contribution.
- **Holding Standard:** The investment/productive-use requirement, similar to a 1031's.
