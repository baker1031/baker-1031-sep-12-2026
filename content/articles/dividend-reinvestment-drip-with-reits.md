# Dividend Reinvestment (DRIP) With REITs

*Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I have been thinking about how easily a dividend can be treated as a minor event: cash arrives, a setting is toggled, and the account moves on. Over a long holding period, that small choice can materially change the number of shares an investor owns. It can also change the tax records the investor needs to keep.

A dividend reinvestment plan, or DRIP, automatically uses a dividend from a REIT or REIT fund to buy more shares of that same holding instead of paying cash to the investor. Those additional shares can pay dividends of their own, which can buy further shares. That is compounding.

First-order thinking says, “Reinvesting is always better because compounding is good.” Second-order thinking asks whether the investor needs current cash, what is already reflected in the REIT price, whether reinvestment is increasing an already concentrated position, and whether a taxable account creates a tax bill without cash to pay it. A DRIP is a useful accumulation tool for an investor who does not need present income. It is not a substitute for valuation discipline, diversification, or tax records.

## How a REIT DRIP works

A REIT DRIP is straightforward. When the REIT pays a quarterly or monthly distribution, the dividend does not arrive as spendable cash. It is used automatically to buy additional shares of the same REIT or REIT fund. The purchase can include fractional shares so that the full dividend is invested rather than leaving a small cash balance.

There are two common routes.

- A **brokerage DRIP** is enabled through a setting at the brokerage, either for one holding or account-wide. The broker can reinvest dividends from REITs, REIT ETFs, and other stocks into additional shares, usually commission-free and at the market price.
- A **company-sponsored DRIP** is run by the REIT itself or its transfer agent. The investor enrolls directly with that plan. Some company plans have historically offered a small reinvestment discount or optional cash purchases, though they can require more paperwork and separate account administration.

In either case, the dividend buys shares instead of becoming cash. The setting is normally reversible. It is a mechanism, not a prediction about the next share price.

## The power—and limit—of compounding

Compounding is the central reason a DRIP can matter. One dividend buys additional shares. Those shares produce their own dividends at a later distribution. Those dividends buy still more shares. Over time, the share count and the potential income stream can grow without an investor adding new money.

REITs can make this effect more noticeable because they pay relatively high dividends under the **90% distribution rule**. A larger income stream can mean more shares are purchased at every reinvestment. Over many years, reinvested dividends can account for a meaningful part of total REIT return rather than price appreciation alone. [REIT dividend yield](evaluating-a-reits-dividend-yield.html) is therefore a starting point for understanding the cash flow a DRIP may reinvest, not a promise of future income.

The key qualifier is easy to miss. Compounding does not keep a REIT’s price from falling. If shares decline, the reinvested dividends may have bought shares that are worth less later. Reinvesting one REIT can also increase exposure to that one holding over time. Returns and yields are never guaranteed, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

## Tax treatment of reinvested dividends

In a taxable account, reinvesting a REIT dividend does not make it tax-free. The IRS treats it as income in the year paid even though the investor never receives cash. Most REIT ordinary dividends are taxed as ordinary income. Qualified REIT dividends in taxable accounts can receive the **20% Section 199A deduction**, and the investor will generally receive a **Form 1099-DIV** reporting the distributions whether they were reinvested or paid in cash.

That creates a practical cash-flow issue: tax can be due on income that went immediately into new shares. A DRIP does not defer or eliminate that tax. A traditional or Roth IRA is different: reinvested dividends are not currently taxed inside the account. The treatment for an individual account should be confirmed with a tax advisor.

Cost basis is the other important record. Each reinvested dividend creates new shares with a cost basis equal to the amount reinvested, adding to total basis in the position. If those additions are missed, an investor can effectively pay tax twice—once when the dividend was reported as income and again at sale because the recorded basis was too low and gain too high. Brokerages commonly track reinvested-share basis, but it is worth verifying, especially when shares move between accounts or a company plan is used.

## DRIP versus taking cash

The right choice depends on stage of life and purpose.

A DRIP often fits the **accumulation phase**: the investor is building wealth, does not need current income, and wants every dividend returned to work without trying to time additional purchases. It can be a disciplined, hands-off approach to growing a position.

Taking dividends as cash often fits the **distribution phase**: retirement income, current spending, portfolio rebalancing, or a cash reserve may be more important than adding shares. Many investors reinvest during working years and then turn the DRIP off when they need the income. [Income-focused REIT distribution strategies](income-reit-distribution-strategies.html) are part of that broader decision.

The choice is not permanent. A brokerage setting can usually be turned on or off for one holding or the whole account. The question is whether cash is more useful now than one more purchase of the same asset.

## Setting up a DRIP

For most investors, a brokerage DRIP is the simple route. In the brokerage account, turn on dividend reinvestment for a specific REIT, a REIT ETF, or all eligible holdings. The broker then uses dividends to purchase additional whole or fractional shares, typically commission-free at the market price on the payment date. The setting can usually be turned off just as easily.

A company-sponsored plan is the other route. Enrollment happens through the REIT or transfer agent rather than the broker. Some plans may have a small discount or optional cash-purchase feature, but they can involve more paperwork and a separate account. For an investor with several holdings, a brokerage plan is often easier to manage.

Before enabling either version, confirm the details: any commissions, whether fractional shares are purchased, and how the platform records the cost basis of each reinvested share. A one-click setting is useful only if the tax record is reliable.

## DRIPs and account placement

Account location changes the experience. In a taxable account, each reinvested REIT dividend creates current tax and an additional basis record, even though no cash arrives. That annual tax bill can be a meaningful cash-flow consideration.

In a tax-advantaged account—a traditional or Roth IRA, for example—reinvested dividends are not currently taxed and the investor does not face the same taxable-account basis-tracking burden. Because most REIT dividends are ordinary income, many investors find that holding and reinvesting REITs in a tax-advantaged account can shelter that income while compounding continues. The trade-off is that the Section 199A deduction is a taxable-account benefit. For many investors, tax-deferred or tax-free compounding can outweigh that deduction, but the appropriate placement depends on the investor’s full circumstances.

## Key takeaways

- A REIT DRIP automatically reinvests each dividend in more shares of the same REIT, through a brokerage setting or a company plan.
- Compounding means dividends buy shares that generate further dividends; REITs’ higher payouts can amplify that cycle.
- In a taxable account, reinvested dividends remain taxable in the year received and increase cost basis that must be tracked.
- Reinvestment can suit accumulation; cash can suit current income. The setting can change as needs change.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a REIT DRIP?

A REIT DRIP is a dividend reinvestment plan that automatically uses REIT or REIT-fund dividends to buy more shares of the same holding instead of paying cash. Quarterly or monthly dividends can purchase whole or fractional shares. A brokerage DRIP is enabled through a brokerage setting, usually commission-free; a company-sponsored DRIP is run by the REIT or its transfer agent. Both are meant to keep income invested for compounding.

### How does compounding work with a REIT DRIP?

Each reinvested dividend purchases additional shares, and those shares can pay more dividends at later distributions. The cycle can increase share count and income over a long period without new contributions. REITs’ relatively high dividends under the 90% distribution rule can make the reinvested income stream meaningful. The result is not guaranteed, and reinvestment does not protect against a price decline.

### Are reinvested REIT dividends taxable?

Yes in a taxable account. A reinvested dividend is income in the year paid, just as though it had been received as cash. Most REIT dividends are ordinary income, qualified REIT dividends can receive the 20% Section 199A deduction, and Form 1099-DIV reports the distribution. Reinvestment can therefore create tax due without a cash payment. In an IRA, reinvested dividends are not currently taxed. Confirm treatment with a tax advisor; Baker 1031 does not provide tax advice.

### How does a DRIP affect my cost basis?

Each reinvestment adds new shares with a basis equal to the dividend amount used to buy them. That steadily raises total cost basis. Missing those additions can lead to tax twice: first on dividend income and again at sale if the investor understates basis and overstates capital gain. Brokerages commonly track the additions, but the record should be checked, particularly after an account transfer or when using a company plan.

### Should I reinvest my REIT dividends or take them as cash?

It depends on whether the investor is accumulating wealth or using the income. Reinvestment can fit an investor who does not need cash and wants compounding. Cash can fit retirement income, spending, rebalancing, or reserves. Many investors reinvest while working and switch off the DRIP later. The decision can usually be changed at any time as needs evolve.

### How do I set up a REIT DRIP?

The usual method is to enable a brokerage dividend-reinvestment setting for one holding or an entire account. The broker then buys additional REIT, REIT ETF, or other shares—including fractional shares—usually commission-free at the market price on the payment date. An investor can instead enroll in a company-sponsored plan through the REIT or transfer agent. Company plans may have discounts or optional cash purchases but more paperwork. Confirm commissions, fractional-share treatment, and basis tracking either way.

### Is a DRIP better in a taxable or a tax-advantaged account?

A DRIP is often more tax-efficient in a tax-advantaged account because reinvested dividends are not currently taxed and there is no comparable taxable-account basis burden. In taxable accounts, each dividend is current income, although the 20% Section 199A deduction can apply to qualified REIT dividends. A traditional or Roth IRA can shelter the ordinary-income character of REIT dividends while they compound. The correct placement depends on the investor’s situation and should be confirmed with a tax advisor.

### Do all REITs offer dividend reinvestment?

Most publicly traded REITs and REIT ETFs held at a brokerage can use a brokerage DRIP, whether or not the REIT sponsors its own formal plan. Some REITs also offer transfer-agent plans. Non-traded REITs often offer distribution-reinvestment plans whose pricing, availability, and discounts are set by the specific program. Check brokerage settings and the relevant offering documents.

### Can I turn a DRIP off later?

Yes. A brokerage DRIP is normally turned off by changing the account setting, after which future dividends are paid in cash. A company plan may require notice to the administrator and can take effect on the next distribution cycle. Turning the plan off does not change shares already accumulated; those shares continue to carry the cost basis recorded at each reinvestment.

### Does reinvesting dividends guarantee higher returns?

No. Compounding can increase the effect of an investment that performs over time, but it does not change the underlying risk. A declining REIT price can leave reinvested shares worth less, and reinvesting in one REIT can increase single-position concentration. Periodic reinvestment can smooth an average purchase price by buying more shares when prices are lower, but it is an accumulation process, not a guarantee of gains. Returns and yields are never promised.

### What is the difference between a brokerage DRIP and a company DRIP?

A brokerage DRIP is managed by the brokerage. One setting can reinvest dividends from REITs, ETFs, and stocks, generally at market price and often commission-free. A company-sponsored DRIP is managed by the REIT or transfer agent and requires direct enrollment. It may offer a specific feature such as a small reinvestment discount or optional cash purchases, but can mean a separate account and more administration. Brokerage is often simpler for several holdings.

### Do REIT ETFs offer dividend reinvestment?

Yes. A brokerage DRIP can use REIT ETF distributions—typically paid quarterly—to buy additional ETF shares, including fractional shares, usually commission-free. The ETF already provides a basket of REITs, and the DRIP reinvests the combined income into that diversified holding. In a taxable account, the distributions remain taxable in the year received and increase basis.

### Will I still get a 1099 if I reinvest my REIT dividends?

Yes. Form 1099-DIV reports REIT dividends whether reinvested or paid out. It can distinguish ordinary dividends, qualified REIT dividends eligible for the Section 199A deduction, return of capital, and capital-gain distributions. Return-of-capital amounts are not currently taxable but reduce basis. Keep the form and reinvestment records, and confirm the specific treatment with a tax advisor.

### Is a DRIP a good strategy for retirement savings?

It can be useful in the accumulation years. Reinvestment can compound a REIT income stream over a long retirement-saving horizon, and a traditional or Roth IRA can avoid current tax on dividends. As retirement approaches, the DRIP can be switched off so distributions become cash income. Diversify the holdings, size REIT exposure to risk tolerance, and do not treat compounding as a return guarantee. Coordinate account placement and the transition to income with financial and tax advisors.

## Glossary

- **DRIP:** A dividend reinvestment plan that buys more shares with dividends.
- **Dividend Reinvestment:** Using dividends to buy more shares instead of taking cash.
- **Compounding:** Reinvested dividends buying shares that pay more dividends.
- **Brokerage DRIP:** Reinvestment enabled through a brokerage-account setting.
- **Company-Sponsored DRIP:** A reinvestment plan run by the REIT or its transfer agent.
- **Fractional Shares:** Partial shares bought so the entire dividend is reinvested.
- **Cost Basis:** Your investment amount, increased by each reinvestment.
- **Form 1099-DIV:** The form reporting dividends, even when reinvested.
- **Ordinary Income:** How most REIT dividends are taxed, reinvested or not.
- **Section 199A Deduction:** The 20% deduction on qualified REIT dividends in taxable accounts.
- **Return of Capital:** A distribution that reduces basis rather than being taxed now.
- **Accumulation Phase:** The wealth-building stage when reinvesting suits best.
- **Distribution Phase:** The income stage when taking dividends as cash suits best.
- **Tax-Advantaged Account:** An IRA or 401(k) where reinvested dividends are not currently taxed.
- **Transfer Agent:** The administrator that runs a company-sponsored DRIP.
- **90% Distribution Rule:** The REIT rule producing high dividends that a DRIP reinvests.
