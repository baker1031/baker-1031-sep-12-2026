# How to Choose a Qualified Intermediary

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

I keep coming back to the same part of a 1031 exchange: the QI may hold the entire sale proceeds for weeks or months, yet there is no comprehensive federal licensing regime behind that title. That should change the starting point for the selection. This is a custody decision before it is a fee decision.

Your QI may hold the proceeds for the full 180 days. That makes fund security, disbursement controls, bonding, experience, financial strength, fees, red flags, and the firm’s answers to direct questions all worth examining before the relinquished property closes.

First-order thinking says, “A QI is required, so I will use the one who answers the phone or charges the least.” Second-order thinking asks where the funds will be titled, whether one person can move them, what happens if the firm gets into trouble, whether the coverage fits the money at risk, and whether the firm has actually handled this exchange structure through difficult conditions. The latter questions are not overcautious. They are the actual investment in protecting both the exchange and the proceeds.

## Why QI Selection Is So Important

The [qualified intermediary](what-is-a-qualified-intermediary.html) sits at the center of the exchange and holds the sale proceeds for weeks or months. If those funds are mishandled, lost, or trapped in a QI failure, an exchanger may lose both the money and the deferral. Careful replacement-property diligence cannot repair that outcome. [What happens to funds when a 1031 exchange fails](failed-1031-exchange-what-happens-to-funds.html) is therefore not a side question.

There have been cases of [qualified intermediaries misappropriating client funds or failing during financial stress](1031-exchange-fraud-qualified-intermediary-safety.html). They are not the normal case, but they explain why a fast fee comparison is inadequate. A few hundred dollars of price difference is small beside hundreds of thousands of dollars in sale proceeds. For the exchange period, choose the QI you would trust with savings that cannot easily be replaced.

## The Lack of Federal Regulation

Unlike banks and broker-dealers, QIs are not under one comprehensive federal licensing and oversight system. State rules differ. Some states impose bonding, segregated-account, or registration requirements; others impose little or nothing. There is no single federal regulator standing behind the QI.

That leaves the exchanger responsible for the diligence. A firm calling itself a QI does not automatically meet a particular level of fund security or financial strength. The protections are the ones you ask for, verify, and document.

This is not an argument against using a QI. It is required, and most QIs are reputable, well-run businesses. It is an argument for choosing deliberately: established firms with clear controls and a long record deserve more weight than the least expensive firm or the name offered casually by a closing agent.

## Fund Security: Segregated & Qualified Accounts

The central protection is how the QI holds the money. Insist on **segregated, qualified accounts**, preferably a separate account for each client’s exchange. Do not accept proceeds that are commingled with other clients’ funds or, worse, the QI’s operating capital. Commingling can expose funds to another client’s dispute, the QI’s creditors, or a harder-to-detect misuse of a pooled account.

Ask how the accounts are titled, at which bank funds are held, and whether the QI uses qualified escrow or qualified trust arrangements. Those arrangements add a legal layer around custody. A capable QI should answer those questions readily and in detail.

The bank is part of the question. Funds held at a large, well-capitalized institution are safer than money placed with a small or troubled bank. You are entitled to know where proceeds will sit. Evasion on that point should not be treated as a minor inconvenience.

## Dual Authorization and Controls

Where money sits is only half the issue. Ask how it moves. **Dual authorization** requires two approvals for a disbursement, sometimes including the client. It prevents a single person at a QI from moving funds improperly and is one of the stronger protections against internal fraud.

Ask as well about written disbursement procedures, background checks for employees who handle funds, and independent audits of operations and accounts. Those controls are what separate a disciplined firm from one where a single bad actor can do serious harm.

Strong firms usually describe their procedures with confidence because sophisticated clients ask about them. Vagueness or reluctance about authorization and movement should be treated as a meaningful warning.

## Bonding and Insurance

A sound QI carries a **fidelity bond** against employee theft or misappropriation and **errors-and-omissions (E&O) insurance** for professional mistakes. Neither should be merely symbolic. Ask for the coverage limits and compare them with total funds under administration and the size of your transaction.

A bond of a few million dollars is thin protection for a firm holding hundreds of millions of dollars across clients. The coverage needs to be meaningful relative to the exposure. Bonding and insurance do not replace proper custody, but together with segregated accounts and dual authorization they create layers of defense around proceeds.

## Experience & Track Record

Experience is a competence issue and a safety issue. A QI that has handled thousands of exchanges over many years has confronted recurring problems, developed procedures, and shown some staying power through different market conditions.

Ask how long the company has been in business, how many exchanges it handles each year, and whether it has operated through downturns. Longevity alone is not sufficient, but a firm that has weathered multiple cycles is less likely to fail the first time conditions become difficult.

Also ask about your own structure. A QI experienced with ordinary delayed exchanges may be less prepared for a reverse, improvement, or mineral exchange. Each can require specialized documents and sometimes property-parking entities. Experience needs to fit the actual transaction, not just the label “1031.”

## Financial Strength

Because the QI holds the proceeds, its own solvency becomes part of the investor’s risk. A firm with audited financials, a clean balance sheet, and institutional support is less likely to fail in a way that threatens client money.

Ask whether it will provide audited financial statements and whether it is part of, or backed by, a larger well-capitalized organization. Some strong QIs are affiliated with banks or title companies. That backing can add stability and sometimes additional client protections.

Financial strength and custody work together. A weak firm is less threatening to money that is rigorously segregated in qualified accounts, but the strongest combination is a financially sound firm *and* sound fund handling. That is the combination to seek.

## Reputation and References

A QI’s reputation among real-estate attorneys, CPAs, and advisors can be a useful signal because these professionals see performance over many transactions. They learn which firms execute cleanly and which cause problems.

Ask the QI for references. Ask your own attorney, CPA, or advisor which firms they trust. An independent advisor who works with many QIs can often offer perspective based on repeated experience. Do the basic background work as well: look for complaints, litigation, and regulatory actions where available. A clean record developed over years is difficult to manufacture.

## Fee Transparency

QI fees are modest relative to the tax at stake. A standard delayed exchange commonly has a base fee plus per-property charges for additional replacements; reverse and improvement exchanges cost more. Security should remain the primary decision factor, but fee clarity remains a useful sign.

Request a written fee schedule and ask about interest on the funds, often called the **float**. Some QIs retain that interest as compensation; others share it or credit it to the client, particularly for larger balances. Fees plus float show the full economics.

Unusually low pricing paired with fuzzy answers about custody deserves attention. It can signal corner-cutting or a heavy reliance on float income that does not align with an exchanger’s interest in safety. A trustworthy intermediary explains both its pricing and its fund handling without being pushed.

### Key takeaways

- Select for fund security first: segregated qualified accounts, dual authorization, bonding, and insurance.
- Favor an experienced, financially strong QI with a long, clean record.
- Understand both fees and float; unusual cheapness or vagueness is a warning.

## Specialized Experience for Complex Exchanges

If an exchange is not a standard delayed transaction, specific experience becomes essential. **Reverse and improvement exchanges** can require the QI or an affiliate to form and operate an exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT) that parks property under the IRS safe harbor. Not every QI performs this work well.

**Mineral and royalty exchanges** can involve deeds, division orders, and the routing of trailing royalty checks. **DST-heavy exchanges** benefit from a QI accustomed to sponsor coordination and the paperwork for fractional interests. Confirm this experience before engaging the firm. A QI may be excellent at a simple delayed exchange and still introduce costly errors in a structure it has rarely handled.

## Red Flags to Avoid

The clearest warning is reluctance or vagueness about fund holding, title, and authorization. A trustworthy QI should be clear about all three. Commingled accounts, or refusal to confirm segregation, are serious concerns.

Other concerns include a fidelity bond or E&O policy that is small in relation to funds held, a very short operating record, pressure to proceed without complete documents, unusually low pricing paired with evasive security answers, and a history of complaints or regulatory problems.

Trust diligence rather than a sales pitch. A firm that resists straightforward questions about custody, controls, financial strength, and references is communicating something. With the proceeds at stake, it is worth hearing it.

## Questions to Ask Before Hiring

Treat the process as an interview for someone who will hold the proceeds. Ask how funds are held and titled, whether accounts are segregated per client and at which bank, whether dual authorization is required, and what fidelity-bond and E&O limits exist relative to the firm’s funds under administration.

Then ask how long the firm has operated, how many exchanges it handles annually, whether it provides audited financials and references, who receives interest on held funds, and whether it has handled your exchange type—reverse, improvement, mineral, or DST.

The answers and the willingness to give them are the evidence. A QI that responds clearly and specifically is more reassuring than one that deflects. An independent advisor can help frame the questions and interpret the response.

## How an Independent Advisor Helps You Vet a QI

Most investors should not have to vet a QI alone. An independent, sponsor-agnostic advisor who works in exchanges has seen which QIs hold funds securely, communicate under deadline pressure, and execute cleanly, and which ones do not. That accumulated experience is difficult to reproduce from a few calls. It is also part of [choosing a 1031 exchange company](how-to-choose-a-1031-exchange-company.html) across all of its roles.

An advisor can shortlist QIs, help ask the right questions, and distinguish genuinely segregated qualified accounts from protective-sounding language that does not deliver the same protection. The advisor can also match a QI to a reverse, improvement, or mineral exchange when a standard QI is not enough.

An independent advisor has no incentive to favor one QI for its own benefit; the objective is an exchange that closes cleanly. That alignment can be useful when assembling the QI, CPA, and advisor before the sale.

The advisor can also coordinate the QI with the rest of the process: engage the QI before closing, confirm documents, see that funds flow correctly, and keep QI deadlines aligned with the replacement timeline. The QI handles custody and mechanics; the advisor helps ensure those mechanics support a well-structured, on-time exchange.

## Making the Final Decision

Put security at the top: segregated qualified accounts, dual authorization, adequate bonding and insurance, real experience, financial strength, a clean reputation, transparent fees, and any specialized ability your exchange needs. A QI that performs well on those factors can be the safer choice even when it is not the cheapest.

Engage the QI before the relinquished property closes, ideally while the contract is being negotiated. That gives the QI time to prepare documents and gives the investor time to finish diligence. Selecting one in a rush at closing is exactly the position to avoid.

Use the team. An attorney, CPA, and independent advisor may know reputable QIs and ones to avoid. The QI decision is too important to make alone or hurriedly. The right firm quietly supports the entire exchange.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I choose a qualified intermediary?

Start with fund security: segregated qualified accounts, dual authorization, fidelity bonding, and E&O insurance. Then assess experience, financial strength, reputation, and fee transparency. Ask how funds are held and titled, the coverage amounts, annual exchange volume, and whether audited financials and references are available.

### Are qualified intermediaries regulated?

There is no comprehensive federal licensing regime for QIs. State rules vary; some require bonds and account protections and others do little. No single regulator stands behind the firm, so due diligence on security and strength is the investor’s responsibility.

### What protects my funds with a QI?

Segregated qualified accounts, preferably one per client; dual authorization for disbursements; fidelity bonding for theft; E&O insurance for mistakes; and a financially strong QI. Confirm each exists and is meaningful for the transaction before engaging.

### Why is fund security more important than the fee?

The QI can hold the entire proceeds—often hundreds of thousands of dollars—for weeks or months. A small fee difference is insignificant beside loss of funds or a QI failure. Safety comes first.

### What is dual authorization?

It is a control that requires two approvals, sometimes including the client’s, before money moves. It reduces the chance that one person inside the QI can disburse funds improperly and is a strong defense against internal fraud.

### How much bonding and insurance should a QI have?

Enough to matter relative to the firm’s total held funds and the transaction size. Compare the precise fidelity-bond and E&O limits with assets under administration. A small bond at a firm holding hundreds of millions offers thin protection.

### Does the QI’s financial strength matter?

Yes. The QI’s solvency affects the proceeds it holds. Audited financials and, sometimes, institutional or bank backing can reduce the chance of a failure that imperils the funds.

### What red flags should make me avoid a QI?

Avoid vague answers about custody or authorization, commingled accounts, coverage too small for funds held, a very short record, pressure to move without documents, low pricing coupled with evasiveness, and complaint or regulatory history.

### Should my QI have experience with my exchange type?

For a standard delayed exchange, broad experience may be enough. For reverse, improvement, mineral, or DST-heavy exchanges, confirm specific experience because parking entities and specialized documents can create costly failure points.

### How do I research a QI’s reputation?

Request references. Ask attorneys, CPAs, and advisors who repeatedly work with QIs. Check available complaints, litigation, and regulatory actions. Repeat professionals are often a useful source of evidence about reliable and unreliable firms.

### Who keeps the interest on my exchange funds?

It varies. Some QIs retain the interest, while others share or credit it, especially on larger balances. Ask directly. Fund safety should remain more important than chasing yield on the held money.

### When should I select and engage a QI?

Do it before the relinquished property closes, preferably during contract negotiation. That allows correct exchange documents and real diligence. A rushed selection at closing is a setup for poor choices.

### Can my attorney or CPA recommend a QI?

Yes. Professionals who regularly handle exchanges often know which QIs are reliable. An independent advisor can also point to firms it has worked with successfully. Still verify the QI’s security and experience yourself.

### Is a bigger or bank-affiliated QI safer?

Often, a larger or bank-affiliated QI brings more financial strength, institutional controls, and sometimes additional client protection. Size alone is not a guarantee. A smaller QI with rigorous segregation, dual authorization, strong coverage, and a clean record can be sound, while a large firm with weak controls is not automatically safe. Evaluate practices, not only a brand or balance sheet.

### What is commingling and why is it dangerous?

Commingling mixes client funds with other clients’ money or a QI’s operating capital. It can expose proceeds to other parties’ disputes, creditors, and harder-to-detect misappropriation. Demand segregated qualified accounts, ideally one per client, so the funds remain legally and practically separate.

### How do I verify a QI’s fund-security claims?

Ask for written specifics: account title, bank, whether segregation is per client, dual-authorization process, and exact fidelity-bond and E&O limits. Request audited financials and references, and have the attorney, CPA, or advisor review them. Evasiveness is information too.

### Does the cheapest qualified intermediary save me money?

Rarely in a way that should drive the choice. QI fees are small compared with tax deferred and proceeds at risk. A few hundred dollars of savings can trade away stronger protection for hundreds of thousands in custody. Unusually low pricing can also suggest cutting corners or dependence on float. Choose safety and competence first.

### Can I switch qualified intermediaries during an exchange?

It can be difficult and risky after funds are held and contracts assigned because the QI is part of the structure that prevents constructive receipt. Choose carefully before engagement; changing QIs after closing can endanger the exchange. Front-load the diligence.

### What role does the QI play after the exchange closes?

After the replacement closes and any excess cash, or boot, is released, the QI’s active role largely ends. It should retain and provide the documentation needed by you and your CPA for Form 8824 and records. Keep your own copies of the exchange agreement, assignments, identification notice, and closing statements in case the exchange is questioned.

## Glossary

- **Qualified Intermediary (QI):** An independent party that holds exchange proceeds and documents a deferred 1031 transaction.
- **Segregated Account:** A separate account for one client’s exchange funds, not mixed with other funds or QI operating money.
- **Dual Authorization:** Two approvals required to move exchange funds, protecting against internal fraud.
- **Fidelity Bond:** Insurance against employee theft or misappropriation of held funds.
- **Errors & Omissions (E&O) Insurance:** Coverage for professional mistakes by the QI.
- **Qualified Escrow / Trust:** Arrangements that add legal protection to how exchange funds are held.
- **Commingling:** Mixing client funds with other clients’ or operating funds; a practice to avoid.
- **Float:** Interest earned while the QI holds exchange funds.
- **Audited Financials:** Independently examined statements that show a QI’s financial strength.
- **Exchange Accommodation Titleholder (EAT):** An entity that parks property in reverse and improvement exchanges and calls for specialized QI experience.
- **Track Record:** A QI’s history and volume of exchanges, showing competence and staying power.
- **Constructive Receipt:** Access to or control of proceeds that disqualifies an exchange; the QI is intended to prevent it.
- **Institutional Backing:** Affiliation with a well-capitalized organization, such as a bank or title company, that can add stability.
