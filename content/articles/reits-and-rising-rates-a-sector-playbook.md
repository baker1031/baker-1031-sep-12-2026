# REITs and Rising Rates: A Sector Playbook

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

REIT · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read

I have been thinking about how quickly a rate headline can turn “REITs” into one trade in investors’ minds. That shortcut misses the part that matters: the lease, the balance sheet, and the source of the income.

First-order thinking sees rates rise and expects every REIT to fall. Second-order thinking separates a long fixed lease from a nightly hotel room, a floating-rate balance sheet from fixed-rate debt, and a valuation repricing from a weakening property. This is general, educational commentary, not investment advice. Current conditions can differ, and past performance does not guarantee future results.

For the broader structure of the category, start with the [2026 guide to private and non-traded REITs](reits-guide.html).

## How Rates Hit REITs

[Rising interest rates affect REITs through several distinct channels](how-interest-rates-affect-reit-returns.html). First is the discount-rate channel. A property’s value reflects the present value of future cash flows. When rates rise, those cash flows are discounted more heavily. Capitalization rates can rise as well, which pressures property and REIT values even if current rents have not changed.

Second is financing. Most REITs use debt. Higher rates make refinancing maturing debt and funding new acquisitions more expensive, squeezing cash flow and slowing growth. The exposure is greater when debt floats with rates or has large near-term maturities. Third is bond-yield competition. When bonds offer more income with less risk, a REIT distribution can look less attractive until its price or yield adjusts.

Those three channels work at once: lower valuations, higher borrowing costs, and competition for income-seeking capital.

## Rate-Sensitive Sectors

The sectors most exposed are usually the ones whose income resembles fixed income. Net-lease REITs are a clear example. Their long fixed leases, often 10 to 15 years or more with little escalation, create predictable cash flow. When rates rise, the present value of that income can fall much as a bond price falls. The long leases also cannot quickly reset to capture higher market rents.

Healthcare REITs that rely on long leases can share that sensitivity. Mortgage REITs, or mREITs, are often more exposed still. They hold mortgages and mortgage-backed securities rather than directly owning property, and earn the spread between asset income and borrowing costs. A move in rates or the yield curve can compress that spread, reduce the value of fixed-rate mortgage assets, and pressure book value. Significant leverage can magnify each of those effects.

High yield in this area can therefore come with substantial interest-rate risk. Long-duration or spread-based income deserves a closer look than the sector label alone.

## More Resilient Sectors

Short-lease, pricing-power sectors have more room to respond. Residential REITs can reset apartment rents at annual renewals. Self-storage operates on month-to-month arrangements and can adjust often. Some industrial REITs benefit from strong demand and contractual escalators. Hotels reprice nightly, although a rate-driven economic slowdown can still weaken travel demand.

These examples also help explain [which REIT sectors best hedge inflation](which-reit-sectors-best-hedge-inflation.html). The shared feature is the ability to raise rents quickly enough to offset part of the valuation and financing pressure. That is partial protection, not immunity. Pricing power does not make a cyclical business risk-free.

## Balance-Sheet Quality Matters

Across sectors, balance-sheet quality can matter as much as property type. Low leverage, fixed-rate debt, and laddered maturities give a REIT time. Interest cost is more stable, maturities are spread out instead of requiring a large refinancing at once, and management has more flexibility to operate or acquire when a weaker competitor is constrained.

The opposite setup can overwhelm an otherwise reasonable sector position. With heavy leverage, floating-rate debt, or large near-term maturities, interest expense can rise immediately and refinancing can squeeze cash flow and distributions. That is why leverage belongs near the top of the [risks every REIT investor should know](reit-risks-every-investor-should-know.html). Two REITs in the same sector can have very different rate exposure because their financing is different.

## Key Takeaways

- Rising rates affect REITs through higher discount rates that lower valuations, higher borrowing costs, and competition from higher bond yields.
- The most rate-sensitive sectors are long-duration, bond-like sectors: net-lease, healthcare, and especially mortgage REITs.
- More resilient sectors are short-lease, pricing-power sectors: residential, self-storage, some industrial, and hotels, which can raise rent to offset part of the pressure.
- Balance-sheet quality is decisive: low leverage with fixed-rate, laddered debt generally withstands higher rates better than over-levered, floating-rate structures.

## Rate Moves vs. Real-Estate Fundamentals

The market often reacts to rates before it sees the next quarter’s property results. A rate increase can bring a quick, sentiment- and valuation-driven price decline. That move can occur even when rents, occupancy, and income at the underlying properties are still growing.

Over time, fundamentals still matter. A REIT with rising rents, strong occupancy, and a solid balance sheet may grow income in a higher-rate setting, and its price can recover as that becomes visible. A REIT with falling rents, vacancies, tenant defaults, or a stressed balance sheet faces a different problem. Investors should not mistake every rate-driven drop for permanent impairment, but neither should they dismiss real deterioration as merely a rate move.

## Positioning for Higher Rates

The practical playbook favors pricing power and financing resilience. Within a REIT sleeve, that can mean greater emphasis on residential, self-storage, some industrial, and, for investors comfortable with cyclicality, hotels. It can mean more caution toward net-lease, healthcare, and especially mortgage REITs, whose income behaves more like fixed income when rates climb. Within any sector, low leverage and fixed-rate, laddered debt are useful defenses.

This is not an argument to avoid rate-sensitive sectors entirely. Their durable income can be valuable when rates are stable or falling, and rate cycles eventually turn. It is an argument for tilt, quality, and diversification rather than an all-or-nothing rate call. Positioning should fit the investor’s goals and risk tolerance and be revisited as conditions change. It is not a recommendation of a specific REIT, sector, or allocation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do rising interest rates affect REITs?

Rates affect REITs through three channels. Higher discount rates and cap rates can lower value even with unchanged rent. Higher rates raise refinancing and acquisition costs, especially for floating-rate or near-term-maturing debt. Higher bond yields can make REIT distributions less appealing to income investors. The effect is not uniform: pricing-power sectors can grow rent to offset part of the pressure, while sector and balance-sheet differences can be substantial. Verify current conditions, which change.

### Which REIT sectors are most sensitive to rising rates?

Net-lease, healthcare, and especially mortgage REITs are generally most sensitive. Net-lease REITs have long fixed leases, often 10 to 15 years or more, that cannot reset quickly. Long-lease healthcare income can have a similar bond-like character. Mortgage REITs hold mortgages and mortgage-backed securities and earn a spread that can compress as rates and the yield curve shift; leverage can amplify the effects on distributions and book value. This is general commentary, not a recommendation of any specific REIT.

### Which REIT sectors are more resilient to rising rates?

Residential REITs with annual leases, self-storage REITs with month-to-month arrangements, some industrial REITs with strong demand and escalators, and hotels with nightly pricing can reset rent more quickly. That pricing power can offset part of the pressure. Hotels remain economically cyclical, so a rate-driven slowdown can reduce demand. Short leases help, but they do not guarantee results.

### Why are mortgage REITs so sensitive to interest rates?

An equity REIT owns property and earns rent. An mREIT owns mortgages and mortgage-backed securities and earns the difference between what those assets pay and its borrowing cost. If short-term borrowing costs rise faster than yields on longer-term assets, the spread compresses. Rate increases can also reduce the market value of fixed-rate mortgage assets. Because mREITs often use substantial leverage to amplify the spread, both income compression and book-value movement can be magnified. Their high yields therefore reflect substantial rate risk.

### Why does balance-sheet quality matter for REITs when rates rise?

It decides how much financing pressure reaches cash flow. Low leverage, fixed-rate debt, and laddered maturities lock in more borrowing cost, spread refinancing over time, and preserve operating and acquisition flexibility. Floating-rate debt reprices quickly; large near-term maturities can require expensive refinancing and threaten distributions. Two REITs in the same sector may behave very differently because of that difference in financing.

### Do all REITs fall when interest rates rise?

No. REITs as a group may face pressure, but long-duration sectors such as net-lease, healthcare, and mortgage REITs are often more exposed than residential, self-storage, certain industrial properties, or hotels. Low-leverage REITs with fixed-rate, laddered debt can be more resilient than over-levered, floating-rate peers. There is also a difference between a quick sentiment-driven price reaction and longer-term property fundamentals. Verify current conditions rather than assuming a uniform result.

### How should I position a REIT sleeve for rising rates?

The general approach is to favor pricing power, stronger balance sheets, and diversification. That can mean tilting toward short-lease sectors and being more cautious with long-duration, bond-like income. It does not mean an all-or-nothing bet: rate-sensitive sectors can provide durable income in stable or falling-rate periods, and rate cycles turn. The position should fit goals and be revisited as conditions change. This is educational commentary, not an allocation recommendation.

### Are net-lease REITs bad when rates rise?

“Bad” is too broad. Net-lease REITs have a specific vulnerability: long fixed leases, often 10 to 15 years or more, with creditworthy tenants and limited rent escalation. That predictability can be appealing in stable or falling-rate periods. In rising-rate periods, however, the present value of long fixed income can decline and rent cannot reset quickly to higher market levels. A low-leverage, fixed-rate balance sheet can cushion the impact. Weigh durable contractual income against rate sensitivity rather than treating the sector as inherently unsuitable.

### What is the difference between a rate-driven price drop and a fundamental problem?

A rate-driven price drop is a valuation and sentiment response to higher discount rates and bond-yield competition. It can happen even when property operations are intact. A fundamental problem is deterioration in the business itself: falling rent, rising vacancy, tenant defaults, or a stressed balance sheet that impairs income and prospects. The two can be marked down together. A rate-driven decline can reverse if rents grow and the balance sheet remains solid; a fundamental problem may persist or worsen.

### Do REITs eventually recover after rates rise?

REITs with sound fundamentals and strong balance sheets have historically tended to recover over time from rate-driven weakness, but there is no guarantee and timing is unpredictable. Rates do not rise forever, and valuation pressure can ease if rates stabilize or fall. A REIT with declining operations, a stressed balance sheet, or structural sector pressure may not recover. Focus on the distinction between temporary repricing and lasting impairment; past performance does not guarantee future results.

### Should I avoid REITs entirely when rates are rising?

Avoiding the whole category can be an overreaction. It gives up potential income, diversification, and inflation-related rent growth and requires correctly timing the rate cycle. A measured approach is to emphasize pricing power and stronger financing, remain diversified, and be cautious about long-duration, bond-like sectors. That is not a market-timing call or a recommendation; it is a framework for evaluating uneven rate exposure.

### How do rising rates compare to inflation for REITs?

They are related but distinct. Rising rates generally pressure valuations, borrowing costs, and income-yield competition, especially for long-duration sectors. Inflation can help a REIT with pricing power by raising rent and property income. The two often occur together because central banks may raise rates to combat inflation. A REIT can therefore benefit from higher rent while suffering valuation pressure. The outcome depends on pricing power, balance-sheet strength, and whether real rates, meaning rates minus inflation, remain contained.

### Does the yield curve matter for REITs?

Yes, especially for mREITs. The yield curve shows rates across maturities. An mREIT often borrows short-term and holds longer-term mortgage assets, earning the spread. A steep curve, with long rates well above short rates, can be favorable; a flat or inverted curve, with short rates near or above long rates, can compress the spread. For equity REITs, the curve matters more indirectly through financing costs and its signal about growth and inflation expectations. Watch the curve’s shape, not only the headline rate.

### How can I tell if a REIT has a rate-resilient balance sheet?

Start with leverage: lower debt relative to assets or income, often measured as debt-to-EBITDA or loan-to-value, provides more cushion. Then examine fixed versus floating debt; mostly fixed-rate debt does not reprice immediately. Review the maturity schedule, since laddered maturities reduce the need to refinance a large amount at once. Finally, assess liquidity and coverage: cash, undrawn credit lines, and interest-coverage ratios indicate flexibility. Use the REIT’s own disclosures and current conditions rather than the sector label alone.

## Glossary

- **Interest-Rate Sensitivity:** How much a REIT’s value moves with interest rates.
- **Discount Rate:** The rate used to value future cash flows; a higher rate lowers valuations.
- **Cap Rate:** A property’s income yield used to value real estate.
- **Duration:** Sensitivity of value to rate changes; long-lease income is long-duration.
- **Bond-Yield Competition:** Higher bond yields competing with REIT distributions.
- **Net-Lease REIT:** A REIT with long fixed leases producing bond-like income.
- **Healthcare REIT:** A REIT owning medical or senior-housing property, often long leased.
- **Mortgage REIT (mREIT):** A REIT earning a rate spread and highly rate-sensitive.
- **Residential REIT:** A REIT owning apartments with annual leases and pricing power.
- **Self-Storage REIT:** A REIT with month-to-month leases and strong pricing power.
- **Pricing Power:** The ability to raise rent to offset rising rates and costs.
- **Leverage:** The amount of debt a REIT uses.
- **Fixed-Rate Debt:** Debt whose interest cost stays constant as rates rise.
- **Floating-Rate Debt:** Debt whose cost rises immediately with rates.
- **Laddered Debt:** Debt maturities spread over time to reduce refinancing risk.
- **Yield Curve:** Rates across maturities; its shape affects mortgage REITs.
