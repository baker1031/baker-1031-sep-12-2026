# How Much Tax Does a 1031 Exchange Defer?

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

**1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 15 min read**

I have been thinking about how quickly a sale conversation can narrow to one familiar number: the federal capital-gains rate. The actual decision is usually broader. A 1031 exchange can defer four tax layers at once, and a long-held, depreciated property in a high-tax state can carry a combined bill greater than one-third of its gain.

The first-order question is, “What rate applies?” The second-order question is, “What capital remains invested if each applicable layer is deferred, and what do the gain, depreciation, income, state, deadlines, fees, and replacement choices mean for the decision?” This guide quantifies the four layers, uses high-tax-state, long-held, and no-income-tax-state illustrations, and shows how to estimate the deferred amount. It is general education, not tax advice.

## The Question Every Investor Asks

Before committing to the work and discipline of a 1031 exchange, an investor usually wants a number: how much tax would an outright sale trigger, and how much could an exchange defer? That is the right framing. The larger the deferred amount, the more the exchange's effort, fees, and deadlines may matter.

The deferred amount is simply the tax that would be due on an outright sale instead of an exchange. It depends on gain, depreciation taken, income, and state. When the applicable layers are stacked, the result is often larger than expected.

The calculation has four components. Once they are clear, the examples below make the estimate more concrete.

## The Four Taxes You Defer

A fully structured 1031 exchange can defer up to four taxes:

1. Federal long-term capital gains tax at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**;
2. [Depreciation recapture](depreciation-recapture-real-estate.html), up to **25%** on the depreciated portion;
3. The **3.8%** [net investment income tax](net-investment-income-tax.html), or NIIT; and
4. State capital-gains tax, from **0%** to over **13%**.

The layers do not apply to exactly the same part of every gain. Capital-gains rates apply to appreciation. Recapture applies to the amount attributable to prior depreciation. NIIT and state tax generally apply to the whole gain for taxpayers subject to them. Together, they create the effective rate on the gain.

The range can run from the mid-teens for a modest gain, lower income, and a no-tax state to well over **35%** for a large, heavily depreciated gain, high income, and a high-tax state. That spread is why a personal estimate is more useful than a headline rate.

## Federal Long-Term Capital Gains

Property held more than one year is taxed federally at [long-term capital-gains rates of **0%**, **15%**, or **20%**](capital-gains-tax-rates-investment-property.html), depending on taxable income. Most investment-property sellers with meaningful gains land in the **15%** or **20%** bracket on the appreciation portion.

This layer applies to appreciation: sale price less adjusted, depreciated basis, excluding the recapture portion. It is the tax rate most people know. On a long-held property, it may not be the largest component because recapture and state tax can be larger.

A 1031 exchange defers this federal capital-gains tax in full by carrying the gain into the replacement property rather than recognizing it at sale.

## Depreciation Recapture: Often the Biggest Layer

On a long-held rental, the gain attributable to depreciation already taken—[unrecaptured Section 1250 gain](depreciation-recapture-1031-exchange.html)—is taxed at a maximum **25%** rate. The longer a property has been held and depreciated, the larger this layer can become. It is often the largest single tax on a long-held property.

For example, **$200,000** of depreciation can mean roughly that much gain is subject to recapture, or as much as **$50,000** at **25%**, before reaching capital gains on appreciation. A 1031 exchange defers that recapture alongside capital gain. That is a major part of why the strategy can be meaningful for heavily depreciated properties.

## The 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax

Higher-income taxpayers can owe an additional **3.8%** NIIT on capital gains when modified adjusted gross income exceeds certain thresholds. A large property gain can itself push a seller over those thresholds.

Nearly four points can look small until it is applied to a large gain: **3.8%** on **$500,000** is **$19,000**. In a qualifying exchange, no gain is recognized at that point, so the NIIT is deferred with the other layers.

## State Capital Gains Tax

State treatment varies widely. Texas, Florida, Nevada, and Washington impose no state capital-gains tax, so a seller there faces only the federal layers. California taxes capital gains as ordinary income at rates above **13%** at the top.

For a high-income seller in a high-tax state, the state layer can exceed the federal capital-gains tax. Some states also have withholding or clawback rules that can affect deferred gain. A 1031 exchange can defer state capital-gains tax as well, which is why the potential amount is often greatest in high-tax states.

## Key Takeaways

- Four taxes can stack: federal capital gains (**0%** / **15%** / **20%**), recapture (**25%**), NIIT (**3.8%**), and state tax (**0%** to **13%+**).
- On long-held property, recapture is often the largest single layer.
- The combined effective rate can run from the mid-teens to over **35%**, depending on the facts.

## Worked Example: High-Tax State Sale

Consider an illustrative California investor selling a long-held rental with a **$500,000** gain, including **$150,000** of depreciation recapture and **$350,000** of appreciation. Federal capital gains at **20%** on **$350,000** is **$70,000**. Recapture at **25%** on **$150,000** is **$37,500**. The **3.8%** NIIT on **$500,000** is **$19,000**. California tax at roughly **13%** on **$500,000** is about **$65,000**.

Together, that is roughly **$191,500** of tax on a **$500,000** gain, or about **38%**. An outright sale leaves **$308,500** of that gain to reinvest after those taxes. A 1031 exchange instead reinvests the full **$500,000** gain, plus original basis, subject to the requirements of the exchange.

The figures are illustrative. Income, basis, and current rates control the actual result. The example shows why potential deferral can be substantial for a high-basis-depreciation property in a high-tax state.

## Worked Example: Long-Held Property

Now consider a rental held for **25** years with a **$400,000** total gain. Assume **$250,000** is depreciation recapture after a long period of depreciation, and **$150,000** is appreciation.

Recapture at **25%** on **$250,000** is **$62,500**. That already exceeds the **$30,000** of federal capital gains at **20%** on **$150,000**. Add **$15,200** of **3.8%** NIIT and **$20,000** in a moderate-tax state at **5%**, and the combined tax approaches **$127,700** on a **$400,000** gain.

Recapture is the largest part of the illustration. The example is illustrative, but it captures a useful pattern: more years of depreciation can make the recapture layer more important than the appreciation layer.

## Worked Example: No-Income-Tax State

For contrast, consider an illustrative Texas investor with a **$500,000** gain: **$100,000** of recapture and **$400,000** of appreciation. Assume income is low enough to avoid the top capital-gains bracket and the NIIT.

Federal capital gains at **15%** on **$400,000** is **$60,000**, and recapture at **25%** on **$100,000** is **$25,000**. The result is roughly **$85,000**, or about **17%** of the gain. There is no state tax and no NIIT in this illustration.

The same **$500,000** gain can therefore imply roughly **$85,000** of deferred tax in this case and nearly **$191,500** in the California illustration. State and income are not side issues; they are part of the calculation.

## How to Estimate Your Own Deferral

Start by separating total gain into appreciation and recapture. Recapture is roughly the depreciation taken; appreciation is the rest of the gain. Apply the **15%** or **20%** capital-gains rate to appreciation and the **25%** rate to recapture.

Then add **3.8%** NIIT if income is high enough and the state capital-gains rate, which is zero in a no-tax state. The sum is the estimated deferred tax under a fully structured exchange.

It remains an estimate. Exact basis, depreciation schedule, income, and current rates control. A 1031 calculator can organize the inputs, and a CPA can refine the figures for the individual's situation.

## Why Long-Held Properties Defer More

Long-held properties often defer more tax for two reasons. They have commonly appreciated more, increasing the capital-gains layer. More importantly, they have often accumulated more depreciation, increasing the higher-rate **25%** recapture layer.

An investor who owned and depreciated a rental for decades may find that recapture alone exceeds capital-gains tax. That is the profile where the deferred amount can become startlingly large.

It also explains the appeal of repeated exchanges for long-term holders. Each exchange can defer a growing stack of capital gains and recapture. If the final property is held until death, a [step-up in basis](1031-exchange-step-up-in-basis-at-death.html) can eliminate deferred gain for heirs. That outcome depends on facts and law and should not be assumed without tax and legal advice.

## The Compounding Value of the Deferred Amount

The deferred amount is not just a tax number. It is capital that remains available to reinvest. Using full pre-tax gain can make the equity base larger in each cycle, and returns on the deferred capital can [compound over years](how-a-1031-exchange-builds-long-term-wealth.html).

One way to think about it is an interest-free, indefinite loan from the government that remains invested until gain is recognized or eliminated. Over a decade, returns on that amount can exceed the tax itself, and through multiple exchanges the gap can grow. This is an illustration of compounding, not a promise of return.

The deferred amount matters not only because it is not paid today, but because of the capital it can leave working tomorrow.

## Recapture vs. Capital Gains: Which Is Bigger?

The counterintuitive part of this calculation is that depreciation recapture often exceeds capital-gains tax on a long-held property. Investors naturally focus on appreciation, but every dollar of depreciation is subject to recapture at up to **25%**, while appreciation is generally taxed at **15–20%**.

That means a property's depreciation history can be more important than its appreciation in determining the deferred amount. The properties that defer the most are not necessarily those that appreciated the most; they can be the ones depreciated most over a long hold.

It also explains why avoiding boot is critical. Recapture is recognized first when partial gain is triggered. On a heavily depreciated property, a clean, zero-boot exchange protects deferral of the higher-rate recapture, which is often much of the deferred amount.

## The Deferred Tax and Your Reinvestment Power

The deferred amount is the difference in how much capital remains working. An exchange reinvests the entire pre-tax gain plus original basis; an outright sale reinvests only what remains after the tax layers take their share.

In the high-tax-state illustration, that difference is roughly **$191,500** on a **$500,000** gain. At a **6%** annual return, that extra capital alone earns more than **$11,000** in the first year, and it can earn more as it compounds. These figures illustrate a potential reinvestment difference, not an investment result.

Over a decade, compounding on the deferred tax can rival or exceed the tax itself. Across multiple exchanges, the gap can widen. An investor who remains in real estate and ultimately holds until death may also have a potential step-up in basis, but that requires individual tax and legal analysis.

## Putting the Deferred Amount in Perspective

Once the estimate is in hand, weigh it against the cost and discipline of an exchange. Qualified-intermediary fees, the deadlines, and the work of finding a replacement property are all part of the decision.

Selling outright means paying the tax now and reinvesting what remains. Exchanging means retaining the pre-tax capital, subject to 1031 requirements, and potentially deferring it until a later taxable disposition or a potential step-up at death. For a meaningful embedded gain in a long-held or high-tax-state property, that difference can shape wealth for decades.

For most investors with meaningful embedded gain in a long-held or high-tax-state property, the deferred amount is often large enough that a 1031 exchange is worth the effort. Run the individual numbers first: gain, depreciation, income, and state determine whether that conclusion fits the situation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much tax does a 1031 exchange defer?

It can defer federal capital gains (**0%** / **15%** / **20%**), depreciation recapture of up to **25%** on the depreciated portion, the **3.8%** NIIT, and state capital-gains tax from **0%** to over **13%**. In a high-tax state, the combined effective rate can exceed one-third of the gain. A properly structured exchange defers the applicable layers; it does not erase the need for individual tax analysis.

### What are the four taxes a 1031 defers?

They are federal long-term capital-gains tax, depreciation recapture, the **3.8%** net investment income tax, and state capital-gains tax. Capital gains apply to appreciation, recapture applies to the depreciated portion, and NIIT and state tax generally apply to the whole gain for taxpayers subject to them.

### Does a 1031 defer depreciation recapture?

Yes. Recapture on the depreciated portion of a long-held property, taxed at up to **25%**, is deferred with capital gain and NIIT. The tax attributes are carried into the replacement property through carryover basis.

### How do I estimate my 1031 tax savings?

Separate gain into appreciation and recapture, with recapture roughly equal to depreciation taken. Apply the capital-gains rate to appreciation and **25%** to recapture, then add **3.8%** NIIT if applicable and the state rate. The sum is an estimated deferred tax. Confirm it with a CPA.

### Why is the deferred amount bigger than I expect?

Most investors think about the federal capital-gains rate alone. A 1031 can also defer depreciation recapture, often the largest layer on a long-held property, the **3.8%** NIIT, and state tax. Four stacked layers can produce a much larger number than the federal rate alone.

### Is the deferral bigger for long-held property?

Usually, yes. Long-held properties can have more appreciation and, more importantly, much more accumulated depreciation. Recapture at up to **25%** can exceed capital-gains tax on a property held for decades.

### How much does a 1031 save in a no-income-tax state?

Potential deferral is lower than in a high-tax state because there is no state layer. It is then federal capital gains, recapture, and any applicable NIIT. It can still be substantial, but is typically smaller than the equivalent gain in California.

### Does the 3.8% NIIT apply to my property sale?

It can apply for higher-income taxpayers when modified AGI exceeds the relevant thresholds, and a large property gain can push a seller over them. A 1031 exchange defers it along with the rest of the recognized gain, assuming the exchange is properly structured.

### What's the highest the combined rate can be?

For a high-income seller of a heavily depreciated property in a high-tax state, the combined effective rate can exceed **35%** of the gain: federal capital gains at **20%**, recapture at up to **25%** on the depreciated portion, NIIT at **3.8%**, and state tax over **13%**.

### Does a 1031 calculator give an exact number?

No. It is a planning estimate. Exact outcomes depend on basis, depreciation schedule, passive losses, income, state, and current rates. Use a calculator to assess whether an exchange is worth investigating, then confirm figures with a CPA.

### Is the deferred tax ever paid?

It is generally paid when the replacement property is sold without another exchange, at which point deferred gain and the related layers come due. If exchanges continue and the final property is held until death, a step-up in basis can eliminate deferred gain for heirs. Individual tax and legal advice is essential.

### How does the deferred amount keep working for me?

By reinvesting the full pre-tax gain, the equity base is larger each cycle and the deferred amount can earn returns. It can be viewed as an interest-free, indefinite loan that remains reinvested. Over a decade, the returns on that amount can exceed the tax itself, though no return is guaranteed.

### Should I do a 1031 if my gain is small?

It depends. A small gain defers less tax, so the effort and qualified-intermediary fees may not be worth it, especially with a high basis. The potential is generally greatest with a large gain, significant depreciation, and a high-tax state. Run the individual numbers.

### Where can I find a 1031 calculator?

Baker 1031 offers 1031 and capital-gains calculators at [baker1031.com/calculators](calculators.html). They use sale price, basis, depreciation, state, and income to estimate deferred tax. Treat the result as a planning estimate and confirm it with a CPA.

### Does my income bracket change the deferred amount?

Yes. Higher income can place a seller in the **20%** capital-gains bracket and trigger the **3.8%** NIIT, increasing the estimate. Lower income may mean the **15%** or even **0%** capital-gains rate and no NIIT. Income bracket is a key input.

### Is the recapture rate really higher than capital gains?

Yes. Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain, the depreciation recapture on real estate, is taxed at a maximum **25%** rate, above the **15–20%** long-term capital-gains rate. This is why heavily depreciated properties can carry a large deferred-tax amount.

### On a long-held property, is recapture bigger than capital gains tax?

Often, yes. Decades of accumulated depreciation can be substantial, and it is recaptured at up to **25%**. That higher-rate recapture can exceed capital-gains tax on appreciation and become the largest single component of the deferred amount.

### How much extra capital does deferral leave me with?

The deferred tax is capital that stays invested rather than being paid to the government immediately. In the **$500,000** high-tax-state illustration, that is roughly **$191,500** more deployed into the replacement property than an outright sale would allow. That capital can compound year after year, but no return is promised.

### Does deferral matter more for long-term investors?

It often does. Long-term investors can accumulate more depreciation, creating larger recapture, and more appreciation, creating larger capital gains. The deferred amount also has more time to compound. Investors who hold until death are positioned for a potential step-up in basis, subject to individual facts and law.

## Glossary

- **Deferred Tax:** The combined tax a fully structured 1031 exchange postpones—the amount due on an outright sale.
- **Long-Term Capital Gains:** Gains on property held over one year, federally taxed at **0%**, **15%**, or **20%** by income.
- **Depreciation Recapture:** Gain from prior depreciation taxed up to **25%** (unrecaptured Section 1250 gain).
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A **3.8%** federal surtax on certain investment income for higher-income taxpayers.
- **State Capital Gains Tax:** State-level tax on gains, ranging from **0%** to over **13%**.
- **Effective Tax Rate:** The combined rate of all applicable taxes on a gain.
- **Adjusted Basis:** Cost plus improvements minus depreciation; it is subtracted from value to find gain.
- **Appreciation:** The portion of gain above original basis, taxed at capital-gains rates.
- **Realized Gain:** Sale proceeds minus adjusted basis; the total gain potentially taxed.
- **Carryover Basis:** The relinquished basis carried into the replacement property, preserving deferred gain.
- **Compounding:** Earning returns on full pre-tax equity each cycle, the source of deferral's long-term value.
- **Step-Up in Basis:** The reset of basis to fair-market value at death, which can eliminate deferred gain for heirs.
- **Modified AGI:** Adjusted gross income with certain add-backs, used to determine NIIT applicability.
