# What Is a Qualified Intermediary (QI)?

*1031 Exchange · Baker 1031 Research · Updated June 2026 · 16 min read*

[Back to All Insights](insights.html)

I have been thinking about the part of a 1031 exchange that gets the least attention until it goes wrong: who holds the proceeds. The first-order view is that the qualified intermediary is a form-and-wire service. The second-order reality is that the QI is the structure that keeps the taxpayer from receiving the money, and it may hold the entire sale proceeds for months. Fund safety, controls, and independence therefore matter as much as the paperwork.

A qualified intermediary is not optional in a deferred exchange. This guide explains the constructive-receipt rule, what the QI does and does not do, fees, fund security, the limited federal regulation of the industry, and how to vet the person or firm before handing over proceeds.

## What Is a Qualified Intermediary?

A qualified intermediary (QI), also called an accommodator or exchange facilitator, is an independent third party that facilitates a [1031 exchange](1031-exchange-guide.html). It holds the proceeds from the sale and uses them to acquire the replacement property. The QI stands between the taxpayer and the money so the taxpayer has neither actual nor constructive receipt.

Without a QI, the sale and purchase are simply two taxable transactions. By holding the funds and documenting the transaction as an exchange from the outset, the QI connects them into one like-kind exchange for tax purposes.

Independence is not cosmetic. The QI cannot be the taxpayer, the taxpayer’s agent, attorney, CPA, or certain related parties that have acted for the taxpayer during the prior two years. That separation is part of what makes the arrangement work.

## Why You Can't Touch the Proceeds

The basic rule is bright-line: the taxpayer cannot receive the sale proceeds. If the money reaches the taxpayer or comes under the taxpayer’s control, the IRS treats the transaction as a taxable sale and the exchange fails.

The flow is buyer of the relinquished property to QI, then QI to seller of the replacement property. The investor directs the process but does not handle the cash. “I will hold it for a few days,” or routing it through a personal account before the new closing, destroys the exchange. The QI exists to prevent exactly that outcome.

## Constructive Receipt Explained

Constructive receipt catches the investor who never physically takes a check but can access the funds. A taxpayer is treated as having received money when it is available or controllable, even if not spent.

Proceeds placed in the taxpayer’s bank account, in an account the taxpayer can access, or available on demand are constructive receipt. The exchange fails regardless of whether a dollar is used. The exchange agreement restricts access to the QI-held funds until the exchange is complete or fails. Trailing items, such as final rent or royalty payments on the old property, also need careful handling so exchange value is not inadvertently received.

## What a Qualified Intermediary Actually Does

Before the sale closes, the QI prepares the exchange agreement and related documents. It takes assignment of the sale contract, receives proceeds at closing, and holds the funds in a separate account. It receives the written replacement-property identification within 45 days, takes assignment of the purchase contract, and disburses funds to acquire the replacement within 180 days.

It also documents the exchange for qualification and for Form 8824 reporting. In specialized exchanges, including minerals, it can coordinate asset-specific paperwork such as deeds and division orders. What it does not do is give tax or investment advice. A QI is a facilitator and custodian, not the person deciding whether to exchange or what to buy.

## QI Fees and What They Cover

Fees are usually modest compared with the tax at stake: a base charge for a standard delayed exchange plus per-property charges for additional replacements. Reverse and improvement exchanges, which use parking entities, cost more because their structure is more complex.

Some QIs earn income on float—the interest earned while funds are held. Ask how the QI is paid, including whether it retains or shares interest. But a few hundred dollars of fee difference should be secondary to the question of whether the QI’s controls can safely protect the proceeds.

## Fund Security: Segregated & Qualified Accounts

The central service is safekeeping. Look for funds in **segregated, qualified accounts**, ideally a separate account for each client, rather than commingled with other clients’ money or the QI’s operating funds. Ask how the accounts are titled, whether the bank is reputable and well capitalized, and whether qualified escrow or qualified trust arrangements add protection.

Dual authorization is a useful control. Requiring two approvals to move funds, including the client’s in some structures, reduces the danger of one bad actor. A QI unwilling to explain how funds are held and moved is a QI to avoid.

## Bonding, Insurance & Controls

A strong QI carries a **fidelity bond**, which addresses employee theft or misappropriation, and **errors-and-omissions insurance**, which addresses professional mistakes. Both should be sized to the volume of funds the QI holds.

Ask for the actual coverage amounts and judge them against the transaction and total funds under administration. Thin coverage offers thin protection. Background checks, audited financials, and written procedures are further controls. The best firms tend to be transparent because sophisticated clients ask these questions.

## The Lack of Federal Regulation

Unlike a bank or broker-dealer, a QI is not subject to a comprehensive federal licensing and oversight regime. State rules vary: some impose bonding, account, and registration requirements, while others impose relatively little.

That gap makes investor diligence essential. There have been cases of [QIs misappropriating client funds](1031-exchange-fraud-qualified-intermediary-safety.html) and failing under financial stress, [leaving exchangers without proceeds](failed-1031-exchange-what-happens-to-funds.html) and without a completed exchange. The response is not to avoid QIs—they are required and most are reputable—but to [choose carefully, favoring established firms](how-to-choose-a-qualified-intermediary.html) with transparent fund handling, strong controls, and a long record.

## When to Engage Your QI

Engage the QI **before** the relinquished-property sale closes, ideally while the purchase and sale agreement is being negotiated. The QI has to be in position to take assignment of the contract and receive the proceeds at closing.

Once the taxpayer has closed and received the proceeds, engaging a QI is too late. No one can undo receipt retroactively. This is among the most common fatal mistakes in the 1031 process. Reaching out a week or two before closing gives room to prepare documents and conduct the diligence that protects the funds.

## Key Takeaways

- A QI is required and holds sale proceeds so the taxpayer avoids constructive receipt.
- Choose for security first: segregation, dual authorization, bonding, insurance, and transparent controls.
- Put the QI in place before closing. After closing is too late and the exchange fails.

## What a QI Does Not Do

A qualified intermediary is a custodian and facilitator. It does not give tax, legal, or investment advice, and it does not find or vet replacement property.

Use a CPA for tax advice and Form 8824 reporting. Use a knowledgeable, ideally independent advisor for direct, net-lease, or DST replacement sourcing and diligence. The strongest exchanges use all three roles: QI for custody and mechanics, CPA for tax, and advisor for the replacement decision and deadline coordination. Confusing the jobs creates planning gaps.

## Why the QI Requirement Exists

The QI is not an arbitrary middleman. It solves a tax problem. For a sale and purchase to count as one like-kind exchange, the taxpayer cannot receive the proceeds in between. Early exchanges were genuinely simultaneous swaps, which were impractical for most transactions.

Treasury regulations established safe harbors, including the qualified-intermediary safe harbor, allowing an independent party to hold funds without the taxpayer being treated as receiving them. The QI bridges the gap between sale and purchase while preserving non-recognition of gain. That is why a modern delayed exchange can sell today and buy months later, provided the QI holds the funds and the deadlines are met. Without this mechanism, there are just two taxable transactions.

## How the Funds Move: Step by Step

At the relinquished closing, buyer funds go directly to the QI, which has been assigned the sale contract, rather than to the taxpayer. The QI places them in a segregated account. During the exchange period, the taxpayer identifies replacement property in writing to the QI within 45 days. When the replacement is ready, the QI—having taken assignment of the purchase contract—wires the funds to that closing.

The proceeds never pass through the taxpayer’s hands or a controlled account. If excess cash remains, for example because the replacement cost less, the QI releases it as taxable [boot](what-is-boot-1031-exchange-how-to-avoid.html), typically at the end of the exchange period.

## QIs in Reverse and Improvement Exchanges

In a standard delayed exchange, the QI mainly holds funds. Reverse and improvement exchanges use a related arrangement with more work. An **exchange accommodation titleholder (EAT)**, typically formed by the QI, temporarily takes title to one property under the IRS safe harbor in Rev. Proc. 2000-37 because the taxpayer cannot own both old and new properties at once and still qualify.

In a reverse exchange, the EAT parks the replacement until the relinquished property sells. In an improvement exchange, it holds the replacement while exchange funds build or renovate it within 180 days. These structures cost more and are more complex; confirm that the QI is experienced with parking arrangements before relying on one.

## Who Earns the Interest on Your Funds?

Funds held for months can earn interest. Some QIs retain it as compensation, while others share it or credit it to the taxpayer, especially on larger balances. That is not only a fee issue. Where and how the funds are invested affects safety.

A non-interest-bearing segregated account at a strong bank emphasizes safety. Aggressively investing held funds to chase yield introduces risk. Ask directly how interest and custody work. A clear answer is a good sign; evasiveness is a red flag.

## How the QI Fits With Your CPA and Advisor

The QI is one of three roles that rarely overlap. It holds funds, documents the exchange, and executes mechanics, but it gives no advice. Treating it as tax or investment advisor is a category error.

The CPA estimates gain and the four taxes that could be deferred, advises on structure, tracks carryover basis and depreciation across exchanges, and prepares Form 8824. Basis math compounds over a multi-exchange lifetime and becomes indispensable when a step-up at death resolves the deferred gain.

The advisor owns the replacement-property decision. An independent, sponsor-agnostic advisor can source and vet property, help structure for full deferral, manage the 45- and 180-day timing, and coordinate QI, CPA, and lender. The combined cost of the full team is small next to tax triggered by a single error, which is why experienced investors assemble it before listing the relinquished property.

## Red Flags and How to Vet a QI

Vet a QI as you would someone holding your life savings. Ask: How are funds held and titled? Are accounts segregated per client? Is dual authorization required for release? What are the fidelity-bond and E&O coverage amounts? How long has the firm been in business, how many exchanges does it handle, and can it provide references and audited financials?

Red flags include opaque fund handling, commingled accounts, coverage too small for funds held, pressure to move quickly without documentation, and an unusually short record. Transparency and established operations matter. An independent advisor may identify firms used successfully before, but the final diligence responsibility remains with the investor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a qualified intermediary?

An independent third party, also called an accommodator, that holds sale proceeds and uses them to acquire replacement property so the taxpayer never takes actual or constructive receipt. A QI is required for a deferred exchange.

### Why can't I hold the proceeds myself?

Actual or constructive receipt disqualifies the exchange and makes gain taxable. The QI holds the funds between sale and purchase so the taxpayer never controls them.

### What is constructive receipt?

It is being treated as having received money because it is accessible or controlled, even without physical possession. A personal deposit or ability to draw on proceeds is constructive receipt and fails the exchange.

### How much does a qualified intermediary cost?

Generally a base fee plus per-property charges, with higher cost for reverse and improvement exchanges. Some QIs earn interest on held funds. Security matters far more than small fee differences.

### When should I hire a qualified intermediary?

Before the relinquished-property sale closes, ideally during contract negotiation. The QI must take assignment and receive proceeds at closing; engaging one after closing is too late.

### Are qualified intermediaries regulated?

There is no comprehensive federal licensing regime. State regulation varies, which makes diligence on security, controls, bonding, and record essential.

### How do I keep my exchange funds safe?

Use a QI with segregated qualified accounts, ideally one per client, dual authorization, adequate fidelity bonding and E&O coverage, and clear answers about every control. Avoid commingling and opacity.

### Does the QI give tax or investment advice?

No. It is a custodian and facilitator, not a tax, legal, or investment advisor and not a replacement-property finder. Use a CPA and an advisor for those roles.

### Can my attorney or CPA be my qualified intermediary?

Generally no. The QI cannot be a recent agent, such as attorney, CPA, or real-estate agent, during the prior two years. Independence is essential.

### What happens to my money if a QI fails?

It depends on custody. Segregated qualified accounts and adequate bonding are protective; commingled funds at a failed QI can be at risk. Vet controls before engagement.

### Why does the qualified intermediary requirement exist?

The safe harbor lets an independent party hold funds so the taxpayer is not treated as receiving proceeds between sale and purchase. It makes the delayed exchange possible.

### Does the QI find my replacement property?

No. The QI holds money and documents the exchange. It does not source or vet property or provide tax or investment advice.

### How do the funds move in a 1031 exchange?

Buyer funds go to the QI at the relinquished closing, the QI holds them in a segregated account, replacement is identified in writing within 45 days, and the QI wires directly to the replacement closing. Excess cash is taxable boot at the end.

### Who keeps the interest on my exchange funds?

It depends on the QI and balance. Some retain it and others share or credit it, particularly for larger balances. Safety should come before yield because aggressive investment of held funds adds risk.

### Can the QI handle a reverse or improvement exchange?

Only a QI experienced with parking arrangements should. These structures use an EAT, typically formed by the QI, to hold title under the Rev. Proc. 2000-37 safe harbor. Confirm the experience before buying before selling or before funding improvements.

### Is the qualified intermediary fee worth it?

Almost always. Base and per-property fees are modest relative to capital gain, depreciation recapture, NIIT, and state tax that can be deferred—often tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars. The fee buys the mechanism, custody, and documentation of the proceeds.

### How do I research a qualified intermediary's background?

Ask about years in business, volume, audited financials, and references. Confirm segregated qualified accounts, dual authorization, fidelity bond, and E&O limits. With no comprehensive federal regime, favor established firms that explain how they hold and move funds.

## Glossary

**Qualified Intermediary (QI).** An independent party holding proceeds and documenting a transaction so the taxpayer avoids constructive receipt; required for a deferred 1031.

**Accommodator.** Another name for a qualified intermediary.

**Constructive Receipt.** Access to or control over proceeds, which disqualifies an exchange even without physically taking cash.

**Exchange Agreement.** The contract with the QI that establishes the like-kind exchange and restricts the taxpayer’s access to funds.

**Assignment of Contract.** Assignment of sale and purchase contracts to the QI so the exchange flows through it.

**Segregated Account.** A separate account holding one client’s exchange funds, rather than commingled funds.

**Dual Authorization.** A control requiring two approvals to move exchange funds.

**Fidelity Bond.** Insurance protecting held funds against employee theft or misappropriation.

**Errors & Omissions Insurance.** Coverage for professional mistakes by the QI.

**Commingling.** Mixing multiple clients’ funds, or client and operating funds, in one account; a practice to avoid.

**Qualified Escrow / Trust.** Arrangements adding a protective layer to the custody of exchange funds.

**Float.** Interest earned on exchange funds while held during the exchange period.

**Form 8824.** The IRS form used to report a like-kind exchange and carryover basis.

**Independence Requirement.** The rule that the QI cannot be the taxpayer’s recent attorney, CPA, broker, or other agent.
