By Jerry Baker · Founder & Managing Principal, Baker 1031 Investments · Reviewed September 8, 2026

Income from lending against real estate rather than owning the buildings. Access typically comes through REITs, including 721 exchange-eligible structures.

Request Investment Access

Every real estate deal has an owner and a lender. Credit is the other side of the table: instead of buying the building and hoping it appreciates, you lend against it and collect interest. Your return is contractual rather than residual, and your risk is that the borrower cannot pay and the collateral is not worth what it was underwritten at.

For a 1031 investor there is one fact to settle immediately. Exchange proceeds cannot go here. Debt instruments are excluded from the definition of real property. Credit is where taxable money goes — the boot, the portion you decided not to exchange, or money that was never in an exchange at all.

What you are investing in

A position in the capital stack. From safest to riskiest: senior mortgage debt, then mezzanine debt, then preferred equity, then common equity. Each layer is paid only after the one above it.

"Debt is safer than equity" is the shorthand, and it is not reliable. Position in the stack is what determines risk, not the label. A listed mortgage REIT puts it bluntly in its own risk factors: mezzanine loans "are by their nature structurally subordinated to more senior property-level financings … As a result, a partial loss in the value of the underlying collateral can result in a total loss of the value of the mezzanine loan" (Blackstone Mortgage Trust, Form 10-K FY2025).

The legal mechanics are worth knowing because they explain why. A lender is secured only up to the value of the collateral — beyond that the claim is unsecured (11 U.S.C. §506(a)(1)) — and a subordination agreement is enforced as written (§510(a)). Subordination reorders who absorbs the loss. It does not create value.

What you actually own, by vehicle

Retail investors almost never own loans directly. What you own is a share of an entity that does, and the entity's regulatory identity determines your protections and your liquidity.

That table is the single most useful thing on this page. "Private credit" is used loosely enough to cover all four, and they are not the same investment.

What your responsibilities are

Modest, and mostly about entering with clear eyes. Meet the accreditation or suitability standard where one applies. Read the repurchase or redemption terms before you need them, not after. Understand that your income is ordinary and plan for the tax. Beyond that, you are a passive holder.

Who manages it, and what they are on the hook for

A manager originates or buys the loans, underwrites them, monitors them, and works them out when they go wrong. That last function is the one that separates managers, and it is invisible until it matters.

Two decisions belong to the manager and land on you. Leverage: many credit vehicles borrow against their loan book, which magnifies both the yield and the loss. And redemptions: in a non-traded vehicle the board decides whether to honor, prorate, reduce or suspend your request.

Does this qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No, in every form. Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5)(i) excludes from real property, "regardless of the classification of such property under State or local law": stock, "bonds, or notes"; "other securities or evidences of indebtedness or interest"; partnership interests; certificates of trust or beneficial interests; and choses in action.

A mortgage note is an evidence of indebtedness. A debt-fund LP interest is a partnership interest. A BDC or interval fund share is a security. A mortgage REIT share is stock. All excluded.

If anyone offers you a "1031-eligible mortgage fund" or a "debt DST," that is the highest-priority correction in this whole category. A DST works as replacement property because its owners are treated as owning the underlying real estate. A trust holding notes does not produce that result.

What it does to your tax picture

Moving from owning to lending changes the character of everything, and mostly not in your favor:

One partial offset. If the credit is held through a REIT, the ordinary dividend is generally a qualified REIT dividend eligible for the 20% deduction under §199A(e)(3) — a dividend that is neither a capital gain dividend nor qualified dividend income. That deduction was made permanent in 2025. It does not reach BDC or interval fund income, because those are regulated investment companies rather than REITs, and §199A(e)(3) reaches dividends "from a real estate investment trust."

Who invests in this

  • Someone deploying the taxable slice of a sale — boot, or proceeds never intended for exchange.
  • Someone who wants current income and is willing to give up appreciation to move up the stack.
  • Someone who has decided they want real estate exposure without owning the residual risk of a specific building.
  • An IRA or other tax-deferred account, where the ordinary-income character costs nothing.

Who does not

  • Anyone completing a 1031 exchange. The proceeds cannot come here.
  • Anyone in a high bracket investing taxable money for after-tax income, without doing the arithmetic. Ordinary income plus the 3.8% surtax and no depreciation is a materially different outcome from a rental property's headline yield.
  • Anyone who believes "debt" means "safe." A mezzanine or preferred sleeve can be wiped out by a partial decline in collateral value.
  • Anyone relying on the stated redemption frequency. See the next section — that reliance was tested in 2026 and the caps held.

What to expect as an investor

Regular income, usually monthly or quarterly, more predictable than equity distributions because it is contractual at the borrower level. A 1099 or a K-1 depending on vehicle. Little or no appreciation — you are being paid to lend, not to own. And liquidity that is defined by the vehicle's own terms rather than by a market.

On that last point the SEC is direct about interval funds: "You will not be able to sell your fund shares whenever you want. You must wait for the next repurchase offer, which could be as long as twelve months away," and "you may actually only be able to sell a certain percentage of the shares you request to have repurchased" (Investor Bulletin: Interval Funds). On non-traded BDCs: shares "are illiquid, which means they cannot be sold readily in the market," and investors "can generally only sell their shares when the BDC offers to repurchase them" (Investor Bulletin: Non-traded BDCs).

The risks

  • Credit risk. The borrower stops paying. Recovery depends on collateral value and your position.
  • Subordination risk. Below the senior loan, a modest decline in value can take you to zero.
  • Leverage at the fund level. A BDC can operate at 150% asset coverage — roughly $2 borrowed per $1 of equity — which cuts both ways.
  • Redemption risk. Documented and current. See below.
  • Valuation risk. Private loans are marked by the manager. The SEC's Investor Advisory Committee noted in September 2025 that NAV calculations for these vehicles "are not governed by governmental or independent securities, financial or accounting rules," and pointed to "valuation discrepancies across different BDCs holding stakes in the same loans."
  • Interest rate risk. Floating-rate loans reprice; fixed-rate paper loses value when rates rise.
  • Complexity. FINRA names interval funds and non-traded REITs among complex products requiring heightened supervision (Regulatory Notice 22-08).

What the current data actually shows

Two numbers, both from the Federal Reserve, that a balanced conversation should include.

Commercial real estate credit is stressed but not broken. The delinquency rate on commercial real estate loans at all commercial banks (series DRCRELEXFACBS) ran 0.78% in the first quarter of 2023, roughly doubled to about 1.55%, and has sat near that level for six quarters — 1.53% in the second quarter of 2026. For scale, the post-financial-crisis peak was 8.76% in the second quarter of 2010. Anyone implying current conditions resemble 2009 is contradicted by the Fed's own series; anyone implying nothing has changed since 2022 is contradicted by the same one.

The redemption caps in semi-liquid credit funds were reached this year. The Federal Reserve's May 2026 Financial Stability Report reports that redemption requests "increased from relatively low levels during the fourth quarter of 2025 and accelerated further in the first quarter of 2026," that "most managers chose to cap redemptions at 5 percent of NAV," and that "accepted redemption requests somewhat exceeded new inflows in the first quarter of 2026, the first time this has occurred." The Fed's own assessment is that the resulting financial-stability risks "appear limited and manageable" — but for an individual investor wanting out, a cap that binds is a cap that binds.

The worst case

A subordinate position in a deal that goes wrong: the property is worth less than the senior debt, the senior lender takes the collateral, and your position recovers nothing. Total loss, from a partial decline in value — the sponsors' own filings say so.

The second worst case is milder but far more common: the loans are performing, the fund is fine, and you simply cannot get your money when you ask for it. Your request is prorated at the cap, then again the next quarter, while the board considers whether to reduce the cap further. Nothing has defaulted. You just do not have your money.

The best case

The loans perform, you collect contractual income at a rate above what the same sponsor's equity would have distributed early in a project's life, and you get your principal back at maturity without ever taking a view on where cap rates go. Held through a REIT the income carries the 20% §199A deduction; held in an IRA the ordinary character costs nothing. For an investor who wants real estate income without real estate residual risk, this is the structure that delivers it.

The investment process

  1. Confirm the money is taxable, not exchange proceeds. This is step one and it is disqualifying.
  2. Pick the vehicle type — listed, interval fund, non-traded BDC or REIT, or private fund. The liquidity and regulatory differences are larger than the yield differences.
  3. Qualify where accreditation or suitability applies.
  4. Read the repurchase terms and the fee table.
  5. Subscribe, and note any early-redemption discount period.
  6. Hold, collecting income and reporting it as ordinary.
  7. Exit by sale (listed), repurchase offer (interval fund or non-traded vehicle, subject to caps), or wind-down.

How to review one of these

  1. Where in the stack, precisely? Senior, mezzanine, preferred equity, or a blend — and if a blend, what percentage in each.
  2. Loan-to-value, and measured against what. An appraisal from when? By whom? For context, bank supervisory LTV limits under the interagency real estate lending guidelines are 65% for raw land, 75% for land development, 80% for commercial construction and 85% for improved property. Those bind banks, not private lenders — but they are the honest benchmark against which a "conservative" claim should be read.
  3. Fund-level leverage, and what happens to it if asset values fall.
  4. The redemption plan, and its actual history. Caps, proration, early-redemption discount, board suspension rights — and whether requests have been fully honored in the last eight quarters. Filings disclose this.
  5. Who marks the book, how often, and with what independent input.
  6. Non-accrual and modification rates, and what the manager did with troubled loans rather than what they say about workouts.
  7. Whether distributions are covered by net investment income, or supplemented from capital or borrowings.
  8. Concentration — borrowers, property types, geography, and vintage.

Frequently asked questions

Can I 1031 exchange into a mortgage fund or debt DST?

No. Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5)(i) excludes notes, evidences of indebtedness, securities and partnership interests from the definition of real property, regardless of state law. A trust holding loans does not produce the result a DST holding real estate does. If a marketing piece says otherwise, that is the error to correct first.

Is real estate debt safer than equity?

It is senior to equity in the same deal, which is not the same thing. Your risk depends on where you sit in the stack and on the loan-to-value beneath you. A mezzanine position can be wiped out entirely by a partial decline in collateral value — Blackstone Mortgage Trust says exactly that in its own 10-K risk factors.

What is the difference between a BDC and an interval fund?

An interval fund is a registered investment company under the Investment Company Act, making periodic repurchase offers of 5% to 25% of shares under Rule 23c-3. A BDC is not a registered investment company; it elects BDC status and is subject to a different set of provisions, including a 70% qualifying-asset test and leverage limits. The SEC states plainly that "BDCs are not registered with the SEC as investment companies."

How is the income taxed?

Interest is ordinary income under §61(a)(4), with no depreciation to shelter it, plus the 3.8% net investment income tax where applicable. Held through a REIT, the ordinary dividend generally qualifies for the 20% deduction under §199A(e)(3). Held through a BDC or interval fund, it does not — those are regulated investment companies, not REITs.

Can I get my money out quarterly?

You can request it. Whether you receive it depends on the cap, the queue and the board. The Federal Reserve reported that in the first quarter of 2026 "most managers chose to cap redemptions at 5 percent of NAV," and that accepted redemptions exceeded new inflows for the first time. Read any liquidity claim as capped, prorated and suspendable.

What happens if the borrower defaults?

The lender enforces against the collateral. A secured claim is secured only up to the value of that collateral (11 U.S.C. §506(a)(1)); anything beyond is unsecured. Whether you see any of it depends on your position and on what the property is actually worth, which is why LTV and stack position matter more than the stated coupon.

How bad is commercial real estate credit right now?

Elevated and stable rather than deteriorating. Bank CRE delinquencies roughly doubled from 0.78% in early 2023 to about 1.55%, have held near that for six quarters, and were 1.53% in the second quarter of 2026 — against a 2010 peak of 8.76%. The Fed also notes CRE prices stabilizing after significant declines, with refinancing needs the live vulnerability.

What LTV should I expect?

It varies by strategy, and there is no regulatory standard for private lenders. The interagency supervisory limits for banks — 65% raw land, 75% land development, 80% commercial construction, 85% improved property — are a useful benchmark but do not bind a mortgage REIT or a private fund. Ask for the weighted-average LTV, the date of the appraisals, and who performed them.

Are the yields I am quoted reliable?

They are targets, not contracts. A yield is a function of credit risk taken and leverage employed, and a number materially above comparable alternatives is telling you something about one of those two. Ask which.

Does this work in an IRA?

The ordinary-income character costs nothing in a tax-deferred account, which makes credit a reasonable fit. Note that leverage inside an investment can create unrelated business taxable income for a retirement account, so the vehicle's structure matters. That is a question for your custodian and your CPA on the specific fund.
