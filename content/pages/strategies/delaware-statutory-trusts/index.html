By Jerry Baker · Founder & Managing Principal, Baker 1031 Investments · Reviewed September 8, 2026

Own a fractional interest in institutional real estate held by a trust. A passive replacement-property option, without day-to-day management.

Request Investment Access

If you have just sold an investment property and you do not want to become a landlord again, a DST is the structure most exchangers end up looking at. You buy a fractional interest in a trust that already owns institutional real estate, the trust already has its financing in place, and a professional sponsor runs it. For federal tax purposes you are treated as owning a slice of the underlying real estate, which is what lets a DST interest work as replacement property in a 1031 exchange.

That last sentence is doing a lot of work, and the rest of this page is mostly about what sits underneath it — what you own, what you give up, and what has to be true for the tax treatment to hold.

What you are investing in

A Delaware statutory trust is a real entity under Delaware law, not a tax fiction. It is created by a governing instrument and a certificate of trust filed with the state, and it holds property for the benefit of its beneficial owners (12 Del. C. §3801). Beneficial owners get the same limitation of personal liability that stockholders of a Delaware corporation get — though note the statute makes that a default the trust agreement can change (§3803(a)), so it is a document to read rather than a fact to assume.

In practice the sponsor identifies a property or portfolio, arranges the debt, forms the trust, and sells beneficial interests to accredited investors. What reaches you is a finished transaction: the property is already under contract or already owned, the loan terms are already set, the rent roll is what it is. You are deciding whether to join something, not helping to build it.

Worth knowing that Delaware law itself imposes none of the restrictions this structure is famous for. A Delaware statutory trust can be as active as its trust agreement allows. The restrictions come from federal tax requirements written into the governing instrument, which is a distinction that matters when you read the documents.

What you actually own

You own a beneficial interest in the trust. What that gets you, for tax purposes, is the interesting part.

In Rev. Rul. 2004-86 the IRS held that a DST meeting certain conditions is an investment trust under Treas. Reg. §301.7701-4(c), classified as a trust rather than a business entity, and that its beneficial owners are treated as the owners of the trust's assets. In the ruling's words, the investors "are each considered to own an undivided fractional interest in Blackacre for federal income tax purposes," so the exchange is "the exchange of real property for an interest in Blackacre, and not the exchange of real property for a certificate of trust or beneficial interest."

Be careful with the shorthand here, because it is everywhere and it is not quite what the ruling says. The IRS did not rule that a DST interest is real property. It ruled that the trust is a grantor trust and that a grantor is treated as owning the underlying assets — and the real-property conclusion follows from that. The distinction matters because the tax treatment depends on the grantor-trust characterization holding up, not on a label.

Practically, that deemed ownership is what gives you a share of depreciation, a share of the rental income, and a carryover basis that follows you out of your old property and into this one (IRC §1031(d)).

What your responsibilities are

Fewer than you are used to, which is the point, and more than nothing.

  • Qualify. DST interests are sold under Regulation D, so you have to be an accredited investor — broadly, over $1 million in net worth excluding your primary residence, or $200,000 of individual income ($300,000 joint) in each of the two most recent years with a reasonable expectation of the same this year (17 CFR §230.501(a)(5), (a)(6)). Since 2020 holding a Series 7, 65 or 82 in good standing also qualifies you (SEC Release 33-10824).
  • Hit the deadlines. 45 days to identify, 180 days to close, and the 180 is measured to the earlier of that day or your return's due date including extensions (§1031(a)(3); Treas. Reg. §1.1031(k)-1(b)(2)). These are statutory. There is no hardship extension — the only relief is a federally declared disaster where the IRS publishes guidance covering it (Rev. Proc. 2018-58, §17).
  • Do not touch the money. Sale proceeds go to a qualified intermediary. If you receive or control them, even briefly, the exchange fails on constructive receipt (§1.1031(k)-1(f)(1), (g)(4)).
  • Read the PPM. The private placement memorandum is the offering. Everything that governs your investment — fees, debt terms, sponsor obligations, what happens on a sale — is in it, and nothing anyone says on a call changes it.
  • File. Form 8824 for the exchange year, and your share of trust items every year after.

What is not your responsibility: leasing, maintenance, capital calls in the ordinary case, the loan, the tenants, or the eventual sale.

Who manages it, and what they are on the hook for

A DST has a trustee and, in nearly every offering, a sponsor affiliate that manages the property. Delaware law puts the business "under the direction of its trustees" unless the governing instrument says otherwise (12 Del. C. §3806), and the governing instrument almost always does say otherwise — delegating day-to-day operation to the sponsor.

Here is the part that surprises people. The trustee's powers are deliberately, severely limited, because that is the price of the tax treatment. In the situation the IRS ruled on, the trustee may not:

  • "exchange Blackacre for other property"
  • "purchase assets other than the short-term investments described above"
  • "accept additional contributions of assets (including money) to DST"
  • "renegotiate the terms of the debt used to acquire Blackacre"
  • "renegotiate the lease with Z or enter into leases with tenants other than Z, except in the case of Z's bankruptcy or insolvency"

— and may make "only minor non-structural modifications" to the property unless the law requires more. Cash between distributions goes into short-term obligations and is paid out quarterly.

The industry calls these the "seven deadly sins." That phrase does not appear in the ruling, in any IRS publication, or in anything the SEC or FINRA has published. The IRS does not number them and does not present them as a checklist. They are facts about the trust the IRS was asked to rule on, and they are the factual predicate for the holding. Sponsors write them into the trust agreement because a trust that behaves differently is a different trust than the one the IRS blessed.

The practical consequence is the thing to internalize: the manager cannot rescue a deal by restructuring it. A trustee who could renegotiate the loan, raise more equity, or re-tenant the building would be a trustee whose trust is probably not a grantor trust any more. The rigidity that protects the tax treatment is the same rigidity that removes the tools a normal owner would reach for in trouble.

Most trust agreements answer this with a conversion clause — if the trustee determines action outside those limits is necessary to preserve the property, the trust converts to an LLC taxed as a partnership. The industry calls it a "springing LLC." There is no IRS ruling and no Delaware statutory provision addressing the mechanism; it is a drafting convention. What is not in doubt is the consequence: once you hold a partnership interest, you hold something that Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5) expressly says is not real property, so a 1031 exchange on the way out is gone.

Does this qualify for a 1031 exchange?

Yes, subject to the conditions above — and the authority is worth knowing precisely, because this is the question the whole page turns on.

Rev. Rul. 2004-86 holds that "a taxpayer may exchange real property for an interest in the Delaware statutory trust described above without recognition of gain or loss under §1031, if the other requirements of §1031 are satisfied." Two limits sit in that sentence. "Described above" means the ruling's own fact pattern — one property, one net-leased tenant, nonrecourse debt, a trustee with no discretion. And "the other requirements of §1031" means everything else still applies.

There is an obvious objection, and it deserves a straight answer. The 2020 regulations defining real property expressly exclude "certificates of trust or beneficial interests" (§1.1031(a)-3(a)(5)). So how does a DST interest survive? Because the grantor-trust analysis happens first: you are not treated as receiving a trust certificate, you are treated as receiving the underlying real property. Treasury said so directly in the preamble to those regulations — "nothing in the proposed regulations or the TCJA is contrary to the view that a transfer of an interest in a DST, if a grantor trust, is treated as the transfer of the underlying property held by the DST" (T.D. 9935, 85 FR 77365).

Note the conditional. If a grantor trust. A DST that drifts outside the ruling's conditions and gets classified as a business entity instead is holding a partnership interest, and a partnership interest is excluded. The tax result is not a property of the acronym; it is a property of how the trust is written and run.

Two related points that get oversold:

  • Identification. Because you are buying a fractional interest, identification practice names the DST and a dollar amount. The three-property, 200% and 95% rules apply as they always do (§1.1031(k)-1(c)(4)). There is no IRS-blessed format specific to DSTs; your QI and your CPA should agree the wording.
  • Depreciation. A DST does not hand you a fresh depreciation schedule, whatever a brochure says. Under Treas. Reg. §1.168(i)-6 your carried-over basis keeps depreciating on the old property's remaining recovery period, method and convention. Only the excess basis — new money above what carried over, including your share of debt above your old debt — starts a new schedule. For a long-held relinquished property that carried-over piece is often nearly exhausted.

Who invests in this

The recurring profile is narrower than the marketing suggests. In practice it is someone who:

  • has sold appreciated real estate and faces a tax bill large enough that deferral changes the arithmetic;
  • is done managing property — retiring, moving, dividing an estate, or simply tired of the 11pm call;
  • needs to place an exact dollar amount to avoid boot, and a fractional interest lets them hit the number;
  • wants to spread one property's proceeds across several assets, markets or sponsors;
  • can genuinely leave the money alone for the whole hold, because there is no way to get it back early; and
  • is investing money that is not their liquidity.

Who does not

This list is the one most competitor pages leave out, and it is the more useful of the two.

  • Anyone who might need the money. There is no redemption right and no meaningful secondary market. Illiquidity here is not a discount you accept, it is a fact you live with for the whole term.
  • Anyone who wants control. You cannot influence the lease, the loan, the capital plan or the timing of a sale. If not being able to act on your own read of the market would keep you up at night, this structure will.
  • Anyone whose exchange math does not work. If the deferral is small relative to what you give up, paying the tax and investing freely can be the better answer. That comparison is arithmetic, and it should be run before anything is identified.
  • Anyone who is not accredited. Not a judgment — a rule.
  • Anyone buying because the 45th day is close. The clock is a terrible reason to own something for ten years. It is, however, the most common one.

What to expect as an investor

Mechanically: you receive distributions, usually monthly or quarterly, made from property cash flow after debt service and expenses. You get an annual statement of your share of income, expenses and depreciation for your return. You get periodic sponsor reporting on the property. You get no vote on operations.

Two expectations worth setting honestly. First, distributions are not interest — they are a share of what the property produces, they are not guaranteed, and they can be reduced or suspended. Second, whatever rate is discussed at the outset is a projection in an offering document, not a promise; the PPM will say so, and it means it.

The hold is typically several years to a decade, and the timing is not yours. The sponsor decides when to sell, subject to the trust's terms. Your job at the end is to be ready for a decision you did not schedule.

The risks

Structure-level first, because these exist because of how a DST is built:

  • The trustee cannot adapt. No debt renegotiation, no new equity, no re-leasing outside a tenant bankruptcy. A problem that a normal owner would refinance out of has fewer exits here.
  • Conversion risk. The springing-LLC clause is the pressure valve, and pulling it costs you the ability to 1031 out.
  • Concentration. Many DSTs hold one property, sometimes with one tenant. The ruling's own fact pattern is a single net-leased building. One asset, one market, sometimes one credit.
  • Leverage. DST debt is typically fixed-rate and nonrecourse, which is good, but it matures. Maturity inside a structure that cannot renegotiate is the most important date in the offering.
  • Sponsor dependence. You are underwriting a manager as much as a building — their track record, their balance sheet, and their behavior in a bad year.
  • Illiquidity and valuation. No public market, no daily price, and no reliable way to sell before the sponsor sells.
  • Tax risk. If the trust fails the grantor-trust conditions, the analysis that makes the whole thing work stops applying.
  • Fees. Load, acquisition, asset management and disposition fees all reduce what reaches you and are disclosed in the PPM. They are a diligence item, not a footnote.

Then the ordinary real-estate risks, which have not gone anywhere: tenants default, markets soften, insurance and taxes rise, buildings need capital.

The worst case

Stated plainly, because it is worse than "you could lose your investment," and this is the single most under-disclosed thing about the structure.

If the property fails — tenant gone, loan matures into a market that will not refinance, lender forecloses — your beneficial interest can go to zero. That is the part everyone expects.

The part they do not: you can lose the investment and owe tax in the same year. You carried a low basis into this from the property you sold (§1031(d)), and because you are treated as owning the underlying real estate, a disposition of that real estate — including an involuntary one, with debt relief counted as proceeds — is a taxable event measured against that low basis. The deferred gain from the original sale does not disappear when the building does. Anyone considering leverage in a DST should have their CPA model that specific scenario before signing, not after.

The best case

The property performs, distributions arrive on schedule, the sponsor sells into a decent market, and you take the proceeds into another 1031 exchange — another DST, or back into property you manage yourself — with the original deferral intact and a new one layered on it. That can repeat.

If you hold to the end of your life, your heirs take a basis stepped to fair market value at your death (§1014(a)), and the deferred gain that has been rolling forward is not theirs to pay. For a lot of families that is the actual plan, and it is a legitimate one. It requires never needing the money.

The investment process, step by step

  1. Before you sell. The single highest-leverage conversation. Model the tax, decide whether exchanging is right at all, and line up a qualified intermediary. Note that QIs are not federally licensed or examined — there is no registration regime — so who holds your money is a real diligence question.
  2. Engage the QI before closing. The exchange agreement has to be in place before the relinquished property closes. This is where exchanges most often die.
  3. Day 0. Your sale closes. Both clocks start.
  4. Days 1–45. Review offerings, read PPMs, and identify in writing. Identify more than one; availability is first-come and an offering can close before your money arrives.
  5. Subscribe. Accreditation verification, subscription documents, and the QI wires directly to the trust. The money never touches your account.
  6. Days 46–180. Close. Any leftover proceeds are boot and are taxable.
  7. Filing. Form 8824 with that year's return.
  8. The hold. Distributions, annual tax reporting, sponsor updates.
  9. Full cycle. The sponsor sells. You decide: take cash and pay, exchange again, or take an UPREIT election if one is offered.

How to review an offering

What I look at, in order, and what I would want you to ask about anything you are shown — here or elsewhere.

  1. The debt, first. Not the projected yield. Loan-to-value, fixed or floating, and above all the maturity date against the projected hold. A loan maturing before the expected sale, inside a trust that cannot renegotiate, is the defining risk of this structure.
  2. The rent roll and what it depends on. Who pays the rent, what is their credit, when do leases expire, and what happens if the largest one does not renew.
  3. The market, specifically. Not "Sun Belt growth" — who is hiring within a commute of this asset, and what is being built nearby.
  4. The sponsor's full-cycle record. Not assets under management. Deals taken from purchase to sale, and what happened in the ones that went badly.
  5. The load. Total fees and expenses at every layer, and what portion of your dollar reaches the real estate.
  6. Reserves. What is set aside, and what happens if it runs out in a structure that cannot accept additional contributions.
  7. The exit clause. Read the springing-LLC provision. Know what triggers it and who decides.
  8. The distribution source. Whether the first year's distributions come from operations or from your own capital.

One regulatory note that belongs here. Neither the SEC nor FINRA has issued guidance addressing DSTs specifically. FINRA's guidance on 1031 exchange offerings is NASD Notice to Members 05-18 from 2005, which addressed tenant-in-common interests and mentions neither DSTs nor Rev. Rul. 2004-86. So if a page tells you an offering is "SEC-approved" or "FINRA-approved," that is not a thing that exists. What does exist is a broker's obligation to investigate before recommending, and to have a reasonable basis for the recommendation (FINRA Rule 2310; Reg BI, 17 CFR §240.15l-1).

Frequently asked questions

Is a DST guaranteed to qualify for my 1031 exchange?

No. Rev. Rul. 2004-86 holds that a taxpayer may exchange into a DST "described above" — the ruling's own narrow facts — "if the other requirements of §1031 are satisfied." Qualification depends on how the particular trust is written and operated and on you meeting every other exchange requirement, including the deadlines. It is not a property of the acronym.

What are the "seven deadly sins," really?

Industry shorthand for the limits on trustee powers in Rev. Rul. 2004-86 — no exchanging the property, no new assets, no additional contributions, no renegotiating the debt, no re-leasing outside a tenant bankruptcy, only minor non-structural modifications, and cash held short-term and distributed. The IRS does not use the phrase, does not number them, and does not present them as a checklist. They are the facts of the trust it ruled on.

Can I get my money out early?

Assume not. DST interests have no redemption right and no established secondary market. Any resale is a private transaction at a price you may not like, if you find a buyer at all. Money that might be needed should not be here.

Do I get a new depreciation schedule?

Only on the excess basis. Under Treas. Reg. §1.168(i)-6 the basis that carried over from your relinquished property keeps depreciating on that property's remaining recovery period, method and convention; only new money above the carried-over basis is treated as newly placed in service. Claims of full fresh depreciation on the whole investment are wrong.

What happens when the DST sells?

You are treated as having disposed of your share of the real estate, and the 45- and 180-day clocks start again from that disposition. You can take the cash and pay the tax, exchange into another property or DST, or — if the sponsor offers it — consider a 721 exchange into a REIT operating partnership, which is a different provision with different consequences.

Can I use a DST for part of my exchange?

Yes. Exchangers commonly place a portion into one or more DSTs to absorb an exact remaining amount and avoid boot, alongside a property they buy directly. The identification rules apply to the whole exchange, not to each piece.

What if the tenant leaves or the loan matures badly?

This is the structural risk. The trustee cannot renegotiate the debt and cannot re-lease outside a tenant bankruptcy or insolvency, so the trust's options are limited by design. Most trust agreements provide for conversion to an LLC if action outside those limits becomes necessary — which preserves the property and forfeits your ability to 1031 out.

Are DST distributions guaranteed?

No. They are a share of what the property produces after debt service and expenses. They are projections in an offering document, they can be reduced or suspended, and in some offerings early distributions include a return of your own capital. Ask which.

Who regulates DSTs?

Nobody, specifically. The interests are securities sold under Regulation D, so the securities laws and your broker-dealer's obligations apply, and the tax treatment rests on IRS guidance. But no SEC or FINRA rule addresses DSTs by name, and no regulator approves individual offerings. Treat any claim of regulatory endorsement as a red flag.

Can I lose more than my investment?

You cannot be required to contribute more — DST debt is typically nonrecourse to investors and Delaware law gives beneficial owners limited liability by default. But you can lose the investment and owe tax in the same year, because a foreclosure or forced sale is a taxable disposition measured against the low basis you carried in. That combination is the scenario to model before you invest, not after.
