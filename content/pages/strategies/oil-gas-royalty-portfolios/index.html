Revenue tied to energy production, without operating an energy business. An additional real-asset income category.

Request Investment Access

A mineral or royalty interest is a real property interest in the oil and gas underneath a piece of ground. You are not drilling anything and you are not running an energy company. You own a share of production, and when a well produces, you receive a share of the revenue — free of the cost of drilling and operating it.

That combination is why minerals appear in 1031 conversations at all. Under the right facts a mineral interest is real property, so it can serve as replacement property. But the analysis is more state-specific and more fact-dependent than any other structure on this site, and there is one recapture trap that a technically perfect exchange does not solve.

A page-specific note. Our property-type page on oil & gas royalties covers the asset itself — how minerals work, what drives revenue, what the sectors look like. This page covers the narrower question of using them as 1031 replacement property. If you are researching the asset class, start there.

What you are investing in, and the four interests that are not the same

Most confusion in this area comes from treating four different interests as one thing. The IRS's own definitions, from its Oil & Gas Audit Technique Guide (Publication 5652):

InterestWhat it isBears drilling and operating cost?
Working interestThe operating interest — "entitles its owner to share in the production, but this owner must bear its share of the development and operation costs"Yes
Royalty interest"Entitles its owner to share in the production from the mineral deposit, free of development and operating costs, and extends undiminished over the productive life of the property"No
Overriding royaltyCarved out of the working interest, "the life of which is coexistent with that of the lease" — so it dies when the lease doesNo
Net profits interestAn override payable out of the working interest's net income — so it can pay nothing in a high-cost period. The IRS groups it with royalties and production payments as a non-operating interest (PLR 202150005)Not directly, but economically exposed

Two distinctions do real work later. Cost burden: a working interest is an active business with liability and capital calls; a royalty is not. Duration: a mineral or royalty interest in perpetuity survives lease expiration; an overriding royalty does not, and duration has mattered to like-kind analysis historically.

A fifth thing that looks like a royalty and is not: a production payment. Under 26 U.S.C. §636(a) a carved-out production payment is treated "as if it were a mortgage loan on the property, and shall not qualify as an economic interest." It is debt. And the label does not control — Treas. Reg. §1.636-3(a)(2) provides that a right "economically equivalent to a production payment shall be treated as a production payment … regardless of the language used to describe such right." An interest called a royalty that terminates after a set payout is a production payment, and a production payment is not real property.

What you actually own

An interest in minerals in place, held under the law of the state where the ground sits. What that interest is called, how it is conveyed, and whether it is real property are all questions of state law — which is precisely why the federal answer bends to it.

What your responsibilities are

  • Nothing operational for a royalty interest. No drilling decisions, no capital calls, no liability for a blowout. That is the entire point of the interest type.
  • Verify the state-law classification for every property in the package, before identification. This is not boilerplate; see below.
  • Track depletion and basis, because both feed a recapture calculation later.
  • Report royalty income annually, generally on Schedule E.
  • Read the interest type in the conveyance, not the name in the marketing.

Who manages it, and what they are on the hook for

The operator — the working-interest owner — makes every decision that determines your revenue: whether to drill, when, how many wells, whether to shut in when prices fall, and whether to hold acreage by production before a lease expires. You have no vote.

A publicly traded royalty partnership states the position in its own risk factors better than any brochure: "We do not control operations and development of the Royalty Properties," and "The decision to explore or develop these properties … will be made by the operator and other working interest owners" (Dorchester Minerals, Form 10-K FY2025). Its other two headline risks are worth quoting for the same reason: "Our cash distributions are highly dependent on oil and natural gas prices, which have historically been very volatile," and "Our oil and natural gas reserves and the underlying properties are depleting assets."

Does this qualify for a 1031 exchange?

Often yes — and the answer runs through state law, which is unusual.

Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(1) defines real property to include "unsevered natural products of land," and (a)(3) is explicit that this covers "mines; wells; and other natural deposits" — with a limit that decides a lot of cases: "Natural products and deposits, such as crops, timber, water, ores, and minerals, cease to be real property when they are severed, extracted, or removed from the land." Minerals in the ground are real property. Produced oil, and a revenue stream from already-produced hydrocarbons, are not.

Then (a)(6) sends the question to the states: property is real property "if, on the date it is transferred in an exchange, the property is real property under the law of the State or local jurisdiction in which that property is located." The IRS applies the same principle in its own rulings — "whether property constitutes real or personal property generally is determined under state or local law" (PLR 200805012).

The supporting authority on oil and gas specifically is older and thinner than practitioners generally admit. The most-cited ruling is Rev. Rul. 68-331, holding that a leasehold interest in a producing oil lease and a fee interest in improved ranch property are both "continuing interests in real property" and therefore like-kind. That ruling is from 1968 and its text is not published on any government site; the reliable government source is the IRS's own description of it in PLR 200805012, which is what we cite. Several other rulings quoted in industry material — notably one said to hold that royalty interests are real property — we could not locate in any government source at all, and we do not rely on them.

Two consequences follow, and they should shape how you approach a mineral exchange.

  • The classification genuinely varies. Texas is explicit: "real property" includes "a mineral in place" and interests in it (Tex. Tax Code §1.04(2)). Oklahoma treats oil and gas interests as interests in land, though on a different theory. And Kansas declares by statute that "all oil and gas leases and all oil and gas wells … are hereby declared to be personal property" for valuation and taxation (K.S.A. 79-329). That statute is limited to valuation and taxation and does not by its terms decide the §1031 question — but it shows exactly why the analysis has to be run property by property, state by state, rather than assumed.
  • A package spanning several states is several analyses, not one. Get them in writing before the 45th day.

The recapture trap

This is the most important paragraph on the page and the least marketed fact in the category.

Mineral property carries §1254 recapture: intangible drilling costs and depletion previously deducted come back as ordinary income on disposition. Unlike depreciation recapture in an ordinary real estate exchange, §1254 recapture is not fully deferred by a §1031 exchange. Treas. Reg. §1.1254-2(d)(1) limits recapture to the gain recognized plus "the fair market value of property acquired that is not natural resource recapture property."

Read that in the two directions people actually go:

  • Minerals into minerals: recapture is generally deferred, because the replacement property is itself natural resource recapture property.
  • Minerals into an apartment building, a DST, or net-lease retail: the fair market value of that non-mineral replacement property is counted, and §1254 ordinary-income recapture is triggered up to that amount. You can complete a perfectly valid 1031 exchange and still owe ordinary income tax.

Anyone exchanging out of long-held minerals into conventional real estate should have that number computed before identifying anything.

Depletion — the real benefit, and its real limits

Percentage depletion is the genuinely unusual tax feature of royalty ownership. It is computed on gross income from the property rather than on basis, so unlike cost depletion it is not capped by what you paid. Congress removed it for most oil and gas taxpayers in §613A(a), then preserved it for independent producers and royalty owners in §613A(c) at a 15% rate.

The limits belong in the same breath as the benefit:

  • 15%, not a larger number (§613A(c)(1));
  • capped at 100% of taxable income from the property (§613(a));
  • capped at 65% of your overall taxable income (§613A(d)(1));
  • limited to 1,000 barrels a day of depletable quantity (§613A(c)(3)(B));
  • unavailable to retailers and to refiners running over 75,000 barrels a day (§613A(d)(2), (d)(4));
  • and it reduces your basis, which feeds the §1254 recapture above.

One currency point. Material still circulating describes a suspension of the taxable-income limit for marginal production. That provision, §613A(c)(6)(H), was struck in 2018. Any analysis relying on it is eight years stale.

Who invests in this

  • Someone who already owns minerals — often inherited — and is exchanging into more of them.
  • Someone who wants real-asset income uncorrelated with the property cycle, and understands they are taking commodity risk instead.
  • Someone who values the absence of operating obligations: no tenants, no roofs, no capital calls.
  • An IRA, in the specific case of royalty interests, because §512(b)(2) excludes "all royalties (including overriding royalties)" from unrelated business taxable income — while a working interest is expressly not excluded, and any leverage pulls income back in under §512(b)(4).

Who does not

  • Anyone who wants stable, predictable income. Revenue is price times volume, and both move. See the numbers below.
  • Anyone exchanging into non-mineral real estate without modeling §1254 first.
  • Anyone who has not confirmed the state-law classification of every property in the package.
  • Anyone who wants a hedge against real estate. Minerals are their own asset class with their own cycle, not a defensive version of property.
  • Anyone attracted by a pitch that leads with recent energy prices. The SEC lists exactly that as a red flag.

What to expect as an investor

Monthly revenue checks that vary — sometimes a lot — with price and production. A depletion deduction that shelters part of it. A slow decline in volume from any given well as the reservoir depletes, offset only if the operator drills more. And no ability to influence any of it.

Here is what volatility actually looks like, from the EIA's own series. Annual average WTI spot: $93.17 in 2014, $43.29 in 2016 — down 54% in two years. Then $39.16 in 2020 to $94.90 in 2022 — up 142% in two years. More recently, monthly averages went from $57.97 in December 2025 to $102.13 in May 2026, then back to $83.90 by August 2026. Natural gas is more volatile still on a percentage basis: Henry Hub annual averages of $2.03 in 2020, $6.45 in 2022, $2.19 in 2024.

And a caution against reading national headlines as personal income: US crude production hit a record 13.66 million barrels a day in 2025 while WTI averaged $65.39, below the prior three years. National production growth is not revenue growth for the acreage you own.

The risks

  • Commodity price risk, quantified above. This is the dominant one.
  • Depletion, in the physical sense. Reserves are a wasting asset; every well declines. Dorchester's filing: properties "experience declines in production due to depletion of their oil and natural gas reservoirs."
  • Operator control. Someone else decides whether your acreage gets drilled, and leases must be drilled before expiration — "generally within three years" — to be held by production.
  • Title and classification risk, which is the §1031 risk specifically.
  • §1254 recapture on a non-mineral exchange.
  • Illiquidity and valuation in a private program.
  • Fraud risk. The SEC has an investor alert devoted to private oil and gas offerings, noting that "the number of fraud cases related to private securities offerings for oil and gas ventures has increased" and listing red flags including "'can't miss' wells and 'guaranteed' returns," "abnormally high rates of return," and pitches "referring to recent news events like high oil or gas prices."

The worst case

Prices fall, the operator shuts in marginal wells, leases expire undrilled, and your monthly check approaches zero while the interest itself becomes hard to value and harder to sell. Because the underlying reserves deplete, an interest can decline toward worthlessness without anything dramatic happening — no default, no foreclosure, just less oil.

The tax version: you exchange out of minerals into an apartment DST, complete a valid §1031 exchange, and receive an ordinary-income §1254 recapture bill for the intangible drilling costs and depletion deducted over decades — against replacement property that generates no cash to pay it.

The best case

An operator drills your acreage into a strong price environment, revenue rises without any capital contribution from you, percentage depletion shelters a meaningful share of it, and the interest — being real property in its state — remains available for another exchange or for a step-up at death. Held in an IRA, royalty income is excluded from UBTI outright. That is a genuinely good outcome and it does happen.

The investment process

  1. Establish the interest type in the conveyance documents: mineral, royalty, overriding royalty, net profits, or something that is economically a production payment.
  2. Get the state-law classification in writing for every property, from counsel, before the 45th day.
  3. Model §1254 on the relinquished property if you are exchanging out of minerals into anything else.
  4. Diligence the operator and the acreage — who is drilling, decline curves, and lease expirations.
  5. Identify and close within the ordinary 45- and 180-day windows.
  6. Set up reporting for royalty income and depletion.

How to review one of these

  1. Interest type first, and the actual document. A "royalty" that terminates on a payout is a production payment, and §636 makes it debt.
  2. State-law classification, per property, with an opinion behind it — not a sentence in a brochure.
  3. The §1254 exposure on both sides of the trade.
  4. Who operates, and their drilling record on this acreage.
  5. Decline curves and reserve reports, and who prepared them. FINRA has told firms that for "energy development and exploration programs," obtaining "expert opinions from engineers, geologists and others" is necessary before recommending (Regulatory Notice 10-22). If your broker cannot say what engineering review they did, that is your answer.
  6. Lease expiration dates against the drilling schedule.
  7. Price assumptions in any projection, tested against the range above rather than the last twelve months.
  8. Fees and the sponsor's own economics, including any retained interest.

Frequently asked questions

Do oil and gas royalties qualify for a 1031 exchange?

Often, but it depends on the interest and the state. Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3 treats unsevered minerals as real property and then defers to the law of the state where the property sits. Minerals in place are real property; produced oil is not. The classification must be confirmed for each property rather than assumed for the package.

What is the difference between a royalty and a working interest?

Cost burden and liability. A working interest owner "must bear its share of the development and operation costs" and is in an active business; a royalty owner shares in production "free of development and operating costs." That difference also drives the UBTI answer for retirement accounts and the risk profile generally.

Can I exchange minerals for an apartment building or a DST?

Yes, if both are real property — but read the §1254 section above first. Treas. Reg. §1.1254-2(d) triggers ordinary-income recapture of intangible drilling costs and depletion up to the fair market value of replacement property that is not itself natural resource recapture property. A valid exchange into conventional real estate can still generate a tax bill.

Is percentage depletion really 15% of my income, tax-free?

Not quite. It is 15% of gross income from the property, subject to a 100%-of-taxable-income-from-the-property limit, a 65%-of-overall-taxable-income limit, a 1,000 barrel-per-day quantity limit, and exclusions for retailers and larger refiners. It also reduces basis and feeds §1254 recapture later. It is a real benefit; it is not a blanket 15% shelter.

What is a production payment and why does it matter?

A right to a share of production that terminates once a specified sum is realized. §636(a) treats it as a mortgage loan rather than an economic interest, and the regulation treats anything economically equivalent as one "regardless of the language used to describe such right." Being debt, it is not real property, so it does not work as replacement property.

How volatile is the income really?

Very. Annual average WTI fell from $93.17 in 2014 to $43.29 in 2016, and rose from $39.16 in 2020 to $94.90 in 2022. Monthly averages ran from $57.97 in December 2025 to $102.13 in May 2026 and back to $83.90 in August 2026. Natural gas swings more. Budget on a range, not a number.

Can I hold these in an IRA?

Royalty income, including overriding royalties, is excluded from unrelated business taxable income under §512(b)(2). A working interest where the holder is not relieved of development costs is expressly not excluded, and debt-financed property pulls income back in under §512(b)(4). So the interest type and the leverage both matter — confirm with your custodian and CPA for the specific program.

Does an overriding royalty work the same as a mineral interest?

Not in duration. An overriding royalty is carved out of the working interest and its life is "coexistent with that of the lease" — when the lease ends, it ends. A mineral or royalty interest can extend over the productive life of the property. Duration has mattered in like-kind analysis, so the distinction is worth confirming in the documents.

Has FINRA warned investors about oil and gas programs?

The investor-facing warning is the SEC's, not FINRA's — an Investor Alert on private oil and gas offerings that lists specific red flags and reminds investors "you could lose your entire investment." FINRA's oil-and-gas guidance is directed at firms: Regulatory Notice 10-22 says that for energy programs, expert engineering and geological opinions are necessary before a recommendation.

Why does the state matter so much here?

Because the federal regulation makes it matter. Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(6) asks whether the property is real property under the law of the state where it is located, on the date it is transferred. Texas expressly lists "a mineral in place" as real property; Kansas declares oil and gas leases and wells personal property for valuation and taxation. Same asset class, different starting point.
