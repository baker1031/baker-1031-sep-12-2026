A DST with a potential later path into a REIT’s operating partnership, often considered for estate planning and long-term flexibility.

Request Investment Access

A 721 exchange is what can happen at the end of a DST, not instead of one. The REIT that sponsors the trust contributes the property into its operating partnership, and you receive operating partnership units instead of cash. No tax that day. What you now own is a share of a diversified REIT portfolio rather than a slice of one building.

It is a real provision, it does real work, and it is described inaccurately more often than anything else on this site. So this page spends most of its length on what the authority actually says, because the gap between the marketing and the Code is where investors get hurt.

What you are investing in

An UPREIT — umbrella partnership REIT — is a three-layer structure. A REIT sits on top and owns almost nothing directly. Beneath it is an operating partnership that holds the properties and conducts the business. The REIT is the general partner and owns most of the units.

That is not a description from a brochure; it is how these companies describe themselves in their own filings. Equity Residential: "All of the Company's property ownership, development and related business operations are conducted through the Operating Partnership and EQR has no material assets or liabilities other than its investment in ERPOP," with EQR owning "an approximate 97.0% ownership interest" (Form 10-K, FY2024). ExchangeRight Income Fund, which runs a DST-to-UPREIT program: "We are structured as an umbrella partnership REIT, commonly called an UPREIT, and own all of our assets and conduct substantially all of our business through the Operating Partnership" (Form 10-K, FY2025).

When a DST reaches the end of its life, the operating partnership acquires the property — sometimes by merging the DST into the OP, sometimes by exercising an option over the investors' interests — and issues units in exchange.

What you actually own

A limited partnership interest in the operating partnership. Not REIT shares. Not real estate.

That distinction drives everything downstream. Units usually carry a right, after a lock-up, to be redeemed for cash or exchanged for REIT shares — but the terms are not standard and you have to read them. Prologis: units "generally became redeemable, on a one-for-one basis, for our common stock one year after their issuance" (424B3, Oct. 2025). ExchangeRight, by contrast: holders may have units "redeemed by the Operating Partnership for cash, unless we, in our sole discretion, elect to purchase such Class I Common Units in exchange for Class I Common Shares" — and its shares have no public market, with a repurchase plan capped at "up to 5%" of shares per year.

One is a one-for-one right to listed stock after a year. The other is a cash redemption at the REIT's option, into an unlisted vehicle with a 5% annual repurchase cap. Both are "OP units."

What your responsibilities are

  • Decide, or have the decision made for you. In the option structure, doing nothing is a choice. One DST trust agreement filed with the SEC provides that investors who do not submit a cash election "will be deemed to have agreed to exchange his, her or its Interest(s) for OP Units" (CF Summerfield Multifamily DST trust agreement). Read the election notice when it arrives.
  • Understand you are choosing a permanent path. See the 1031 section below. This is the responsibility that matters most and the one most often skipped.
  • File a partnership K-1 every year rather than reporting your own share of property items.
  • Track your outside basis and your share of OP debt. Both determine what you owe when you eventually convert or sell, and neither is intuitive.

Who manages it, and what they are on the hook for

The REIT, as general partner of the operating partnership, manages everything: acquisitions, dispositions, leverage, distributions, and whether to make the tax elections that determine what your heirs inherit. As a limited partner you have no operational role.

Two management decisions belong to the sponsor and land squarely on you:

  • Whether to sell the property you contributed. If the OP sells it, §704(c)(1)(A) allocates the built-in gain back to you — the contributing partner — even though the OP receives the money. You can owe tax on the sale of a property you no longer own, in a year you receive no corresponding cash.
  • Whether to have a §754 election in effect. This decides whether your heirs get stepped-up depreciation inside the partnership. Without it, §743(a) says partnership property basis "shall not be adjusted … on the death of a partner." The election belongs to the partnership, not to you, and it is revocable (§754).

In the option structure the sponsor also controls the timing of the transaction itself. The trust agreement above gives the operating partnership "the right … but not the obligation" to require the exchange. Whether and when your deferral ends is, to a meaningful degree, someone else's decision.

Does this qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No — and the phrase "721 exchange" is not a term the Code uses. This is the most important section on the page.

Here is what §721 actually says, in full: "No gain or loss shall be recognized to a partnership or to any of its partners in the case of a contribution of property to the partnership in exchange for an interest in the partnership" (26 U.S.C. §721(a)). Notice what is absent: no like-kind requirement, no 45-day identification, no 180-day clock, no qualified intermediary. It is a different provision doing a different job. Your basis carries into the units under §722.

§1031, by contrast, requires "real property … exchanged solely for real property of like kind." An OP unit is a partnership interest. And a partnership interest is on the excluded list in Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5), which says "interests in a partnership" are not real property "regardless of the classification of such property under State or local law."

Which produces the consequence to understand before you elect anything:

A 721 exchange is a one-way door. Once you hold OP units, §1031 is unavailable to you for those units, permanently. The remaining exits are a taxable sale, a taxable conversion to REIT shares, a gift, a charitable transfer, or death. You have not extended your 1031 chain — you have ended it in exchange for a different kind of deferral.

Three more things the authority does not say, which are said constantly:

  • The IRS has not blessed the DST-to-721 sequence. Rev. Rul. 2004-86 addresses DST classification and §1031 and says nothing about §721, UPREITs, or operating partnerships. There is no ruling, revenue procedure, regulation or published case addressing the sequence. The closest authority is Magneson v. Commissioner, 753 F.2d 1490 (9th Cir. 1985), where a 1031 exchange followed the same day by a partnership contribution survived — but the taxpayers received a general partnership interest with "possession and control rights comparable to individual fee ownership," which is not what a passive OP unit is, and the case binds only the Ninth Circuit. The Service still has step-transaction doctrine available (Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331 (1945): "the transaction must be viewed as a whole").
  • There is no required holding period. §721 contains no seasoning rule of any kind. Two years, one year, twelve months — whatever number you are told, it is a practitioner's risk-mitigation convention, not a legal requirement, and it should be presented as one.
  • §721(b) is a live exception. Nonrecognition does not apply where the partnership would be an investment company if incorporated, and §351(e)(1)(iv) expressly counts "any interest in a real estate investment trust" as a security for that test. The usual conclusion is that an OP holding real property directly falls outside the exception — but no ruling or case holds that. It is a tax-counsel opinion, and the offering documents should say who gave it.

Who invests in this

  • Someone already in a DST that is going full cycle, who wants to stay deferred without running another 45-day clock.
  • Someone who has decided this is the last stop — the plan is to hold for life and let heirs take the step-up.
  • Someone who wants one building's worth of exposure replaced by a portfolio, and accepts REIT-level returns in exchange.
  • Someone tired of the exchange treadmill, for whom "no more identification periods" is worth real money.

Who does not

  • Anyone who intends to 1031 again. This closes that door. If exchanging is part of the long-term plan, take the cash-out-and-exchange election instead.
  • Anyone who will need liquidity in a specific year. Reaching cash means a taxable conversion first, then whatever redemption program the REIT runs — and in a non-traded REIT that program is capped and suspendable.
  • Anyone with a very low basis and heavy debt on the contributed property. The debt shift can trigger tax at the moment of contribution. See the worst case.
  • Anyone who has not read the specific partnership agreement. Redemption terms differ so much between sponsors that "OP units" tells you almost nothing on its own.

What to expect as an investor

Distributions from the operating partnership, generally tracking the REIT's own distribution. A K-1 instead of your prior reporting. No vote and no operational say. A lock-up before any redemption right is available. And after that, a decision you should treat as a tax event rather than a liquidity event, because that is what it is.

Expect the value of your units to track the REIT rather than your old building. That is the trade: you have swapped specific risk for portfolio risk, and specific upside for portfolio upside.

The risks

  • Conversion is taxable, and the tax can exceed the value received. Not a footnote — the sponsors say it themselves. See the next section.
  • §704(c) built-in gain. If the OP sells your contributed property, the gain is allocated back to you. Tax without cash.
  • §737's seven-year rule. A distribution of other property to you within seven years can trigger recognition of net precontribution gain (§737(a)).
  • Liquidity, twice removed. An OP unit sits behind a REIT share, which in a non-traded REIT is itself capped and suspendable. The SEC's own bulletin on non-traded REITs warns that liquidity events "might not occur until more than 10 years after your investment" and that redemption programs "are typically subject to significant limitations and may be discontinued at the discretion of the REIT without notice."
  • Sponsor discretion over timing. In the option structure, the OP chooses when.
  • Valuation. In a non-traded REIT there is no market price; unit value derives from a periodically calculated NAV.
  • Step-transaction risk on the sequence itself, which no authority has resolved either way.

The worst case

Two versions, and both are documented in issuers' own filings rather than inferred.

At the front end: contributing mortgaged property to the OP shifts debt to the other partners. Under §752(b) a decrease in your share of partnership liabilities "shall be considered as a distribution of money," and under §731(a)(1) money distributed in excess of your basis is gain. A contribution marketed as tax-free can produce a tax bill on day one if your basis is low and the debt shift is large.

At the back end: converting units to REIT shares is a sale under §741, and the amount realized includes your share of the partnership's liabilities. Prologis states the consequence plainly in its own SEC prospectus: the exchange "will be treated for U.S. federal tax purposes as a sale of the common limited partnership units," and — the sentence to read twice — "it is possible that the amount of gain recognized or even the tax liability resulting from the gain could exceed the value of the shares of common stock received upon the exchange."

That is a sponsor telling you, in a filed document, that converting can cost more than it delivers. It deserves more prominence than it usually gets.

The best case

The contribution is tax-free, you hold the units for life, and your heirs take a basis stepped to fair market value at your death under §1014(a), reached through §742. The gain that has been rolling since your first exchange is never paid by anyone. Meanwhile you have collected distributions from a diversified portfolio and never thought about a tenant again.

Two honest qualifications on that picture. The step-up applies to the units — your outside basis. Whether your heirs also get stepped-up depreciation inside the partnership depends on the OP having a §754 election in effect, which is the sponsor's decision. And whether death clears the §704(c) built-in gain layer is genuinely unresolved: the regulation says a transferee succeeds to the transferor's built-in gain (Treas. Reg. §1.704-3(a)(7)) and no authority carves out death. Anyone whose estate plan depends on that should get an opinion, not a brochure.

The investment process

  1. Notice. The sponsor announces a full-cycle event and sets a deadline for elections.
  2. The election. Typically three options: cash (taxable), another 1031 exchange (deferred, clocks restart), or OP units (deferred, chain ends). Check whether OP units are the default if you do nothing.
  3. Valuation. Your interest is valued and converted to units at that value. Ask how the value was determined and by whom.
  4. Contribution. The property goes into the OP; you receive units. Your CPA should compute the debt-shift consequence before this step, not at filing time.
  5. Lock-up. Typically a year, sometimes longer.
  6. Hold. Distributions and a K-1.
  7. Exit, if ever. Redeem for cash or exchange for shares — both taxable — or hold to death.

How to review one of these

  1. The partnership agreement's redemption terms. Cash or shares? Whose election? What lock-up? These vary enormously and they are the whole liquidity story.
  2. Is the REIT listed or not? If not, read the share repurchase plan — the caps, the discount for early redemption, and the board's suspension rights.
  3. The debt-shift computation, before you elect. Your share of OP liabilities against your basis. This is the number that decides whether the contribution is actually tax-free for you.
  4. Whether a §754 election is in effect, if the estate plan matters.
  5. Who gave the §721 opinion, and what it assumes — particularly on §721(b) and on the sequence from the DST.
  6. The REIT itself. You are buying its whole portfolio, leverage and management, not the building you started with.
  7. Any claim of IRS or regulatory approval. There is none to have. A page that says otherwise is telling you something about the seller.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 721 exchange a type of 1031 exchange?

No. They are separate provisions with different requirements. §1031 requires real property exchanged for like-kind real property within 45 and 180 days. §721 provides nonrecognition on a contribution of property to a partnership, with no like-kind test and no deadlines. An OP unit is a partnership interest, which Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5) expressly excludes from real property.

Can I do another 1031 exchange after a 721 exchange?

Not with the OP units. A partnership interest is not real property for §1031 purposes, so the exchange chain ends there. This is the single most important consequence of the election, and it is permanent.

Is converting OP units into REIT shares tax-free?

No. It is treated as a sale of the units under §741. The amount realized includes your share of the partnership's liabilities, which is why sponsors disclose that the tax can exceed the value of the shares received.

Has the IRS approved the DST-to-721 structure?

There is no ruling, revenue procedure, regulation or published case addressing the sequence. Rev. Rul. 2004-86 covers DST classification and §1031 and does not mention §721. The nearest case, Magneson, is a 1985 Ninth Circuit decision involving a general partnership interest in a direct fee exchange — favorable, but distinguishable and not binding elsewhere.

How long do I have to hold the DST before a 721 exchange?

§721 imposes no holding period. Any stated seasoning is a practitioner convention intended to reduce step-transaction risk, not a legal requirement. Ask whoever quotes you a number what authority it comes from.

Can the sponsor force me into OP units?

Depending on the documents, close to it. Some DST trust agreements give the operating partnership an option over investors' interests and provide that an investor who does not affirmatively elect cash is deemed to have agreed to take units. Read the election notice and the trust agreement, and calendar the deadline.

What happens if the REIT sells the property I contributed?

Under §704(c)(1)(A) the built-in gain from your contribution is allocated back to you, even though the operating partnership receives the sale proceeds. You can owe tax in a year you receive no cash from that sale.

Do my heirs get a step-up on OP units?

Your heirs get a step-up in the units themselves — outside basis — under §1014(a). Whether the partnership also adjusts the basis of its property depends on a §754 election being in effect, which is the partnership's decision, not yours. And whether the §704(c) built-in gain layer survives death is unresolved.

Are OP units liquid?

No, and they are a step further from liquidity than the REIT's own shares. If the REIT is non-traded, reaching cash means converting units to shares — taxable — and then queuing in a capped, suspendable repurchase program.

Is there SEC or FINRA guidance on 721 exchanges?

No. Neither regulator has published a rule, notice, alert or bulletin addressing 721 exchanges, UPREIT transactions, or OP units. "721 exchange" does not appear in the Code or the regulations either — it is industry shorthand. Claims of regulatory endorsement are not just unsupported; under FINRA Rule 2210(d)(1)(B) they are the kind of statement a firm is not permitted to make.
