If you have just sold a property, the honest headline is this: you cannot 1031 into REIT shares. Not into a listed REIT, not into a non-traded one. Shares are excluded from the definition of real property, full stop.

REITs still belong on this page for two reasons. They are where a lot of exchange money ends up eventually — through a 721 exchange at the end of a DST, which is a different provision. And they are the right home for the portion of a sale that is not being exchanged: the boot, the cash you decided to pay tax on, the proceeds you never intended to roll.

What you are investing in

A REIT is a tax election, not an asset class. A company qualifies by meeting a list of statutory tests, and in exchange it deducts the dividends it pays — so income is generally taxed once, at the shareholder, rather than twice.

The tests are in 26 U.S.C. §856 and they are more demanding than most investors realize:

  • Beneficial ownership "held by 100 or more persons" — a test measured over at least 335 days of the year (§856(a)(5), (b)).
  • Not closely held: broadly, five or fewer individuals cannot own more than 50% (§856(a)(6), (h)).
  • 75% of gross income from real property sources — rents, mortgage interest, gains from real property (§856(c)(3)) — and 95% from those sources plus other passive income (§856(c)(2)).
  • 75% of assets in real estate assets, cash and government securities, tested each quarter, with issuer-level concentration limits (§856(c)(4)).
  • And the one everybody quotes: 90% of REIT taxable income distributed each year (§857(a)).

Worth being precise about that last one, because the shorthand is loose. It is 90% of REIT taxable income, computed without the dividends-paid deduction and excluding net capital gain — not 90% of cash flow and not 90% of profit. It is also a condition of qualification rather than a standalone mandate. Because the deduction is what removes the entity-level tax, most REITs distribute closer to 100%.

One current detail: the limit on assets held in taxable REIT subsidiaries moved from 20% to 25% for tax years beginning on or after January 1, 2026 (Pub. L. 119-21, §70439). If you read an older explainer saying 20%, it was right when it was written.

What you actually own

Shares of a corporation. Not real estate, not an interest in real estate, and not anything the tax code treats as real property. That is the whole reason a REIT cannot receive exchange proceeds.

You own a claim on a professionally managed portfolio, its leverage, and its management contract. You get a vote, a dividend, and the right to sell — readily if listed, on the REIT's terms if not.

The differences that matter to you are liquidity, pricing and fees, and they are large. Conflating them is the most common error in this category.

What your responsibilities are

For a listed REIT: open an account, buy, hold, report the 1099-DIV. That is genuinely the whole list.

For a non-traded or private REIT there is more: meeting suitability or accreditation standards, reading a prospectus or PPM, understanding the repurchase plan before you need it, and accepting that you are underwriting a manager you cannot replace.

Who manages it, and what they are on the hook for

Listed REITs are usually internally managed — the employees work for the company, and shareholders elect the board. Non-traded REITs are usually externally managed, and the SEC is direct about the consequence: the external manager "may be paid significant transaction fees by the REIT for services that may not necessarily align with the interests of shareholders, such as fees based on the amount of property acquisitions and assets under management," and may also manage vehicles that compete with the one you are in (SEC Investor Bulletin: Non-traded REITs).

In a non-traded REIT the board also controls the one thing you will care about most: whether your redemption request is honored. More on that below, because it is not hypothetical.

Does this qualify for a 1031 exchange?

No. §1031(a)(1) requires real property exchanged solely for like-kind real property, and Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5) puts "stock … bonds, or notes" and "other securities" on the excluded list "regardless of the classification of such property under State or local law." A REIT share is stock. There is no version of this that works, and no structure that makes a share into real property.

Two adjacent paths exist and should not be confused with the above:

  • A DST is the vehicle that does work as replacement property, because its beneficial owners are treated as owning the underlying real estate rather than a security. That is a grantor-trust result, not a REIT result.
  • A 721 exchange lets property be contributed to a REIT's operating partnership for units, under §721. That is a separate provision, it produces partnership units rather than shares, and it ends your ability to use §1031 on that investment.

So the accurate sentence is: you cannot exchange into a REIT, but exchange money can end up inside one, later, by a different route and at a cost.

How REIT income is taxed

A REIT distribution arrives in up to three characters, and the mix shows up on your Form 1099-DIV:

  • Ordinary dividends — the bulk. Generally not qualified dividend income (§857(c)(2)), so not taxed at long-term capital gain rates.
  • Capital gain dividends — the REIT's designated share of its own long-term gains.
  • Return of capital — not taxed now; it reduces your basis and increases the gain when you sell.

The offset worth knowing: qualified REIT dividends are eligible for the 20% deduction under §199A(b)(1)(B) and (e)(3) — a dividend that is neither a capital gain dividend nor qualified dividend income. That deduction was scheduled to expire after 2025 and was made permanent by Pub. L. 119-21 in July 2025. Note that the IRS's own newsroom page on the deduction still says it ends in 2025; the Code says otherwise, and the Code governs.

Who invests in this

  • Investors placing the taxable portion of a sale — boot, or proceeds never intended for exchange.
  • DST investors reaching a full-cycle event who elect OP units, and end up REIT-adjacent by that route.
  • Anyone who wants real estate exposure with daily liquidity, in which case a listed REIT is the straightforward answer.
  • Investors who want income and diversification and are willing to accept share-price volatility to get it.

Who does not

  • Anyone trying to complete a 1031 exchange. The proceeds cannot go here. If someone offers you a "1031-eligible REIT," that is not a product; ask what they actually mean.
  • Anyone who cannot tolerate seeing a price fall. Listed REITs are marked to market daily, which is honest and uncomfortable in a way that a private appraisal is not.
  • Anyone buying a non-traded REIT for its stated liquidity. Read the next section before deciding.
  • Anyone who assumes an unlisted price is a stable price. It is an appraisal-based estimate, and FINRA requires account statements to say so.

What to expect as an investor

Regular distributions, generally quarterly, at a level the REIT sets and can change. A 1099-DIV rather than a K-1 — simpler than most real estate ownership. Daily pricing if listed; monthly or quarterly NAV if not.

On a non-traded REIT statement you will see a per-share estimated value governed by FINRA Rule 2231(c), which requires the value to be "developed in a manner reasonably designed to ensure that the per share estimated value is reliable," based on at least annual third-party-assisted valuations, and accompanied by mandated disclosure that the securities "are not listed on a national securities exchange, are generally illiquid and that, even if a customer is able to sell the securities, the price received may be less than the per share estimated value provided in the account statement." Where distributions include a return of capital, the statement must carry a legend saying so.

The risks

  • Market risk. Listed REITs trade with the market and are rate-sensitive; a good portfolio can have a bad year on the screen.
  • Liquidity risk in unlisted vehicles. Repurchase plans are capped and can be reduced or suspended by the board. See below.
  • Fees. The SEC's bulletin describes legacy non-traded REIT front-end fees at "up to 15 percent of the offering price." That bulletin dates from 2015 and describes the old fixed-price model — modern perpetual-NAV REITs are structured differently — but fees remain the first thing to quantify.
  • Distributions from capital. The SEC warns that non-traded REITs "may use offering proceeds, which includes the money you invested, and borrowings to pay distributions," which "reduces the value of the shares."
  • Valuation opacity. No market price means an estimate, "based on periodic or annual appraisals … and therefore may not be accurate or timely."
  • Conflicts of interest in external management.
  • Complexity. FINRA lists non-traded REITs among complex products warranting "heightened supervision" (Regulatory Notice 22-08).

The worst case

For a listed REIT: the shares fall a long way and you sell at a loss. Painful, ordinary, and visible in real time.

For a non-traded REIT the worse case is subtler, and it happened recently enough that it should not be discussed hypothetically. Redemption caps are not a formality; boards use them.

Blackstone Real Estate Income Trust's plan caps repurchases at "no more than 2% of our aggregate NAV per month" and "no more than 5% of our aggregate NAV per calendar quarter." Its FY2023 10-K discloses that it "began a period of prorated fulfillment of repurchase requests in November 2022," and that in each of the first three quarters of 2023 and in October, November and December 2023 it "repurchased less than the full amount of shares requested." Starwood Real Estate Income Trust went further: on May 23, 2024 its board cut the monthly cap from 2% of NAV to 0.33% and the quarterly cap from 5% to 1% — an 80%-plus reduction in available liquidity, by board action, disclosed in an 8-K. As of its FY2025 10-K the caps had been raised only to 0.5% monthly and 1.5% quarterly, still well below the original terms.

Both are filed documents, not commentary. The lesson is not that these are bad vehicles — BREIT reports fulfilling every repurchase request received during 2025 — but that a redemption cap is a real constraint that binds exactly when investors most want out. Shares held under a year are also typically repurchased at a discount: 98% of transaction price at BREIT, 95% at SREIT.

The best case

A well-run REIT compounds: rents rise, the portfolio grows, distributions rise with it, and you own professionally managed real estate across dozens of markets without a single phone call about a broken boiler. If it is listed you can sell any part of it on any day, which no other structure on this site offers. And the ordinary dividends carry the 20% §199A deduction, now permanent.

The investment process

  1. Decide which kind. Listed, non-traded, or private — the liquidity difference is the decision.
  2. Listed: buy through any brokerage account. No suitability paperwork beyond the account itself.
  3. Non-traded: subscription documents through a broker-dealer, suitability review, minimum investment, and a prospectus to read — particularly the repurchase plan and the fee table.
  4. Private: accreditation verification and a PPM.
  5. Hold. Distributions, a 1099-DIV, and for unlisted vehicles a per-share estimated value on your statement.
  6. Exit. Sell on the exchange, or submit a repurchase request and accept the cap, the queue and any early-redemption discount.

How to review one

  1. The repurchase plan, first, if unlisted. Monthly and quarterly caps, early-redemption discount, board suspension rights, and the actual fulfillment history in the last two 10-Ks. That history is public and it is the most informative thing in the document.
  2. Where the distribution comes from. Operations, or offering proceeds and borrowings? The filings say.
  3. The full fee stack. Selling commissions, dealer-manager fees, management and performance fees, acquisition and disposition fees.
  4. Leverage, and the maturity schedule against the portfolio's cash flow.
  5. Who calculates NAV, how often, and with what independent input.
  6. The portfolio itself — property types, markets, tenant concentration, occupancy trend.
  7. For an externally managed REIT, the conflicts section. Read it in full; it is where the incentives are described.

Frequently asked questions

Can I 1031 exchange into a REIT?

No. §1031 requires real property, and Treas. Reg. §1.1031(a)-3(a)(5) excludes stock and other securities from that definition regardless of state law. A REIT share is stock. Any offer of a "1031-eligible REIT" is either describing something else — usually a DST or a 721 exchange — or is wrong.

Then how do people end up in REITs after an exchange?

Usually through a DST that goes full cycle into a 721 exchange: the property is contributed to the REIT's operating partnership and the investor receives OP units. That is §721, not §1031, it produces partnership units rather than shares, and it permanently ends the ability to do another 1031 exchange with that investment.

What does the 90% distribution rule actually require?

That the REIT's deduction for dividends paid equals or exceeds 90% of its REIT taxable income, computed without that deduction and excluding net capital gain. It is a condition of qualifying as a REIT, not a promise about your yield, and it is not 90% of cash flow.

Are REIT dividends qualified dividends?

Generally not. Under §857(c)(2) only a designated portion can be qualified dividend income, so most REIT ordinary dividends are taxed at ordinary rates. The offsetting benefit is the 20% deduction for qualified REIT dividends under §199A(e)(3), which was made permanent in 2025.

Is a non-traded REIT safer because the price does not move?

No. The price does not move because it is not traded, not because the underlying real estate is more stable. An appraisal-based NAV updates slowly and, in the SEC's words, "may not be accurate or timely." Absence of visible volatility is not absence of risk.

Can I get my money out of a non-traded REIT?

Only through the repurchase plan, subject to its caps, and only if the board keeps honoring it. Recent filed examples: BREIT prorated repurchases from November 2022 through 2023, and Starwood's board cut its monthly cap from 2% of NAV to 0.33% in May 2024. Assume the cap binds when you most want out, because that is when everyone else does too.

What are the fees?

Listed REITs: whatever your brokerage charges, plus the REIT's own operating expenses. Non-traded: historically much higher — the SEC's 2015 bulletin cites front-end fees "up to 15 percent of the offering price," though modern perpetual-NAV structures differ. Get the fee table from the prospectus rather than from a summary.

What is the per-share estimated value on my statement?

For an unlisted REIT, a figure required by FINRA Rule 2231(c), based on at least annual valuations performed with third-party assistance. Your statement must also disclose that the security is illiquid and that any price you actually receive may be less than that estimate.

Are REIT distributions guaranteed?

No. They are declared by the board and can be cut or suspended. In non-traded REITs some distributions may be funded from offering proceeds or borrowings rather than operations, which the SEC warns "reduces the value of the shares."

Listed or non-traded — which is better?

They answer different questions. A listed REIT gives you daily liquidity and daily volatility. A non-traded REIT gives you a smoother reported value and real constraints on getting out. Neither is superior in the abstract; the honest comparison is between visible volatility and invisible illiquidity, and which of the two you can actually live with.
